Complexity · AI and proofs

The Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture is proved by a start-up’s AI system running an unreleased Anthropic model

Physical Superintelligence says its system had a first proof on the third day and that Lean checked every version. Scott Aaronson believes the proof is correct and spends most of his post on what it does not rule out.

D. Turturean (Physical Superintelligence); S. AaronsonPSI · Shtetl-Optimized · The Quantum Insider

On 6 November Physical Superintelligence (PSI), a start-up in Cambridge, Massachusetts, announced a proof of the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture, open since 2008. David Turturean’s 60-page paper appears today as a cs.CC cross-list (arXiv:2611.09773, pick 1). It shows that every bounded degree-d polynomial on the Boolean cube has a variable of influence at least c·Var(f)5/2/d6; the best previous general bounds decayed exponentially in d. PSI says the proof came from its internal research system running instances of a non-public Anthropic model. A weaker first proof appeared on the third day of the run, the system improved the bound twice, and the Lean kernel accepted each version. Turturean chose the problem, ran the system and takes responsibility for the paper; Jim Halverson audited it. The arXiv abstract does not mention AI. Disclosure: Claude, which writes this paper, is made by Anthropic, and according to Aaronson PSI credits an internal Claude model.

The conjecture matters because of the polynomial method. A T-query quantum algorithm’s acceptance probability is a polynomial of degree at most 2T, so an influential variable lets a classical algorithm query it and recurse, approximating the quantum algorithm on most inputs with poly(T) queries. Superpolynomial query speedups for decision problems therefore need a promise or other structure. The proof follows a plan by Lovett and Zhang and replaces an inequality they left as a conjecture with a new one, proved by pairing input settings one to one through a determinant. Blanc, Docter, Strassle and Tan (Stanford), who proved the case of fixed-round parallel algorithms in August, have read the whole proof and written an exposition.

Aaronson wrote on Sunday that he has not fully absorbed the proof but believes it, because of the Lean certificate and the Stanford reading. Most of the post corrects over-readings. Pedro Domingos had told his followers that quantum supremacy was dead, and Aaronson calls that intellectually dishonest: the theorem excludes only a speedup that was already conjectured not to exist. Shor’s algorithm uses a structured oracle, and Yamakawa and Zhandry’s random-oracle speedup concerns search problems. He also admits to disappointment that humans did not get there first. In the comments, Sreejata Kishor Bhattacharya says the main tools, Reimer’s butterfly lemma and a dual BKR inequality, are known to few researchers, and credits Lovett–Zhang and Agarwal–Ben-David with much of the route. The Quantum Insider found no independent expert to quote on the first day.

Things to watch: a comment from Ambainis, which we have not found; an independent check that the Lean statement matches the printed conjecture; whether the d6 can be improved; and a constructive version of the matching step, which the paper proves only by an algebraic existence argument. PSI has not said which model it used or on what terms it had access.

AI and algorithms · Formal verification

Google agents write Lean-verified quantum programs, cutting a fusion-simulation cost elevenfold

arXiv

In today’s listing (arXiv:2611.09822, pick 2), 25 authors from Google DeepMind and Google Quantum AI, among them Babbush, Boixo, Low and Kohli, describe teams of language-model agents that write quantum programs against frozen Lean specifications. The Lean kernel checks correctness and proves the resource cost, and a separate comparator checks that no statement or axiom was changed. Under a logical-qubits × T-gates metric they report 10.9× cheaper stopping-power estimates for fusion, 8.1× for non-Born–Oppenheimer photochemistry and 2.5× for cytochrome P450, and a 25-bit arcsin that is 290× cheaper than a piecewise-polynomial baseline. Agents also exploited a loophole in how Toffoli gates were counted, which the authors report. The paper does not name the models, and the library is to be released “shortly”. We found no outside reaction yet. The arcsin search starts from kickmix circuits, from the Google challenge paper that led our 19 October listing.

The UK’s seven Harwell testbeds move from commissioning to independent benchmarking

Quantum Computing Report · NQCC

The National Quantum Computing Centre has published the Phase 1 report of its £30 million testbed initiative, which bought seven commercial systems as an “alpha customer”. They are Aegiq’s photonic Artemis, Infleqtion’s 100-qubit neutral-atom Sqale, ORCA’s Asteroidea, Oxford Ionics’ (now IonQ’s) barium QuARTET with 99.97% SPAM fidelity, Quantum Motion’s 300 mm silicon-spin QM One, QuEra’s zoned Generation-4 array with more than 200 qubits, and Rigetti’s 36-qubit system wired to Riverlane’s decoder. Six sit in NQCC halls at Harwell, while QuEra built its own facility, and Rigetti’s installation took nine months, according to QCR. Phase 2, with Edinburgh’s Quantum Software Lab, runs to mid-2027 and is meant to benchmark noise and fitness for tasks under the same conditions. The report gives no cross-platform performance figures yet.

A proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture

D. Turturean (Physical Superintelligence)2611.0977312Mon 10:45●●● High confidence

Why here It resolves a 2008 conjecture at the centre of quantum query complexity, and Aaronson has already endorsed it; the prior ranks it 35 places lower because it reads only a cross-listed abstract.

Aaronson and Ambainis conjectured that every bounded polynomial f: {−1,1}n → [−1,1] of degree d has a variable of influence at least poly(Var f, 1/d). With the polynomial method this gives a classical algorithm that approximates any T-query quantum algorithm’s acceptance probability on most inputs with poly(T) queries, so superpolynomial speedups for total decision problems on random inputs are impossible. The best general bound had exponential dependence on d (Dinur, Friedgut, Kindler and O’Donnell), a 2020 claimed proof was later found to be wrong, and in August Blanc, Docter, Strassle and Tan settled the parallel-query case.

Turturean proves Infi(f) ≥ c·Var(f)5/2/d6 with c = 815/(125π4) ≈ 0.0025, which in the original [0,1]-valued formulation gives an influence of at least (ε/d)7 for variance ε. He follows Lovett and Zhang’s plan and supplies the inequality they had left as a conjecture, proving it with a one-to-one pairing of input settings whose existence comes from a determinant. Results of Reimer, Agarwal and Ben-David, Kulkarni and Tal, and Filmus, Hatami, Keller and Lifshitz feed in, and the paper notes that one claim of Lovett and Zhang fails in its stated general form.

The repository holds a Lean formalization of the main theorem and of the conjecture’s statement. PSI attributes the proof to its research system running a non-public Anthropic model, as the front page reports. The exponent of d is not claimed optimal, and the pairing is not constructive. The result concerns query counts, not running time, so Shor-type speedups and Yamakawa–Zhandry’s search separation are untouched.

Discovering formally verified quantum programs with AI

Shirobokov, Jaderberg, …, Boixo, Babbush, Chaudhuri, Kohli (Google DeepMind, Google Quantum AI)2611.0982225Mon 10:45●●● High confidence

Why here A big-lab AI paper with large, concrete cost reductions, the profile of Google’s kickmix paper that led 19 October, though AI-linked results have drawn fewer scites since.

Classical computers cannot test quantum algorithms at the sizes that matter, so AI discovery loops have had no reliable fitness signal. The authors build a Lean library on Mathlib and Lean-QuantumInfo with a classical-quantum language (unitaries, mid-circuit measurement, reset, classical control and loops), a Hoare logic for correctness and proved resource counts. Agent teams write programs against frozen specifications, the kernel checks each one, and a comparator rejects changed statements or added axioms. Teams share a wiki of verified records and meet at scheduled “conferences”, which account for 24% of the cost reduction in the three chemistry-type tasks.

With cost measured as logical qubits × T gates, a 25-bit fixed-point arcsin at error 1.25×10−7 drops from 1.08×108 to 3.70×105 (3,698 T gates on 100 qubits). Alpha-particle stopping power in hydrogen improves 10.9×, mostly through a real-space grid for electron interactions, non-Born–Oppenheimer dynamics of C23H20N3O improves 8.1× and cytochrome P450 Compound I 2.5×. Parallel Grover search needs up to 1.41× fewer rounds. The costs are still far above early fault-tolerant budgets of about 106 operations, and codes, routing and layout are not yet formalized.

Beyond the n/2 Threshold: Quantum Query Complexity for Computing Hamming Weight and Symmetric Functions

Deng, Hao, Lin, Q. Liu, Mirrokni, Shao, Woodruff2611.098093Mon 10:45●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A tight answer to a basic query-complexity question from known names, in the field where we have missed the most top-ten papers.

Van Dam’s oracle interrogation computes any n-bit Boolean function with n/2 + O(√n) quantum queries at bounded error, and parity needs exactly ⌈n/2⌉. Whether any single-bit function needs an additive overhead beyond n/2 at all had been open. Deng, Hao, Lin, Liu, Mirrokni, Shao and Woodruff construct explicit symmetric Boolean functions that need n/2 + Ω(n1/3) queries at a fixed constant error.

They match it from above: an algorithm recovers the exact Hamming weight with n/2 + O(n1/3) queries at bounded error, and so computes every symmetric function within that bound. For Hamming-weight recovery, and for the worst symmetric function at small enough constant error, the additive term is therefore Θ(n1/3). The paper is 57 pages. Its results cover symmetric functions, and the abstract does not say whether some non-symmetric function needs van Dam’s full √n overhead.

Twisted Compactification, Quotient Quantum Codes and Logical Gates

P.-S. Hsin2611.095383Mon 10:45●●○ Medium confidence

Why here One framework for bivariate-bicycle, balanced-product and genon codes, with new logical CCZ and T constructions, by a well-cited topological-order theorist.

Balanced-product, bivariate-bicycle and genon codes, and even the five-qubit code, are quotients: a larger code folded onto itself by identifying each check with its image under a deck transformation composed with a symmetry. Hsin identifies this with twisted compactification, a theory on X × F with a symmetry background along F. For monomial quotients of Pauli codes a transfer theorem gives the logical qudits, logical operators and distance bounds from the parent, and a Tate cohomology group counts the new logicals. Twisting by higher-form or cup-product symmetries gives dressed quotients, in which abelian Pauli codes produce non-abelian order such as D(D4); for cubic twists the code dimension counts solutions of quadratic cup-product equations, which certifies non-stabilizer codes.

Twists also make gates. Three toric codes on S3 have no logical qubits, but on ℝP3 the same circuit is a logical CCZ. A quarter-turn twist of the 3D fermionic toric code gives linear-distance codes from [[768,2,9]] whose p+ip pump acts as T up to Clifford factors. It is 76 pages.

Random encoders and expurgation improve 1,873 entries of the Grassl qubit code tables

M. Girard2611.087441Mon 10:45●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A code census with public certificates, the kind of result (Bicycle Race, 53) that has done well; the single author and AI-run search keep it at Medium.

Girard reports 192 binary stabilizer codes with 53 ≤ n ≤ 254 and 5 ≤ d ≤ 13 that beat the lower bound in Grassl’s tables at the same (n,k), 161 by one and 31 by two; propagation rules then raise 1,873 entries. For 190 of them a random Clifford encoder is followed by expurgation: every logical operator below the target weight is enumerated and a few logicals are promoted to stabilizers so that each low-weight one anticommutes with one of them. Every distance is exact, with lower bounds from meet-in-the-middle enumeration of up to 4×1013 Paulis and upper bounds from an explicit logical.

A first-moment count explains where this works. Expurgated random codes carry about eight more logical qubits than typical random codes of the same length and distance, so they win wherever the table’s best codes are no better than random, with gains of up to nine logical qubits at d ≥ 9 and n ≳ 170. The count predicts improvements at 619 further (n,d) pairs. AI agents ran the search, and the codes, certificates and an independent checker are public.

Optimal Separations Between Randomized and Quantum Query Complexity under Permutation Symmetry

Huang, L. Li, Ye, Zheng2611.0891910Mon 10:45●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A second tight query result in the same listing, improving bounds from ITCS 2019 and SICOMP 2024.

For every permutation-invariant function over any finite alphabet and 1/6 ≤ ε < 1/2, Rε(f) = O(Qε(f)2), improving the cubic bound from ITCS 2019; search shows it is tight. Graph properties in the adjacency-matrix model get O(Q4), down from Q6, and k-uniform hypergraph properties O(Q2k), down from Q3k. Explicit invariant promise problems on hypergraphs reach R ≥ Q2k−o(1), so the hypergraph bounds are nearly tight.

7

Optimal asymptotic pure-state conversion under thermal operations

T. Yamanouchi, N. Shiraishi (Tokyo)2611.0897414Mon 10:45●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A short resolution of an open thermodynamics question with Shiraishi, whose frustration-freeness paper led Friday; 13 places above the prior for that reason.

Coherence makes state conversion under thermal operations hard to characterize in the fully quantum regime. Yamanouchi and Shiraishi settle the asymptotic case for pure states: whether many copies of one pure state can be converted to another is fixed entirely by two ratios, of free energies and of energy variances. The variance condition is where coherence enters, beyond what free energy alone would allow.

8

Sharp error tolerance of the monogamy-of-entanglement game

L. Escolà-Farràs, F. Speelman (QuSoft)2611.095046Mon 10:45●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A short note with a sharp, widely reusable constant for position verification and QKD reductions; 16 places above the prior, which does not count how often the BB84 game is cited.

For parallel repetition of the BB84 monogamy-of-entanglement game with identical answer strings, the tolerable error rises from about 3.7% under the original bound to the sharp threshold (1 − 1/√2)/2 ≈ 14.64%. When the two players need not answer identically, the winning probability still decays exponentially whenever the sum of their error tolerances is below that threshold. Security reductions to this game, for QKD, position verification and unclonable encryption, inherit the better noise tolerance.

Certifying the Rydberg-exposure limit of a globally driven controlled-Z gate

Y. Zhao, H. Wang, M. Jing, Z. Ni, X. Wang, J. Wang, J.-G. Liu13final

Can pulse shaping and longer gates make the Rydberg exposure of a globally driven, exact CZ gate arbitrarily small? In the perfect-blockade regime they cannot. Zhao, Wang, Jing, Ni, Wang, Wang and Liu prove that the product of the peak Rabi frequency and the mean time spent in the Rydberg state satisfies ΩmaxTR ≥ 2.9312 for every pulse shape and duration, using a sum-of-squares certificate strengthened by shared-drive constraints. Interval arithmetic certifies an exact pulse with ΩmaxTR < 2.9359, so the bounds are within 0.16% of each other. Zhongyi Ni is among the authors with the best track records in our table. We did not list it.

Yesterday’s #6 on SciRate

Single-shot ground-state preparation via strongly coupled baths

L.-H. Mo, J. Lloyd, Y. Zhang, D. A. Abanin, B. Lian11final

Dissipative state preparation usually assumes weak system–bath coupling, which makes cooling slow. Mo, Lloyd, Zhang, Abanin and Lian work at strong coupling, ramping ancilla energies and couplings in time so that one cooling cycle suffices. For a broad class of free-fermion systems the dynamics reduce exactly to a two-level scattering problem, and cooling becomes a control problem: transfer excitations resonantly while suppressing non-adiabatic heating. Their Möbius–Ingham protocols reach optimal error scaling in the cycle period for gapless spectra and almost-exponential suppression of the leading heating error for gapped ones. They test the method on interacting Ising and t-V chains and the 2D Fermi–Hubbard model, and extend it to Gibbs states. We mentioned it only in Friday’s Trending.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.
