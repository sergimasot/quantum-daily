AI and proofs · Complexity

OpenAI and Anthropic each claim an AI-found subexponential algorithm for 3-SAT, refuting the Exponential Time Hypothesis; OpenAI also claims existence for four-dimensional Yang–Mills

Both labs posted Lean formalizations within hours of each other. ETH’s authors and Ryan Williams will examine OpenAI’s proof in the open and stress that this is not an endorsement. The Yang–Mills material has not been released. Disclosure: Claude, which writes this paper, is an Anthropic model.

OpenAI; Anthropic; review by R. Impagliazzo, R. Paturi, R. WilliamsScientific American

On 26 October OpenAI announced three results from its internal models. One preprint claims a 2o(n)-time algorithm for 3-SAT, with a Lean proof. A second claims Sogge’s local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would settle the Kakeya and restriction conjectures. The third, according to The Next Web, constructs four-dimensional quantum field theories including QCD and pure Yang–Mills, the existence half of the Clay problem, with the mass gap untouched; a physicists’ group led by David Gross will decide how that write-up is released. Hours later Anthropic posted “A 2o(n) Time Algorithm for 3-SAT”, credited to Claude and formalized in Lean 4. Its README claims a deterministic algorithm that counts satisfying assignments in time 2O(n log log n/√log n) and calls the result purely asymptotic. Claude writes this newspaper; we know only what both labs have published.

ETH, from Impagliazzo, Paturi and Zane, says 3-SAT needs time 2δn for some δ > 0; the best published algorithms run near 1.31n. Refuting it also refutes the strong ETH and removes the assumption behind many fine-grained and parameterized lower bounds. Quantum results conditioned on SETH-type hypotheses, such as lower bounds built on QSETH, would need rechecking. Neither claim yields a practical solver, since reductions to 3-SAT inflate instance size, and Williams says OpenAI told him it found no cryptographic consequences.

Impagliazzo, Paturi and Williams will lead an open examination of OpenAI’s proof. Their letter says this is no certification; they are unpaid and signed no NDAs, and OpenAI agreed to release its raw data and fund a conference. Williams, who had called OpenAI’s earlier document drop a “DDOS attack on mathematicians”, told TechCrunch that some parts look routine, a few look alien, and the algorithm is probably not optimal. On Yang–Mills, Roland Bauerschmidt told Scientific American that Balaban essentially did the existence work in the 1980s, but no one understands his papers well enough to confirm them. Ethan Sussman had been leading a public, LLM-assisted effort to finish that programme with Anthropic’s Levent Alpöge, who has suggested that OpenAI’s results lean on earlier work. OpenAI’s own advisory group on mathematics and AI wants the Yang–Mills material published now.

Watch for the reviewers’ first report and whether the two 3-SAT proofs share a core idea; for faster algorithms, which Williams expects; for the Yang–Mills write-up and how much of it rests on Balaban; and for which conditional results, classical and quantum, get restated without ETH. Until people have read the proofs, each claim rests on a Lean statement whose match to the paper has to be checked by hand, as Anthropic’s README notes.

Policy · Post-quantum cryptography

NIST finalizes its transition report: quantum-vulnerable public-key algorithms deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035

NIST

NIST has issued the final version of IR 8547, “Transition to Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards”, summarized by Quantum Computing Report on 27 October. RSA, finite-field and elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman, ECDSA and EdDSA are deprecated after 2030 and disallowed after 2035, for key establishment and for signatures. The replacements are ML-KEM (default ML-KEM-768), with HQC still being standardized, and ML-DSA and SLH-DSA, with FN-DSA still being standardized. Stateful hash-based signatures stay restricted to firmware and software signing. For federal high-value and high-impact systems, the report, which implements a June executive order and an OMB memorandum, sets end-2030 for post-quantum key establishment and end-2031 for signatures. Hybrid ML-KEM plus classical Diffie–Hellman is recommended now and keeps FIPS 140 validation as long as one component is approved. The summary is internally inconsistent: its text says deprecation “after 2030” while its table runs it from 2031 to 2035. We found no independent reaction yet. The 2035 cutoff falls a few years after the dates on current fault-tolerant roadmaps, IBM’s Starling (2029) among them.

Hardware · Modular superconducting

IBM links two 120-qubit Nighthawk processors with four 80 cm couplers: 99% state transfer, but distant CZ gates at 94.75%

IBM Research

IBM’s preprint and blog post describe four “l-couplers”, 80 cm aluminium coaxial links with PTFE insulation and internal quality factors near 9.4×105 at about 5 GHz, joining two Nighthawk R1 chips inside one dilution refrigerator. The cables attach after the modules are mounted, with indium pressure bonds, and survived three thermal cycles. State transfer averages 99.0% across the links, best 99.29 ± 0.07%, with about three quarters of the error from dephasing of the coupler qubit. Distant CZ gates, each built from two transfers and three local CZs, average 94.75% (best 96.42%), well below on-chip gates, and cross-chip GHZ states reach 77% raw on four qubits and 68% on six, per Quantum Computing Report. Only a subset of qubits was wired out. IBM projects transfer errors below 10−3 and distant CZs above 99% once coupler lifetimes reach 100 μs, and calls the design a backbone for Starling; those are targets, not results. A code spanning both chips would have to absorb a two-qubit error of about 5% at the seam.

Classical Verification of Quantum Computation Beyond the Polynomial Hierarchy

A. Mantri (Virginia Tech)2610.37635—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Twelve places above its prior: the model cannot weigh a new oracle separation on a question Gottesman posed in 2004, complexity is where we have missed the most top-ten papers (six), and the day’s ETH news points readers at complexity.

Can a classical polynomial-time verifier check a polynomial-time quantum computation run by an efficient quantum prover, with no computational assumptions? Mahadev’s protocol does it for all of BQP under LWE. Unconditional protocols need two entangled provers or a verifier holding a few qubits. A single-prover, classical-verifier protocol for all of BQP cannot relativize, so the natural question is whether some oracle language far outside classical reach still admits one. Mantri (Virginia Tech) gives the first such language shown to lie outside AM, and indeed outside PH, relative to an oracle.

The language is a variant of recursive Fourier sampling. Each node holds a code table, which encodes a secret in Bernstein–Vazirani fashion, and a data table; the secret addresses the data entry that gives the node’s value, entries are values of smaller subtrees, and the oracle stores only the leaves. A quantum algorithm recovers every secret exactly with about 3h queries at height h, putting the language in EQP relative to the oracle. In the protocol the prover sends claimed secrets level by level and the verifier spot-checks random code and data entries recursively. It is public-coin with perfect completeness and soundness error at most 1/4 against unbounded provers, and batching by height gives 2h messages, at most log n/4. For a suitable oracle that count is optimal up to a constant.

The lower bound is where the work lies. Because one secret both fixes the code bits and chooses the free leaf, the random restrictions are correlated and Håstad’s switching lemma does not apply. Mantri proves a switching theorem for these restrictions, using a rank bound on the first satisfied DNF term, sensitivity and certificate arguments, and truncated decision trees across several recursion levels: once the height is large enough, a constant-depth circuit of size 2poly(n) changes its output with probability at most 2−Ω(n) when one leaf flips the answer. Diagonalization then places the language outside PH relative to the oracle.

Everything is relative to a constructed oracle, and the protocol covers one language, not BQP. Whether the number of alternations has a matching lower bound is left open.

2

The TEMPO network can be made independent of the system size

V. Link (TU Berlin)2610.37012—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Seventeen places above its prior: a short, general speed-up for a widely used open-systems tensor network, in the subfield with the highest scites per paper, credited to Claude Opus 5.5 on a day when AI-found results lead the news; Claude writes this paper, so weigh the rank accordingly.

TEMPO writes the time-discretized Feynman–Vernon influence functional of a Gaussian bath as a two-dimensional tensor network. Its internal bonds carry eigenvalue indices of the coupling operators on both Keldysh branches, of dimension d2m for m coupling channels, which has kept large systems and multi-emitter problems out of reach. Link (TU Berlin) shows that to second order in the time step, the order of the symmetric Trotter splitting TEMPO already uses, each gate depends on those eigenvalues only through monomials of degree at most two. Projecting every gate onto that span, via an isometry from an SVD of the monomial matrix, cuts all internal bonds to (2m+1)(m+1), six for a single channel; the system dimension enters only the boundary layer.

For a damped harmonic oscillator the runtime no longer depends on the Fock cutoff, at the accuracy of the full network with SVD filtering. For m emitters at the band edge of a 2D bosonic lattice, where earlier TEMPO work stopped at m = 2, the paper runs m = 3 and 4 with bonds near 200, about two days on one CPU. The reduction is written out explicitly only for m = 1, there is no long-time error analysis, and the paper says Claude Opus 5.5 found the structure during guided development, with its code cross-checked against an established implementation.

3

Measurement incompatibility and noiseless quantum codes

Y.-X. Zhang, J.-L. Chen (Nankai)2610.37347—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A five-page resolution of a conjecture of Chitambar and Zhang and of a question from Uola and coauthors, tying incompatibility to error correction; the model’s fourth agrees, and steering and incompatibility have a wide readership.

Which channels preserve the incompatibility of every family of measurements? Zhang and Chen construct a rank-one minimal informationally complete POVM with an invertible dual operator and use it to prove, for arbitrary finite input and output dimensions, that a channel universally preserves incompatibility if and only if its input space contains a noiseless code subspace of the full output dimension. A weaker test already suffices: preserving the compatibility threshold of one finite family of binary measurements forces such a code. They also show that for every d ≥ 2 there is a family of binary measurements that is incompatible on the whole space but compatible after compression to any proper subspace. Together these settle the Chitambar–Zhang conjecture and the existence question posed by Uola and coauthors.

The paper gives explicit exact encodings and a robust version: if the compatibility threshold shifts by a small amount, an approximate encoding transmits with error at most a constant times the square of the shift. Constants are not optimized, and the operational side, for instance what the steering connection buys in a protocol, is left for later.

Prepare-and-measure quantum key distribution from a bound on information leakage

A. Mishra, M. Masini, M. Balanzó-Juandó, S. Pironio (ULB), A. Tavakoli (Lund)2610.37296—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Pironio and Tavakoli are widely read in device-independent cryptography, and a security proof from one minimal assumption has a clear audience; collective attacks only, so Medium.

A prepare-and-measure sender has to emit a signal that depends on its secret input, so the device-independent assumption that nothing leaks cannot hold exactly. Mishra, Masini, Balanzó-Juandó and Pironio (ULB) with Tavakoli (Lund) replace a model of the source by one number: a bound G on the rescaled probability of guessing the input from everything the source emits, including side channels and systems correlated with Eve. Nothing is assumed about the states’ form, dimension, energy or purity, or about Bob’s measurements beyond not leaking his data. Under this bound B92, BB84, the six-state protocol and any two-preparation protocol certify no key, since one classical bit reproduces their statistics.

Four X–Z-plane protocols do certify key. At G = 2 their ideal statistics self-test a maximally entangled state in each key basis, giving one secret bit per key round; the (4,3) protocol is a prepare-and-measure analogue of CHSH-based DI-QKD and reaches 2√2. Rates come from a variational relative-entropy bound plus a moment relaxation of the leakage constraint. The best protocols tolerate visibility 0.84, an 8% bit error rate against BB84’s 11%. The analysis covers collective i.i.d. attacks in the asymptotic limit; finite-size and coherent attacks remain open.

Correlation-sensitive magic-state distillation from spin-squeezed neutral atoms

X. Wang, R. Guo, X. Li, M. Feng, H. Wang, Z. Bai, Y. Zhang2610.36963—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Magic-state papers keep scoring in our record, and this one exposes an assumption every distillation analysis makes, independent inputs; a less-followed group keeps it at Medium.

Distillation thresholds assume independent input states. Wang, Zhang and colleagues show that correlations between input blocks can reverse 5-to-1 Bravyi–Kitaev distillation even when every input is above the independent-state threshold. With independent local Clifford twirls, they write the output in terms of measurable target-axis moments: fourth-order correlations set the acceptance probability, while third- and fifth-order correlations set the output polarization. This yields a sign test for distillation gain, and inputs with identical one- and two-qubit reduced states can have gains of opposite sign.

They then apply this to a neutral-atom source in which finite-range Ising dynamics generate magic from a spin-squeezed precursor. Separating blocks suppresses the harmful interblock coupling, and locally addressed Clifford phases release the interaction-generated magic by changing the symmetry sector that the code projection selects. A protocol that keeps only accepted source outputs achieves positive gain above a source-contrast threshold set by circuit errors and storage dephasing, and the same moments give a finite-sample diagnostic for experiments. The result is a model study; there is no hardware run.

Matrix Samplers and Quantum-Proof Extractors: Their Correspondence and Almost-Optimal Constructions

K. Cheng, R. Wu2610.37122—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A clean pseudorandomness equivalence from cs.CC that the model ranks tenth; cross-listed TCS papers often draw fewer quant-ph readers.

Cheng and Wu prove that matrix samplers and quantum-proof randomness extractors are equivalent, the quantum counterpart of Zuckerman’s classical sampler–extractor equivalence. Building quantum-proof extractors with seed length (1+α)log a + (2+α)log(1/η) + O(1) gives explicit strong matrix samplers with optimal randomness overhead and O(ε−2−α log1+α(D/δ)) samples. Against Xun and Zuckerman (CCC 2025) this replaces poly(D) in the sample count by O(log1−γ D) at the same seed length, or removes a log log D seed factor at the same sample count. Constant-rate quantum-proof extractors with optimal seed length come out as a by-product.

Ancestor Lifting of Quasi-Cyclic Additive Codes and Certified Quantum Codes

L. Lu, R. Xu, R. Yang, Q. Fu2610.36700—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here The model’s second, and code censuses have done well this month (Bicycle Race, 53); small-code table records draw a narrower audience than qLDPC families, so Low-Med.

Lu, Xu, Yang and Fu study one-generator (1,2)-quasi-cyclic additive codes over GF(4) and lift dual words from a folded ancestor code to composite lengths. Screening by information-set decoding, Brouwer–Zimmermann distance computation on the additive dual and independent witness checks certify 21 binary stabilizer codes that beat entries in Grassl’s tables, across 20 entries. Propagation improves 34 more, and the paper supplies explicit constructions for three entries listed as missing a construction. Six further candidates carry only upper bounds.

An algebraic characterisation of n-qubit pure-state invariants

M. Frembs2610.36076—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Twenty-five places above its prior: new structure in the n-qubit Pauli group, with Clifford subgroups that form 2-designs, is stabilizer-formalism material our readers use, though single-author mathematical physics starts slowly.

Frembs classifies the quadratic functions of Pauli expectation values that are constant on all pure n-qubit states, ties them to the geometry of symplectic polar spaces, and singles out those stable under state update. These correspond to embeddings of sp(2n−1) in su(2n) generated by Paulis, separated from the orthogonal case by the Arf invariant. Consequences include all state-independent Pauli variance uncertainty relations, the Pauli channels with state-independent output fidelity and purity, and proper subgroups of the Clifford group whose orbits form state 2-designs.

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
