Policy · Funding

Atom Computing signs its $100 million Commerce award, six months after the letter of intent

The Boulder neutral-atom company is the latest of the nine firms named in May to turn a CHIPS letter of intent into a contract. Infleqtion and Diraq had not finalised theirs as of mid-September, and we found no announcement from either since.

Atom Computing (company announcement)Atom Computing · Quantum Computing Report · PostQuantum

Atom Computing announced on 13 November that it has signed an agreement with the US Department of Commerce for up to $100 million of R&D funding toward fault-tolerant neutral-atom computers. The company says the money will pay for in-house component development, more parallel engineering testbeds, and joint R&D with suppliers to scale manufacturing in Boulder, with arrays of several thousand atoms as the target (Quantum Computing Report). The agreement replaces a non-binding letter of intent from May. The release gives no schedule, no milestones and no equity terms, and we found no matching announcement from Commerce or NIST.

In May, Commerce signed letters of intent worth $2.013 billion with nine companies and made a minority, non-controlling equity stake a condition of each (NIST). D-Wave, Rigetti, Quantinuum, PsiQuantum, GlobalFoundries ($375 million) and IBM’s Anderon wafer foundry ($1 billion) finalised in September. PostQuantum read the public companies’ filings: the awards are Other Transaction Agreements, roughly half of each is available at signing, and the rest is released on milestones that Commerce alone judges; Rigetti issued Commerce about 7.7 million shares and D-Wave about 7.1 million. As of mid-September only Atom Computing, Infleqtion and Diraq (up to $38 million) were still at the letter stage. Atom is also the only neutral-atom company in Stage C of DARPA’s Quantum Benchmarking Initiative, which could bring up to $300 million more.

We found no comment from researchers or analysts beyond trade-press summaries of the release, and no forum discussion. The release repeats the company’s own milestones (the first platform above 1,000 qubits, sub-threshold behaviour over many rounds of full error correction, the first commercially offered logical-qubit machine), which this paper has not checked. The same day, a Foundation for American Innovation analysis argued that federal quantum money, CHIPS included, has gone mostly to system builders and too little to their suppliers (see below).

What to watch: whether Atom Computing or Commerce discloses the size of the government’s stake and the milestone schedule, which the listed companies had to file; whether Infleqtion, the only listed company still at the letter stage, announces a final agreement before year end; and the National Quantum Strategy update due in December under June’s executive order, which the FAI analysts want to include a plan for buying several machines.

Policy · Supply chains

Policy analysts say component suppliers get under 12% of federal quantum funding

Foundation for American Innovation · The Quantum Insider

Joshua Levine, Prineha Narang, Jacqueline Tame and Alexandra Seymour Gabel of the Foundation for American Innovation argue that the supply chains behind the first fault-tolerant machines may not be the ones commercial machines will need (The Quantum Insider). Makers of refrigeration, materials, optics and control electronics rely heavily on foreign sources and receive less than 12% of federal quantum funding, they write, while AI data centres are buying the optical chips, switches and fibre that quantum developers also need. They propose pooled advance purchase commitments for components and whole systems, priority for inputs shared across platforms (helium-3, silicon-28, several rare earths, fibre), coordination between the CHIPS R&D office, the Office of Strategic Capital and Defense Production Act authorities, and wider sharing of supply-chain data from DARPA’s QBI. Some of the manufacturing investments, they say, will take seven to ten years to pay off.

Trapped ions · Integrated photonics

Cornell cools all three motional modes of a trapped ion with light delivered through the chip

PRX Quantum · Phys.org

Karan Mehta’s group at Cornell, with ETH Zurich and chips made at a commercial foundry in the Netherlands, uses integrated waveguides to form a phase-stable standing wave at a trapped calcium ion (PRX Quantum, Xing et al.). The standing wave cools all three motional modes, spread over about 5 MHz, at the same time, reaching near the ground state of the targeted motion in about 150 µs against about 500 µs with a conventional laser setup, and it also cools below the Doppler limit (Phys.org). In shuttling-based trapped-ion machines the ions have to be re-cooled after transport, so cooling time adds directly to circuit time. The demonstration is a single ion; we found no comment from outside the group.

1

Efficient representations by PEPS: critical free-fermion and chiral topological states

I. Cirac, R. Trivedi (MPQ Garching)2611.17936—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Cirac, a short plain title, and a polynomial-bond answer to the long-standing question of whether PEPS can describe Fermi surfaces and chiral order; known theory names with a clean statement have led most listings.

Can PEPS efficiently describe two-dimensional states with a Fermi surface or chiral topological order? Critical free fermions break the area law by a logarithm, and known no-go results say that a translation-invariant Gaussian PEPS of fixed bond dimension with nonzero Chern number cannot have a local, gapped quadratic parent Hamiltonian. Cirac and Trivedi show that both obstacles go away once the bond dimension may grow polynomially in system size and 1/ε, with ε the global error.

For the 2D nearest-neighbour tight-binding model at half filling, a Gaussian wavelet transform splits each scale into a smaller 2D problem, critical 1D chains and local dimers. The wavelet unitaries become Gaussian matrix-product unitaries, the chains are replaced by MPS (bond dimension O(n1+2/π/ε2) in 1D), and fermionic swaps arrange things so that each row and column passes through at most two wavelet steps. For any uniformly gapped, finite-range quadratic Hamiltonian in any dimension they use a different route: filter a product state repeatedly with eqK. The error falls as ((1−Δ)/(1+Δ))q, so q = O(log N + log 1/ε) layers of constant bond dimension suffice. This gives Gaussian PEPS for p+ip superconductors and an explicit π-flux Chern insulator.

Projecting m copies of these Gaussian approximations Gutzwiller-style raises the error to at most 2mNε and the bond dimension to Dm, so Kalmeyer–Laughlin-type chiral spin-liquid trial states also get polynomial PEPS. None of this contradicts the no-go theorems, which concern exact representations at fixed bond dimension.

The exponents are large (D = O(n6+2κ/ε4+κ) with κ ≈ 31 for the 2D tight-binding model), and the authors say their bounds only establish polynomial scaling. The constructions are explicit but are not a variational algorithm. Interacting critical fermions, lattice gauge theories, continuum limits, and whether translation invariance plus entanglement scaling alone suffice are left open.

Tight Quantum Query Bounds for Collision Finding over Arbitrary Bounded Types

T. Peng, S. Hou, X. Dong2611.17804—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A tight query bound that settles an open problem of Belovs with a new compressed-oracle technique; compressed-oracle lower bounds were the strongest theory thread of early November.

How many queries does it take to find one r-collision in a function whose type, meaning the sizes of all its equality classes, is fixed in advance? For every fixed maximum class size and collision order, Peng, Hou and Dong give a tight answer: the maximum of scales set by the numbers of classes of size at least 2, …, r. The lower bound holds for a uniformly random function of the type and the algorithm works on every such function. The result runs from the abundant-collision regime of set equality to the sparse-target regime of k-distinctness. Finding the unique (k+1)-collision in type (kn, k+1) costs Θk(N3/4−1/[4(2k+1−1)]), which resolves an open problem of Belovs.

For the lower bound they build a “filtered” compressed permutation oracle that projects onto a harmonic subspace of databases after each complete function query. Its comparison error over T queries is O(T/N), which avoids the square-root loss of the unfiltered process. The upper bounds combine sampled collision lists with coloured adaptive learning graphs and a coherent decision-to-search conversion that costs no logarithmic factor. A separate nested quantum-walk algorithm for three-collision search matches the bound up to polylogarithmic factors.

3

Agentic AI for Discovering Fault-Tolerant Quantum Circuits

A. Lahiri, F. Marquardt (MPL Erlangen)2611.18504—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A known group, an AI method and verified records on standard codes; that combination has drawn scites before, unlike claims of AI-found proofs.

Lahiri and Marquardt run an agentic FunSearch loop. An LLM (OpenAI’s GPT-6-luna and GPT-6.1-sol tiers) writes Python programs that output dynamic Clifford circuits, calls a verifier as a tool, and evolves the programs across islands. The verifier checks the logical action over all measurement branches and enumerates every fault up to order ⌊(d−1)/2⌋ under circuit-level noise, with flag detectors allowed. Tasks cover logical state preparation, logical gates, Steane-to-Reed–Muller code switching and surface-code growth from d = 3 to 5 and 5 to 7, on rotated surface codes, colour codes up to [[81,1,9]], the [[5,1,3]] code and the [[8,3,2]] code.

Some results: |1L⟩ on the five-qubit code at depth 8 against 12 for an earlier RL design; a five-qubit-code logical CNOT at depth 16 with 29 two-qubit gates against 84 and 65 for a flagged construction (with different decoding assumptions, the authors note); [[19,1,5]] colour-code preparation at depth 8 against 18; and the native-layout surface code at depth 6–7 for d = 3–9. The stronger model set new records more often. The gains are mostly in depth, ancilla counts are close to earlier work, and qLDPC codes are not attempted.

4

Fast mixing and thermal phases from strong decay of correlations

C. Yin, V. Khemani2611.17433—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Rigorous Gibbs-state theory from a known group that also settles fast mixing for the high-temperature Potts model; we rank it 39 places above the prior because many-body theory is one of our two largest sources of missed top-ten papers.

Yin and Khemani define strong decay of correlations (SDOC): the conditional marginal on a small region moves by at most f(r) in trace distance under any boundary configuration at distance r. This point-to-set condition is stronger than decay of connected correlations but weaker than strong spatial mixing. For a smooth path of commuting, geometrically local Gibbs states that satisfy SDOC, they construct O(1)-time quasi-local Lindbladians that connect the states exactly in both directions, and a quasi-local unitary that connects their canonical purifications. Ma, Khemani and Sang previously had approximate channel circuits of depth log N.

If the path also contains a product state, single-site Glauber dynamics has a spectral gap Ω(1) independent of system size, proved with Lieb–Robinson bounds. Three applications follow: the q-state Potts model throughout its high-temperature phase for any q and dimension, where strong spatial mixing is expected to fail; the low-temperature Ising model in a longitudinal field with frustrating perturbations; and the 3D Z2 lattice gauge theory with a field. The results need commuting Hamiltonians, and the product-state condition is assumed, not proved.

Beyond Blossom: Accelerating MWPM Decoding for Surface Codes With Local Predecoding and Architecture-Aware Optimizations

J. Fu, …, X. Sun, …, G. Yang (Tsinghua, USTC, BAQIS, ICT CAS)2611.18476—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A faster exact matching decoder with public code and latency numbers; decoder papers have taken the top places on three listings this month.

A local predecoder runs before the blossom algorithm. It takes defects in increasing order of candidate degree and matches them along tight edges while keeping dual feasibility, ru + rv ≤ wuv. If every defect is matched this way, complementary slackness certifies the matching as minimum weight and the decoder stops; otherwise the partial matching and radii seed the full solver. The rest of the speedup comes from a cache-friendly 3D layout, SIMD (AVX-512 or NEON) and pipeline parallelism.

On Stim rotated-memory circuits with uniform circuit-level noise at p = 0.05–0.3% and d = 9–33, one core gives a 4.23× geometric-mean speedup over PyMatching 2.3.1 (up to 5.58×), with the same matching weight on every tested input. With 8 cores at d = 33 and p = 0.1%, a block of 33 rounds decodes in 21.40 µs, or 0.65 µs per round, and the decoder meets a 1 µs-per-round budget in 18 of 21 settings against 11 for PyMatching. The code is public. It covers surface codes on CPUs only, with no numbers against FPGA designs such as Micro Blossom.

6

Magic of Dicke states: density, cost and yield

Y. H. Kwan2611.17623—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A short, plainly titled magic result with a fault-tolerance angle; single-author, and the model already ranks it fifth.

The stabilizer Rényi entropy of a Dicke state is extensive at every filling except one half, and Kwan gives the density analytically with subleading terms. At half filling the SRE grows only logarithmically, yet the preparation cost, the average CCZ yield and the relative entropy of magic stay extensive, so a small SRE does not mean a weak resource. At any 0 < f < 1 Dicke states yield an extensive number of CCZ states on average (T states with a catalyst), but the yield fluctuates by O(L) between runs, as if set by a hidden phase shared by all qubits.

7

Quantum phase estimation on a Rydberg quantum simulator

H. Timme, …, J. I. Cirac, I. Bloch, J. Zeiher (MPQ)2611.18122—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here MPQ’s analog-simulator group with Cirac; we rank it 43 places above the prior for the names, though single-lab hardware papers have rarely reached our top five.

The authors reconstruct the complex return amplitude (Loschmidt echo) of a probe state on an analog Rydberg simulator and Fourier-transform it, which gives absolute ground-state energies and gaps in systems of up to 19 atoms in one and two dimensions, with no auxiliary qubits and no controlled evolution. Changing the probe state selects different spectral windows. Within them they compute longitudinal and transverse magnetisation and follow how the two compete across the spectrum. The system sizes remain small enough for exact classical checks.

8

Quantum Preparation without Disturbance: Information Costs and Hypothesis Testing

P. Costa Rico, R. Salzmann, M. Berta, Á. Capel2611.18135—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here An operational meaning for the Belavkin–Staszewski relative entropy from known information theorists; information-theory picks have done middling, so it closes the list.

What does it cost to prepare either of two states with one device while keeping its memory intact? Measured by the Umegaki relative entropy between the two possible memory states, the minimum is the Belavkin–Staszewski relative entropy of the targets; if the memory may be consumed, it drops to their Umegaki relative entropy. For any divergence obeying data processing, memory preservation picks out the maximal extension of its classical restriction. With finitely many outputs the cost climbs from Umegaki toward Belavkin–Staszewski, and the Stein exponent for testing an unknown device converges to the Belavkin–Staszewski value as the outputs per run grow.

Structure-Aware Quantum Circuit Equivalence Checking via Sparse Pauli Propagation

S. Xia, J. Luo, Q. Wang, Y. Suzuki, J. Zhao9Fri 10:20

A cs.SE cross-list that checks whether two unitary circuits are equivalent using sparse Pauli propagation. One strategy compares how the circuits conjugate Pauli generators; the other, a “cut-miter”, multiplies one circuit by the inverse of the other at chosen cut points and exploits the cancellation. A procedure picks between them from circuit features and accounts for numerical and truncation error. On 434 scored pairs of up to 512 qubits it reached the reference verdict on 96.3% with no wrong verdicts, against at most 68.7% for the baselines, and ran 48.5–76.0% faster in aggregate. We mentioned it under Closest to your work instead of ranking it.

Uncertainty-aware quantum machine learning based on Dempster-Shafer theory

M. Kempkes, J. Spiegelberg, E. van Nieuwenburg, V. Dunjko6Fri 10:20

The Leiden group gives quantum classifiers a way to say how sure they are. In Dempster–Shafer theory a model puts weight on sets of labels as well as single labels, and the authors train parameterised circuits to learn these mass functions from data. Because the circuit prepares the corresponding state directly, quantum Dempster–Shafer fusion runs with polynomial resources even when the mass functions cover exponentially many subsets. It is the second time since October that a Dunjko–van Nieuwenburg QML paper has reached the top five without a pick from us.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
