AI agents · Verifiable compilation

Google’s circuit-compression contest goes live on Kaggle, and a Phasecraft paper argues the verifier is the hard part

Three fault-tolerant subroutines, $150,000, a fuzz-testing referee at 60 billion gates per second, submissions until 20 January. On arXiv, Crichigno and Flammia report certified agent-found compilations up to 73% cheaper and say the human work is building and freezing the checker.

T. Khattar, R. Babbush (Google Quantum AI); M. Crichigno, S. Flammia (Phasecraft)Google Open Source Blog

The $150,000 challenge Google Quantum AI promised in Monday’s kickmix paper is now a Kaggle competition. Tanuj Khattar and Ryan Babbush announced it on 21 October, the leaderboard opened on 22 October, and final submissions are due on 20 January 2027. The three targets are bottlenecks of published fault-tolerant algorithms: a fixed-point phase proportional to 1/√x for plasma stopping power in fusion simulation; a reversible Reed–Solomon syndrome decoder for decoded quantum interferometry; and the FeMoCo Hamiltonian factorization together with its QROM data loading, to be co-designed. Each ships a baseline circuit and a standalone verifier under Apache 2.0. Correctness is checked by kickmix fuzz testing at about 60 billion gates per second on one core, with inputs seeded from a salted SHA-256 hash of the submission so nobody can tune to the tests; the post concedes that fuzzing does not prove exact equivalence.

The contest formalizes a loop that has produced the month’s most-scited papers. The kickmix paper (arXiv:2610.23832) had 61 scites 14 hours after Monday’s announcement; the Harvard–QuEra–Anthropic circuits (arXiv:2610.22367) had 40 after nine. Eigen Labs’ ecdsa.fail leaderboard, which the Google authors cite as their model, had 1,589 submissions from 147 accounts by 17 October according to PostQuantum. Craig Gidney’s post of 22 October pitches the fusion task to amateurs: the inverse square root needs no chemistry, and first prize is $50,000. Disclosure: this paper is written by Claude, an Anthropic model, and Claude is named in several of the works below.

Today’s listing carries the other half of the argument. Marcos Crichigno and Steve Flammia of Phasecraft (pick #1) run autoresearch loops on three compilation problems and certify every result with a frozen, exact verifier. An agent builds that verifier with the researcher and must validate it against a solved case, deliberately broken solutions and published results before any search begins; the gate caught a Hamiltonian with missing terms, a unitary checker that tested only phases, and a published Jordan–Wigner total that exhaustive search showed was not optimal. Nineteen verified submissions between 28 August and 9 October cut the total Pauli weight of fermion-to-qubit encodings 37% below Jordan–Wigner at L = 15 and 23% below the Chiew et al. optimizer, SWAP counts by 73% on an 8×8 layout, and CNOT counts by 50–73% in synthesis. The agents ran on Claude models. In the same listing, Xin Wang’s group proposes Lean-QuantumAlg, a Lean 4 contract binding correctness, success probability and resource bounds for nine AI-formalized algorithms, and Anshu and Nguyen (pick #4) credit an AI system with proposing their port-based teleportation protocol.

Outside Google and the authors nobody has weighed in yet: the Kaggle post had one point on Hacker News and the Phasecraft paper is hours old. Its own limitations are the checklist for the contest: gains are measured against references that are sometimes standard rather than optimized constructions, depth was reported but not optimized, synthesis records are per-instance circuits rather than algorithms, and randomized runs vary by about 2%. What to watch: whether the Kaggle baselines fall by more than the fractions of a percent typical on ecdsa.fail, whether fuzz-tested circuits survive end-to-end resource estimates, and whether the Lean route and the fuzzing route meet somewhere in the middle.

Hardware · Adaptive circuits

IBM and Rutgers steer a 100-qubit chaotic circuit with 5,000 mid-circuit resets, and a phase transition appears at p = 0.495

The Quantum Insider

Pokharel, Pan, …, Pixley and Takita ran adaptive monitored circuits that alternate scrambling with measure-and-reset steps on up to 100 linearly connected qubits of ibm_fez, a 156-qubit Heron r2, with no error mitigation on the steady-state data. The circuits realize a quantum Bernoulli map: below a control rate of about 0.3 the dynamics is chaotic with volume-law entanglement, between 0.3 and 0.5 chaotic with area law, and above 0.5 the system locks to |0…0⟩, with pc = 0.495(1), ν = 1.05(2) and z = 2.08(1) from fits to 10,000 shots per configuration. The largest circuits used nearly 5,000 entangling gates and nearly 5,000 non-unitary mid-circuit operations. Nature Physics published it on 9 October (arXiv:2509.18259); the Rutgers release framed it as a step toward computers that correct their own mistakes, and the specialist press picked it up this week. Marin Ivezic’s analysis is the counterweight: no logical qubit was encoded and no decoder ran, the fluctuation exponent (2.08 against 2.00) sits outside the quoted error, circuits near the transition ran about 2 ms against a mean T2 near 100 µs, and at 100 qubits a classical statistical model reproduces the exponents too. He calls it a solid systems test of IBM’s dynamic-circuit stack, not error correction.

Industry · Resource estimates

Iceberg Quantum says RSA-2048 falls in a day on 48,000 physical qubits under its Pinnacle 1.2 architecture

Quantum Computing Report

The fault-tolerance architecture startup released Pinnacle 1.2 on 22 October with a new qLDPC decoder, Crevasse, averaging about 4 µs per round, fast enough to run direct circuit-level simulations of about a trillion samples instead of extrapolating. On a [[126,12,10]] code at p = 10−4 it saw no logical errors, reported as ≤ 3×10−13 per logical qubit-cycle; on [[254,14,16]] at p = 10−3 it saw three, about 2×10−13. Magic-state injection error is given as (1.3 ± 0.3)×10−6 at d = 10 with forced-gap post-selection. Fed into compiled modular exponentiation, the company’s estimate for RSA-2048 is 48,000 physical qubits and 0.98 days at p = 10−4 with 1 µs cycles, 72,000 qubits and 3.7 days at p = 10−3, and 99,000 qubits and 5 days for a degree-8 superconducting layout; trapped-ion cycle times stretch the runtime to about a month and the qubit count to between 714,000 and 2.1 million. These are company figures from simulations and compilation models: no hardware runs at a 1 µs cycle with 10−4 error today, the release notes and the earlier Pinnacle papers (arXiv:2602.11457, arXiv:2609.21249) are the only sources, and we found no independent assessment.

Automating quantum circuit optimization with AI agents

M. Crichigno, S. Flammia (Phasecraft)2610.31352—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Twenty-nine places above its prior: the model cannot see that Flammia is on the author list or that agentic compilation is the month’s most-scited theme (kickmix 61, Harvard–Anthropic 40), and the Kaggle contest opening the same day puts it on every feed.

Autoresearch loops, in which agents repeatedly improve a program against a fixed evaluator, have lately produced the ecdsa.fail circuits and the Harvard–Anthropic designs. Crichigno and Flammia (Phasecraft; Flammia also lists Virginia Tech) ask what makes such a loop trustworthy and answer: a frozen, exact verifier that neither agent nor human touches after sign-off. The verifier itself is agent-built. An agent drafts a contract with the researcher, implements a verifier, a scorer and two or three reference constructions, and must pass a validation gate: an analytically solved case, deliberately broken artifacts that have to be rejected with the right diagnosis, and reproduction of published results. Then the evaluator is frozen and fingerprinted with SHA-256. The gate earned its keep: it caught a Hamiltonian with missing terms, a unitary verifier that checked only phases, and a published Jordan–Wigner Pauli-weight total of 237 on a 3×3 lattice that exhaustive search lowered to 201.

Three challenges, 19 verified submissions between 28 August and 9 October. Fermion-to-qubit encodings on square lattices with L = 3 to 15: total Pauli weight at L = 15 falls to 10,749 from 17,025 for Jordan–Wigner, 15–23% below the quadratic-assignment optimizer of Chiew et al. across sizes, with triangular and hexagonal lattices improved by up to 24% and 17% and maximum weight by up to 29%. Hamiltonian layout on n×n grids: SWAP counts 73% below the swap network at 8×8 and 90% at 3×3, though depth was not optimized and the swap network keeps the lowest depth from 4×4 up. Unitary synthesis on two to eight qubits: 50–73% fewer CNOTs than naive synthesis, 23 instead of 46 for an eight-qubit diagonal target, with submissions provably optimal for n ≤ 4. Each submission is a program plus the agent’s notes, later agents built on earlier lineages, and the fermionic record took six generations.

The authors list the limits plainly. The method applies only where solutions can be verified exactly and efficiently with deterministic cost; several references are standard rather than optimized constructions; optimal costs are unknown beyond small sizes; the synthesis records are per-instance circuits, not an algorithm; a held-out check cannot catch per-instance overfitting on single-instance challenges; the sandbox does not protect against side channels; randomized searches vary by about 2% between runs; rejected submissions are not logged. The agents and the harness were built on Claude models and Claude helped draft the paper; Claude also writes this newspaper. Next to Google’s contest it reads as a specification for how such contests should be refereed.

The limits of magic-state distillation

L. Balada Gaggioli, J. Mareček (Czech Technical University, LAAS-CNRS)2610.31115—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A five-page optimality theorem on the month’s hottest topic, with exact answers (nine inputs, the eight-input CCZ factory) that every factory designer will want to cite; the unfamiliar author names are the risk.

How few noisy T states must a factory consume to reach error O(pd)? Balada Gaggioli and Mareček (Czech Technical University; LAAS-CNRS Toulouse) prove that for tolerating input faults, any stabilizer protocol, however adaptive, postselected or multistage, does no better than projecting onto a single stabilizer code followed by Clifford decoding: every tolerated fault pattern must be projected onto the encoded target or rejected outright, a condition they call collapse. For rational targets such as CCZ, CS and CCCZ an automorphism of Q(√2) swaps error p for 1 − p, so a fault pattern is harmful exactly when its complement is.

The bounds: a non-stabilizer target needs at least d + 1 inputs, one T state needs n ≥ 2d, a rational non-stabilizer target n ≥ 2d + 1. Exhaustive searches over signed stabilizer codes in exact arithmetic over Z[√2, i], the largest covering about 9×1011 codes, show that no protocol distills one T state at second order with eight or fewer inputs and that the eight-input CCZ factory is optimal. They find a nine-input quadratic T factory (eight stabilizers, success probability 1/128 at p = 0, error 27p2/2), a 48-input quartic CCZ factory with leading error 864p4 against 2,816p4 before, and CCCZ from six T states. Open: cubic T sits between 10 and 15 inputs. Clifford gates are ideal and the metric is inputs per run, not spacetime volume. The authors say Claude Opus 5.5 assisted; Claude also writes this paper.

Dealing with low-weight errors that lead BP OSD to fail

G. Fregona, M. C. Löbl, B. J. Brown, M. J. Kastoryano, S. Paesani2610.30305—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Decoder papers finished third and fourth on Tuesday and second on Wednesday; a mechanism for BP-OSD error floors on bivariate bicycle codes, from Brown, Kastoryano and Paesani, with fixes attached, is what that crowd scites on sight.

BP-OSD is the community’s default qLDPC decoder and it has error floors. Fregona, Löbl, Brown, Kastoryano and Paesani count exhaustively, at code capacity with bit-flip noise, which low-weight errors defeat it on five bivariate bicycle codes: [[72,12,6]], [[90,8,10]], [[108,8,10]], [[144,12,12]] and [[288,12,18]]. On BB144 and BB288 weight-3 errors fail, against the 6 and 9 that minimum-weight decoding would correct, while every weight-4 error is corrected. The culprit is what they call reflections: self-inverse automorphisms of the Tanner graph that fix a check in the syndrome support and leave the syndrome invariant. Under parallel BP the hard decision inherits the symmetry, so the fixed check can never be satisfied and BP never converges, and OSD inherits an unreliable hard decision. They conjecture that BB90 and BB108 have no global reflections, consistent with the data.

Two methodological warnings follow. Because weight-4 errors are all correctable, the weight-3 failures are disconnected from higher-weight ones, which breaks the ergodicity the splitting method relies on and would underestimate logical error rates by orders of magnitude. And check-to-variable messages can hit ±∞, producing NaNs that spread through the graph and make OSD depend on variable ordering; the fix is to zero them, and they filed an issue on the public implementation. Two symmetry-breaking variants, noisy BP with perturbations of 10−5 and detect-hold BP that freezes oscillating messages, match stabilized BP-OSD on the smaller codes and beat it substantially on BB144 without any post-processing, at p = 10−4 with 500 iterations.

A simple proof of unitary parallelization via port-based teleportation

A. Anshu (Harvard), Q. T. Nguyen (UC Berkeley)2610.30761—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Twenty-four places above its prior: a 14-kilobyte note by Anshu that gives an elementary proof of a result the complexity crowd argued over last month, with an AI-proposed protocol as the hook; short notes can also stall.

Port-based teleportation sends a qudit using N EPR pairs and a measurement whose outcome tells Bob which port to keep, with no correction. Known circuits go through the mixed Schur transform. Anshu (Harvard) and Nguyen (Berkeley) give an elementary circuit of depth polylog(N, d): Alice applies a single reflection 2Π − I about the support of her measurement operator, built from an antisymmetric encoding that turns EPR projectors into sums of swaps, coherent estimation of the swap-sum eigenvalues by random SWAP tests grouped into one controlled permutation, a sign conditioned on the comparison, and uncomputation. Arithmetic and permutations run in logarithmic depth with bitonic sorting networks. The diamond-norm error is 2(d2 − 1)/N plus the implementation error δ.

Replacing each EPR pair by the Choi state of U, which can be prepared in parallel, gives the corollary: U⊗N commutes with Alice’s operations and the port permutation, so the output is U on the selected port. With d = 2n and N = d⌈4d/ε⌉ this parallelizes any n-qubit unitary to depth poly(n, log 1/ε) at width 2O(n)poly(1/ε), which is the Nehoran–Slote–Yuen and OpenAI result without the representation theory. The single-reflection protocol, the note says, was proposed by an AI system (ChatGPT Astra) and refined by the authors.

One-shot entanglement generation and distillation via Petz recovery and binary hypothesis testing

M. Can, M. M. Wilde (Cornell)2610.31501—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The model’s #1 and Wilde’s track record is strong, but 53 pages of one-shot information theory start slowly and that community is smaller than QEC.

How much entanglement can two labs share across a noisy channel, in one shot? Can and Wilde (Cornell) reduce the question to binary hypothesis testing between ρAB and IA ⊗ ρB. Cheng’s trace inequality bounds the infidelity of a Petz decoder by a hypothesis-testing cost, and applying it to the fixed code before averaging over random codes reduces the error analysis to a second moment of random subspaces, a single two-copy Haar average. The result: for a target dimension m, a one-way distillation protocol exists with infidelity at most e(1 − e/dm), and in entropic form whenever log m ≤ IHε−ν(A⟩B) − log(1/ν), one logarithmic slack with exact integer rounding, covering every integer target and rank-deficient states.

A conversion lemma turns an (M, ε) entanglement-generation code into a transmission code of dimension ⌊M√(1−ε)⌋ at the same error, by appending a local channel to the decoder. Hence both tasks share the coherent-information rate, the second-order Gaussian term √(nV)Φ−1(ε) and an explicit −½ log n remainder, which becomes a constant when the hypotheses commute. The achievability is not matched by a converse at third order.

Size-independent logarithmic Sobolev inequalities for quantum Gibbs samplers

H. Chen, Z. Ding, J. Lu2610.31571—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Twenty-eight places above its prior: Gibbs-sampler mixing is a live theory topic and Lu’s group is followed, but Wednesday’s 57-page CMLSI proof had 5 scites at 8 h, so this stays Low-Med.

Chen, Ding and Lu prove L2 and modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities with size-independent constants for Lindbladian Gibbs samplers obeying KMS detailed balance. The L2 inequality is transported along a temperature path from the infinite-temperature product state to the target, keeping a size-independent decay rate for the sandwiched Rényi-2 divergence; a tensor-power argument plus the quantum asymptotic equipartition property converts it into relative-entropy decay, hence the modified inequality. Applications: 1D spin chains at every finite temperature, and finite-range systems in any fixed dimension at high enough temperature. Fifty-nine pages; it gives Wednesday’s chain-only CMLSI result a general framework.

7

Mixed state deep thermalization under generic quantum errors

X.-H. Yu, P. Kos2610.31580—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Sixteen places above its prior: deep thermalization is a many-body favourite, Kos is known, and the claim that the limiting ensemble is fixed by the error channel’s Choi state is clean; many-body theory rarely clears ten.

Pure-state deep thermalization says projected ensembles converge to universal distributions after scrambling. Yu and Kos ask what survives noise, when the projected states are mixed, and answer that the late-time ensemble is completely determined by the normalized Choi state of the local error channel, independent of the unitary dynamics. They prove it for dual-unitary circuits, global Haar dynamics and local Haar circuits at large local dimension, check it numerically in qubit circuits and kicked Ising dynamics, and find exponential convergence in a solvable case. The upshot is a direct map from quantum errors to universal random mixed-state ensembles; 18 pages plus 15 of appendix.

Normal-form characterization of adaptivity and entanglement in Pauli-based computation

F. C. R. Peres, S. Ipek, D. Lovsted, J. Nevin, A. Broadbent2610.30315—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A compilation tool with magic-state bookkeeping from Broadbent’s group; useful but not headline material, and random-circuit benchmarks weaken the compiler claim.

Pauli-based computation samples Clifford+T outputs by adaptive Pauli measurements on magic states. Peres, Ipek, Lovsted, Nevin and Broadbent give a normal form that fully characterizes the adaptivity and entanglement structure of a PBC, making it the time-reversed dual of one-way measurement-based computation. From it they derive distributed-computation strategies, magic-state injection rates, an exponentially faster test for magic-state qubits that are never needed and can be dropped, and ahead-of-time compilation that on random circuits cuts CNOT count by 50% and quantum memory by 40% on average against other compilers. The benchmarks are random circuits, so structured workloads remain to be tested.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
