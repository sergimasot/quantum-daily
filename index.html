Mathematics and AI · Quantum information

No four mutually unbiased bases in dimension six: a second, independent proof also classifies every triple

Cárdenes Wuttig and Tindall use the topology of 6×6 complex Hadamard matrices to settle Zauner’s 1991 conjecture without relying on OpenAI’s computer-assisted proof. Both proofs rest on certified numerics, and both were developed with AI assistance.

M. Cárdenes Wuttig (Yale), J. Tindall (Flatiron Institute)arXiv:2611.06917—scites

In today’s listing Mateo Cárdenes Wuttig (Yale) and Joseph Tindall (Flatiron Institute) classify every set of mutually unbiased bases in ℂ6. Each MUB triple belongs to a two-parameter family or is a single isolated triple built from the Fourier matrix F6, and none extends to a fourth basis, so Zauner’s 1991 conjecture that dimension six admits at most three MUBs holds. The new ingredient is topological: the order-six complex Hadamard matrices other than Tao’s form a connected space. With the authors’ August classification, which rebuilds each such matrix from four phases, connectedness reduces the problem to covering that space with 2,775 overlapping four-dimensional phase balls and a chart around F6, each certified with interval arithmetic. Six is now the first non-prime-power dimension in which all MUB sets are known.

OpenAI’s mathematics release included a preprint dated 24 September that claims the same bound through a computer-assisted search over balls in nine phases with floating-point interval bounds. Its Lean formalization covers only μ(6) ≤ 5. An independent audit by Lattice24 reported on 16 October that the code and run records were missing from the public release, and OpenAI restored them on 26 October. The new paper reuses OpenAI’s graph-search tests but reproves the inequalities behind them in Lean, and the authors say they rely on none of OpenAI’s certificates. Their reconstruction and connectedness theorems are formalized in Lean. The cover is checked by two independent interval-arithmetic codes (Arb, 33 minutes on 128 cores; Julia, 67 minutes on 64 cores), while the triple classification, about 900 core-hours, has a single certifier. The authors write that OpenAI’s ChatGPT 6 Astra and Claude Opus 5.5 assisted significantly in developing the proof, under their direction. Disclosure: Claude Opus 5.5 writes this paper, and the Lattice24 audit also used Claude agents.

The paper is a day old, and we found no expert comment on it yet. The most detailed public checking of OpenAI’s claim has happened in an issue on Google DeepMind’s formal-conjectures repository, opened on 7 October. Two contributors re-ran the discretization independently and found every cell excluded, and a third reviewed several lemmas with AI help and found no gap. The participants point out that no specialist has reviewed the argument and that μ(6) = 3 is not formalized, and the Lean statement is still marked open. In a post on 4 November, Lance Fortnow wrote that he had not expected AI to move from filling in his proofs to finding them within five years.

Things to watch: whether a specialist in complex Hadamard matrices checks the connectedness theorem, the one new topological step; whether the formal-conjectures entry is closed now that two logically independent proofs exist; and whether the triple classification, which OpenAI’s proof did not attempt, gets a second certifier. The authors’ code is public.

Fault tolerance · AI search

Language-model agents and an integer-programming solver turn up 564 new magic-state factories

Quantum Computing Report · arXiv

Shubham Jain, Adam Wills and Shraddha Singh (IBM Quantum, QuICS and MIT, according to Quantum Computing Report) describe in an October preprint a search for distillation protocols in which language-model agents propose subgroup symmetries, an integer-programming solver builds candidate circuit matrices, an independent engine verifies gate identities and distances, and a reviewing agent reads the logs and steers the next round. What makes moderate sizes tractable is a reformulation: distance at least three is equivalent to choosing nonzero, pairwise distinct syndromes in a binary-matrix representation, where exhaustive classification had stalled at 54 inputs. They report 699 factory classes, 564 of them new, including a [[63,11,3]] protocol with overhead exponent γ = 1.589, an [[850,128,6]] with γ = 1.057, and a [[1715,287,6]] with γ = 0.998, which they call the smallest known pure-T factory below γ = 1; others output CCZ⊗2 or T⊗CCZ⊗4 directly. The circuits are in a public catalog. QCR’s account on 4 November gives exponents but no physical-qubit or spacetime costs, so how these factories compare with magic-state cultivation in practice is not yet clear.

A Quantum Lower Bound for Triangle Finding

Cornelissen (Cambridge), Gilani (Maryland), Zhou (UBC)2611.06751—scites●●● High confidence

Why here The first superlinear quantum lower bound for a textbook problem stuck at Ω(N) for two decades, and quantum lower bounds are the category we have missed most often; 41 places above the prior, which does not weigh the problem’s history.

Triangle finding in the adjacency-matrix model has driven quantum algorithm design from Grover’s O(N3/2) through Magniez–Santha–Szegedy, learning graphs and Le Gall’s Õ(N5/4), but the lower bound never moved past the Ω(N) of unstructured search. The positive-weight adversary cannot help, since the certificate barrier caps it at O(N). Cornelissen, Gilani and Zhou prove Ω(N13/12), and by search-to-decision and subgraph reductions the same bound for triangle detection and for detecting any fixed connected non-bipartite graph.

The proof compares a planted triangle with Bernoulli(p) graphs conditioned to be triangle-free, using Zhandry’s compressed oracle adapted to biased bits and Belovs’ transfer method: a map Υ from negative to positive input registers that almost commutes with every query. Conditioning a dense graph destroys the independence the compressed-oracle commutation identities need, so the authors average over local changes with a conditional-resampling operator and replace those identities with two-coordinate interaction estimates, which require Δp2 to be small. On Kn,n,n at p = Θ(n−1/2) this gives N13/12, and the bound is tight for that input distribution. Closing the gap to N5/4 will need denser or more structured hard instances.

Algorithmic Wave-Particle Duality: Pathfinding in Welded Trees is Hard

Carolan, Childs, Coudron, Gilani (Maryland), Miloschewsky, Podder (Stony Brook)2611.05912—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A tight answer to a well-known question about the original exponential quantum-walk speedup, from Childs’s group, with a quotable framing; 31 places above the prior, which misses the question’s standing.

A quantum walk crosses the welded-trees graph from root to root exponentially faster than any classical algorithm, but it records no path, and whether some quantum algorithm can output an entrance–exit path efficiently has been open; earlier work only excluded algorithms that are both rooted and genuine. Carolan, Childs, Coudron, Gilani, Miloschewsky and Podder prove that pathfinding costs Θ̃(2n/3) queries for trees of height n: any q-query algorithm succeeds with probability O(q/2n/3 + nq3/2n). The matching algorithm runs the walk to the exit, then uses collision finding to join root-to-leaf paths from each side of the weld.

The lower bound uses compressed permutation oracles to track what the algorithm retains and forgets about the graph, and shows that the database stays close to a subspace of states recording no root-to-root path, with error O(√(|B|/N + nq/N)) per query. The authors call the result an algorithmic wave-particle duality: interference reaches the exit, but naming a route costs exponentially more. The hard distribution is not the original welded-trees one, although they say an average-case version follows from a variant. Open: a structure-hiding instantiation of the oracle, which likely needs cryptographic assumptions, and query–space tradeoffs.

Classical Clifford+T Sampling without Computing Marginals

M. Koch (Oxford)2611.06119—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A practical simulation algorithm that samples cultivation circuits in seconds, the stabilizer-tool type that has done well with us, but a short QPL proceedings paper by a single author.

Exact sampling from Clifford+T circuits with low-rank stabilizer decompositions usually goes through gate-by-gate sampling (Bravyi, Gosset and Liu, 2022), where the number of amplitude evaluations grows with the number of non-monomial gates such as Hadamards. That is the wrong cost for QEC circuits, which have few T gates and very many Cliffords and measurements. Koch’s T-by-T sampling reduces sampling a circuit of T-count t to 2t amplitude evaluations of circuits with T-counts 1, 2, …, t, without computing any marginals, and the count is independent of the Clifford structure around the T gates.

Paired with low-rank stabilizer decompositions for the amplitudes, this gives large speedups where T gates sit inside big Clifford computations. The headline benchmark samples end-to-end magic-state cultivation circuits with escape up to distance 19 in a couple of seconds. The paper appears in the QPL 2026 proceedings (EPTCS 456). Cost still grows exponentially in t through the stabilizer rank, so the method helps low-T circuits and not circuits with many T gates.

Efficiently tracking errors and outcomes in universal quantum circuits

T. R. Scruby, J. C. Magdalena de la Fuente2611.05867—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here It removes a basic obstacle to simulating decoding in non-Clifford fault-tolerant circuits, squarely in the QEC-tooling area that leads our scites per paper.

Decoding simulations for universal fault-tolerant circuits are hard because physical T, CS and CCZ gates break Pauli-frame tracking. Scruby and Magdalena de la Fuente show that for circuits with high fault distance, where all logical non-Clifford operations are implemented by physical T, CS and CCZ gates, one can track a modified Pauli frame that carries no logical information and check after decoding that it is still logically trivial.

Because the distribution of non-logical measurement outcomes does not depend on the logical state, all detector outcomes can be sampled correctly under stochastic Pauli noise. Adaptive operations conditioned on logical measurements, such as teleportation and preparation by measurement, are covered whenever those measurements have a known outcome distribution. The method only detects decoding failures and cannot sample the noiseless logical output, which the authors note is what complexity theory requires. It is 13 + 5 pages, with no code listed.

Quantum Key Exchange from Public Quantum Money

E. Goldin, T. Morimae2611.05985—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Goldin had two papers in yesterday’s top seven, including first place, and this is a clean characterization; 51 places above the prior, which does not count author track records.

Goldin and Morimae show that everlasting-secure two-message quantum key exchange, standard 2QKE, efficiently verifiable state puzzles and one-time signatures with quantum signatures and keys are existentially equivalent. Since state puzzles may be weaker than one-way functions, so may 2QKE, and everlasting security comes for free. One-shot signatures, quantum lightning and public-key quantum-money mini-schemes all imply it, and one-shot signatures also give QPKE with quantum public keys and a new “certified freeze” property.

The most entangled states of four and seven qubits

A. Lewis2611.05757—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here It proves two named conjectures (Higuchi–Sudbery, Gour–Wallach), but single-author, partly computer-assisted multipartite-entanglement papers have drawn modest scites; 16 places above the prior.

No four- or seven-qubit state is maximally mixed on every balanced cut. Lewis proves the Higuchi–Sudbery conjecture that the mean entropy of the three 2|2 cuts is at most 1 + ½log23 bits, attained only by M4 and its conjugate, and the Gour–Wallach conjecture for all Rényi orders, with the maximizer switching to cluster states at q ≈ 1.588. For seven qubits the minimal mean purity and maximal mean entropy have distinct unique optimizers from one icosahedral family; those proofs are computer-assisted with rigorous error bounds.

Linear-time erasure recovery for quantum expander codes

A. Krishna, C. A. Pattison2611.06818—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A provable linear-time decoder for qLDPC codes from known names, in the decoder area that has placed well with us, though erasure decoding is a narrower audience than Pauli decoding.

Small-Set-Peeling generalizes the classical peeling decoder to quantum expander codes (Tillich–Zémor; Leverrier–Tillich–Zémor) and corrects up to D − 1 erasures in linear time, the first decoder guaranteed to do so. Combined with the Small-Set-Find erasure conversion of Krishna, Livni-Navon and Wootters, it also yields a graph-local linear-time decoder for Pauli errors.

Quantum Query Lower Bounds from Classical Moment Matching

C. Kayal, F. Magniez, S. Sen, D. Szabó14final

Moment matching has been a standard tool for classical property-testing lower bounds since Raskhodnikova, Ron, Shpilka and Smith, and whether it had a quantum counterpart was open. Kayal, Magniez, Sen and Szabó prove two incomparable versions, Ω(N1/2−1/(2r)) for r matched moments and a sharper Ω(N1/2−1/(2(2r−1))) in a smaller range, using a continuous hybrid argument and the polynomial method. They get optimal bounds for k-distinctness, k-star-freeness and planarity testing. We listed it under “also”; it tied for third with 14.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks, showing each event's next deadline: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
