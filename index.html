Top pick · today’s listing

No √N search on the plane: an electrostatic lower bound makes Tulsi’s walk optimal

A single-author proof claims Ω(√(N log N)) local steps for any quantum walker searching an L × L lattice, closing the question Aaronson and Ambainis left open in 2003. The log comes from the self-potential of a point charge.

Weikang Li (IIIS, Tsinghua University; Hefei National Laboratory)arXiv:2610.23560—scites●●● High confidence

Can a walker that only hops between neighbouring sites of a two-dimensional grid find a marked vertex in Grover time? Aaronson and Ambainis asked in 2003, after giving an O(√N log3/2 N) algorithm. Coined, coinless and continuous-time walks then brought the cost to O(√N log N), and Tulsi’s walk to O(√(N log N)). The only general lower bound was the Grover-type Ω(√N), so a √log N gap remained for two decades.

Li works on a connected vertex-transitive graph where every vertex carries an arbitrary internal workspace. Local steps are mark-independent unitaries that move amplitude only between neighbours. Queries act only on the marked vertex’s workspace and are free, so only local steps T are counted. The key object is an overlap between the unmarked run and the marked runs, weighted by the potential drop A − K(x, m), where K is the graph’s electrostatic Green function and A its self-potential. The weight vanishes at the mark, so queries cannot change the overlap. Averaged over marks it starts at A, and a Dirichlet-energy inequality limits each local step to a change of √(2A/N). Success probability p forces it down to about A(1 − √(1−p)), which gives T ≳ √(NA/2). On the square lattice A grows like ln N/2π, giving T ≥ (1 − √(1−p))·√(N ln N/8π)·(1 + o(1)).

The same argument gives Ω(√N) in three or more dimensions, where the self-potential stays bounded (matching known algorithms), Ω(N) on the cycle with a truncated weight, and Ω(√th) in terms of the classical hitting time on edge-transitive graphs. With several marked vertices the marked set is a conductor and its capacitance sets the bound: sparse periodic subgrids cost about √((N/k) log(N/k)), compact clusters Ω(√(N log(N/k))), with a matching upper bound for known k via random thinning.

This is a v1 from a single author, and it needs vetting. The model excludes long-range hops, multiple walkers and oracle fields beyond the marked vertices, which the paper itself flags. Unknown k, dense marked sets and non-vertex-transitive graphs are left open. If the proof holds, the potential-weighted overlap is a reusable tool for spatially constrained query lower bounds, and Aaronson–Ambainis is a famous enough problem that the paper should travel well beyond the quantum-walk community.

Leveraging verifiable structure in quantum circuits to find more efficient compilations with agentic AI

T. Khattar, N. C. Rubin, C. Gidney, M. Broughton, G. H. Low, R. Babbush (Google Quantum AI)2610.23832—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Gidney and Babbush, AI agents and a $150k public challenge, three days after Lukin’s LLM paper took Friday; it could easily finish first.

Earlier this year Google estimated the cost of breaking 256-bit ECC with a withheld circuit that passed a public test harness; the follow-on ecdsa.fail challenge then had more than 100 participants, mostly from outside quantum computing, roughly halve Toffoli counts using AI coding agents. This paper generalizes the recipe. If a bottleneck subroutine is a “kickmix” circuit, built only from reversible classical logic, diagonal phases and measurement-based uncomputation, every basis input maps to one basis output up to phase. It can then be simulated classically, so correctness becomes fuzz testing. Their kickmix package (C++20/Python, in Qualtran) builds 2×107 and simulates 6×1010 instructions per second per core. Test inputs are seeded by SHA-256 of the circuit bytes plus a private salt, a per-input failure rate ε bounds the trace distance by 2√ε, and 102,400 clean shots certify ε < 0.1% at δ = 2−128.

Three benchmarks seed a Kaggle competition with a $150,000 prize pool. The first is 1/√x for real-space Coulomb simulation (baseline: 260 qubits, 7,735 Toffolis, relative error 2.8×10−8). The second is Reed–Solomon decoding for DQI on optimal polynomial intersection (1,893 qubits, 5.73×106 Toffolis, scored as Q²T with a repetition penalty). The third is QROM and Givens-rotation angle streaming for DFTHC chemistry.

The authors say their own internal results already beat all three baselines. Their outlook: architecture-aware QEC cost models and Lean proofs that generators are correct at every size.

3

A thermally and perturbatively stable classical spin model in two dimensions

S. Balasubramanian, M. Davydova, T.-C. Lin, R. Thorngren2610.22548—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A “first example” claim on self-correcting memory from authors well known in qLDPC and topological-phase circles; short abstracts like this one tend to do well on SciRate when the claim is strong.

A good passive memory should keep many distinct states stable at non-zero temperature, and that stability should survive any small change of the Hamiltonian. The 2D Ising model fails the second test: it has two low-temperature phases, but a uniform field favours one and destabilizes the other. Toom’s rule gives robust 2D classical memory, but as non-equilibrium dynamics rather than as an energy landscape.

The authors take the 2D classical spin model constructed in arXiv:2605.10943 and prove that it hosts many metastable states at non-zero temperature that remain stable under arbitrary Hamiltonian perturbations. They present it as the first such example. The tool is a recursive application of the cluster expansion: the 64-page paper works out the details, and the abstract is two sentences long.

For the quantum side, it reopens a quantitative question: what does a local 2D energy landscape need for stable memory, and how much of that structure can a quantum code inherit? The abstract does not give metastable lifetimes, the number of protected states or the temperature range, so check those in the paper before citing it.

4

Unconditional quantum PAC learning advantage in the large error regime

I. C. Oliveira (Warwick)2610.23268—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here An unconditional learning separation from a respected complexity theorist; learning separations did well last week (Park–Oh 28), but the large-error caveat will cap the excitement.

Do quantum examples give a super-polynomial advantage for PAC learning Boolean functions under the uniform distribution, without assumptions? Oliveira answers yes in the large-error regime. For every advantage λ(n) = n−ω(1) he builds a concept class that a polynomial-time quantum learner, given only quantum random examples, learns to agreement 1/2 + λ(n) with probability at least 1 − 1/nk, and it outputs a polynomial-size deterministic classical circuit. No polynomial-time classical learner reaches agreement 1/2 + λ(n)/nk with probability above 1/nk, even with membership queries.

The engine is a near-optimal quantum learner that returns a hypothesis with a prescribed correlation to any Boolean function. It extends Fourier sampling from parities to quadratic hypotheses, and uses interference to bias sampling toward positively correlated hypotheses, since plain Fourier sampling cannot see the sign of a correlation.

The limitation is spelled out: the analogous separation in the small-error regime would imply NP ⊄ BPP, so the unconditional result has to live near agreement 1/2. Expect it to be read as a sharp statement about where unconditional quantum learning advantages can and cannot exist.

5

Area theorems for quantum error correction

A. Mandal, M. Gökduman, H. D. Pfister (Duke)2610.22710—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Brings a classic coding-theory conservation law to stabilizer decoding, from Pfister’s group; information-theory crossovers into QEC draw steady scites.

In classical coding, the EXIT and GEXIT area theorems tie a code’s rate to the performance of MAP decoding on memoryless channels, which makes them a design tool. The authors build a coset-entropy formalism for nested linear codes: the message picks a coset, randomization picks a codeword within it, and the area under the coset GEXIT curve equals the normalized dimension of the quotient.

For stabilizer codes, the syndrome does not fix the logical effect of the error, and the remaining uncertainty is an entropy over logical correction classes, the quantity a degenerate MAP decoder actually fights. They show it equals a classical coset entropy built from the stabilizer group and its normalizer, so the quantum GEXIT curve has area k/n for every stabilizer code, including under depolarizing noise. For CSS codes on erasure or independent X/Z noise the problem splits into two binary coset problems.

On the erasure channel, stabilizer–normalizer duality gives a folding identity at complementary erasure rates, and with the area theorem a rate bound for vanishing logical error that matches erasure capacity. For depolarizing noise they derive a rate-dependent converse via coherent information. For decoder people, it is a new way to measure how far a decoder is from MAP across the whole noise curve.

Demonstration of logical error suppression in a surface-code memory on a cloud-accessible IBM superconducting processor

S. Kan, …, N. Tubman, Y. Mao, S. Stein2610.23840—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Below-threshold-style suppression on public IBM Nighthawk hardware is newsworthy, but the team is outside IBM and Λ is modest; last week’s lesson says lab brand plus modest Λ does not top the list.

Can a cloud user, not the vendor, get distance scaling out of a rotated surface-code memory on IBM’s square-lattice Nighthawk r2? The authors find that placement matters (qubit quality varies across the chip) and that syndrome extraction must be co-designed with the device: short readout, fused measure-and-reset, and an XX echo on data qubits during ancilla readout. Together these cut the median per-round logical error by 3.8× (X basis) and 1.4× (Z basis) against standard readout without reset or echo.

Raising the protected distance from 3 to 6, with the other distance held fixed, lowers the per-round logical error from 0.53% to 0.24% (Z memory) and from 0.52% to 0.22% (X memory). Under maximum-likelihood decoding the suppression factors are 1.68 and 1.85.

The interesting caveat for DEM builders is in the long runs. In memories up to 150 rounds the distance advantage weakens, alongside long-lived correlations that may reflect a persistent error component, which a stationary, round-independent detector error model would miss. It is a useful public reference point next to Google’s Willow numbers, and a reminder that vendor-agnostic QEC is now possible on cloud hardware.

Bell scenarios encode classical non-causality

A. Leitherer, K. Simonov, R. Kunjwal, A. Acín, K. Sengupta, H. Dourdent2610.23367—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here An unexpected structural identity from Acín’s circle; foundations papers with a crisp “X is exactly Y” claim do moderately well.

In the Acín–Fritz–Leverrier–Sainz hypergraph formalism, measurements are hyperedges, and several product rules combine single-party contextuality scenarios into multipartite ones. These rules are structurally distinct but give the same classical, quantum and boxworld correlations, so the difference looked incidental.

The authors show it is not. The measurements of composite Bell scenarios are exactly the process functions, the deterministic classical protocols that can lack a definite causal order without creating paradoxes. A hyperedge is valid exactly when the corresponding process function is logically consistent, so process functions are characterized by normalization and no-signalling alone. That gives transformations that build non-causal process functions from no-communication ones. The product rules order process functions by causal structure, and a process function is non-causal precisely when its hyperedge is generated by the disjunctive product but not the maximal one.

The result is the first joint hypergraph picture of non-causal process functions and nonlocality-without-entanglement measurements: classical non-causality appears as a non-standard way of composing black boxes.

Typical superpositions are magical

J. T. Heath, A. Urilyon, D. Quinn, G. Kells2610.22476—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A clean, general statement on stabilizer Rényi entropy that magic readers will note, from a group with lower SciRate visibility.

How much magic does a random superposition of m classically simulable states have? For large-N random pure-state ensembles satisfying the conditions of Schur’s lemma, the average stabilizer Rényi entropy is ⟨M2(m)⟩ = N − log2⟨W(m)⟩, and the average witness ⟨W(m)⟩ splits universally into a Haar floor plus an ensemble-specific excess.

As m grows the excess is suppressed, so the SRE grows logarithmically in m before plateauing at the symmetry-restricted Haar value. They derive a closed form for ⟨W(m)⟩ in the thermodynamic limit and check it numerically for superpositions of random stabilizer states and of random fermionic Gaussian states.

For simulation, the point is quantitative: superposing a handful of stabilizer (or matchgate) states already moves the SRE logarithmically toward its Haar value. Methods whose cost tracks the number of stabilizer terms in a superposition sit at the bottom of that curve. Ensemble averages say nothing about structured states, so this is a typicality statement, not a hardness result.

Controlled PROP: theory and applications to quantum circuits

N. Delorme, S. Perdrix2610.23192—scites●○○ Low confidence

Why here A neat completeness result from a leading ZX/circuit-rewriting group; category-theory framing usually limits the audience.

Control, applying a transformation conditional on another system, is everywhere in quantum and reversible computing but awkward in diagrammatic calculi. Delorme and Perdrix axiomatize control functors and extend PROPs to “controlled PROPs”, where control is a constructor of the language.

The payoff is for quantum-circuit equational theories. In the standard PROP setting, any complete axiomatization of quantum circuits needs axioms acting on arbitrarily many qubits. With control as a primitive, they give a simple complete axiomatization in which every axiom involves at most three qubits. For circuit optimizers and verified compilers built on rewriting, that is a much smaller rule set to implement and to trust.

QMBL: the Quantum Many-Body Leaderboard

T. Zerweck, J. Carrasquilla2610.22387—scites●○○ Low confidence

Why here Public leaderboards drew attention last week (Bicycle Race, the qLDPC Challenge), but this is a cond-mat cross-list and a resource note.

QMBL is a maintained, interactive record of published variational ground-state energies for lattice Hamiltonians. Release v0.4.0 holds 1,434 energies on 395 instances of six model families. Of these, 153 instances have no exact solution, so any new record is a direct improvement on an open numerical problem. They are the targets of a companion “Open Problems” benchmark. It is a natural yardstick for anyone claiming that a tensor-network, neural-network or Pauli-propagation method beats the state of the art on a ground state.

4

Parallel repetition for entangled games via conditional relative entropy

R. Jain13final

Jain gives a new information-theoretic proof of a cubic parallel-repetition theorem for finite two-player entangled games. If a game’s entangled value is 1 − ε, its n-fold repetition has value at most exp(−Ω(ε³n/L)) with L = max{1, log(|A||B|)}, valid for every n ≥ 1, recovering Song’s cubic rate. A threshold version bounds the probability of winning at least a 1 − ε + δ fraction of rounds by exp(−Ω(δ⁵n/(ε²L))). The technique defines an “information charge”, the drop in conditional relative entropy when a question reference is discarded. A chain rule bounds the charges coordinate by coordinate, and a main lemma turns small charge into nearby pure-state descriptions with local corrections that depend only on one player’s question. Combined with Song’s sampler, this lets the players approximately prepare the conditioned workspace locally. The paper is 35 pages. It does not improve Song’s exponent; its value is a different, information-theoretic route to it, plus the threshold statement, which may be easier to adapt to other nonlocal-game settings.

6

Quantum prepare-and-measure scenarios: concepts and methods

A. de Oliveira Junior, S. Zamora, J. B. Brask, R. Chaves, A. Tavakoli10final

A 78-page tutorial with 15 figures, written for newcomers, on the scenario in which a sender prepares one of several states and a receiver measures it. It covers how prepare-and-measure setups separate classical from quantum communication, where quantum advantages appear, and how they underpin quantum cryptography and device certification. The core of the text is methodological: frameworks for constraining and characterizing the communication, with emphasis on semi-device-independent approaches, and modern convex-optimization techniques for the correlations these scenarios can produce. It ends with open problems and future directions. The authors include several of the people who built the semi-device-independent programme, which makes it a natural entry point for students. The early count, ahead of two theorems we ranked higher, suggests readers scite reviews from well-known groups to bookmark them.

What this is

A weekday digest of the quant-ph arXiv listing. Every morning it forecasts which of the new papers the community will rate most highly, checks the previous day's forecast against the actual ratings, and adds the significant quantum computing news and upcoming conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate and news sources. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the paper before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Picks are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.
