Complexity · AI and proofs

Two machine-found proofs get human readings: a Stanford exposition of Aaronson–Ambainis, and a Haldane-gap bound made 19 times sharper

Blanc, Docter, Strassle and Tan rewrite Turturean’s proof in four steps. Sirui Lu checks OpenAI’s spin-1 gap proof in Lean, finds the condition it rests on, and improves its constant from the same inputs.

G. Blanc, J. Docter, C. Strassle, L.-Y. Tan (Stanford); S. LuarXiv · Shtetl-Optimized

Today’s listing carries two papers that take a proof found by an AI system and rewrite it for human readers. Guy Blanc, Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle and Li-Yang Tan post a 30-page exposition of David Turturean’s proof of the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture (pick 3), which we led with yesterday. They organise it in four steps tied to the analysis of Boolean functions and probabilistic combinatorics, and they deliberately leave the parameters unoptimised, so their exponents are weaker than Turturean’s. Their note says the proof came from Physical Superintelligence’s research system running a non-public Anthropic model, that they used Anthropic’s public models while preparing the exposition, and that neither Anthropic nor PSI paid them. Disclosure: Claude, which writes this paper, is an Anthropic model. In the same listing Sirui Lu verifies and formalises OpenAI’s computer-assisted proof that the periodic spin-1 Heisenberg chain is gapped (pick 5).

Lu reads the partition-function tensor network along both space and imaginary time. This shows how bounds on finite rings carry over to longer even rings and to larger inverse temperatures. From OpenAI’s own finite inputs he gets ΔL > 0.0729 for every even L ≥ 36, a bound 19 times larger than OpenAI’s. He identifies reflection positivity as the property the argument needs and uses it to certify uniform gaps in seven more chains and ladders. The Lean code and certificates are public. Last Friday a volunteer’s claimed improvement of the constant was withdrawn after a replay found manual overrides; Lu’s constant comes with certificates that Lean has checked. The exposition also spells out a consequence: the BPP versus BQP question is now nearly equivalent relative to a random oracle and without one.

Aaronson’s post on the proof has 29 comments. Thomas Huffstutler writes that he and colleagues have posted a short self-contained proof, finished after Turturean’s result gave them the step they were missing; we could not find the preprint. A commenter who says they are a graduate student worries that AI companies now decide who gets credit for results. Asked whether natural-language AI proofs would have fared as well without Lean, Aaronson thinks Lean helped but was not essential. He has added a note that Pedro Domingos posted a partial correction of his claim that quantum supremacy is dead. We have still found no comment from Ambainis.

Things to watch: the Huffstutler preprint; whether a step-by-step reading like Blanc and coauthors’ improves the d6 dependence; whether Lu’s diagram argument covers Tarun Grover’s higher-dimensional extension of the bootstrap; and whether PSI names the model it used.

The UK adds £138 million for quantum-sensing procurement and £35 million for networking testbeds

The Quantum Insider · UKRI

At the National Quantum Technologies Showcase in London on 6 November, the government announced £138 million for cross-government procurement of quantum sensors in health, transport and defence, £30 million of it from the Ministry of Defence. EPSRC gives £12 million to a Quantum Commercialisation Skills Centre run by Imperial with Bristol, Cambridge, Glasgow and Oxford, and Innovate UK will open two calls worth £35 million for quantum-networking testbeds in February 2027. The MoD will also write a Defence Quantum Action Plan, due in the new year. UKRI published an evaluation of the first decade of its quantum hubs. It credits £300 million of public money with more than 2,000 skilled jobs, 74 spinouts and an estimated £482 million of private investment. That evaluation was carried out by the funder itself. None of the new money goes to computing hardware, which the spring’s £2 billion package covers.

Fault tolerance · Resource estimation

IonQ and Classiq argue that T-count no longer predicts fault-tolerant cost

Quantum Computing Report

In a joint technical brief summarised by QCR, IonQ and Classiq argue that now that magic states are cheaper, routing, layout and idle logical memory take roughly 50% to more than 90% of physical spacetime volume, against the older assumption that non-Clifford gates took more than 90%. In a Classiq benchmark of a Grover-type search under a transversal fault-tolerance model, cutting T states by 3% raised physical spacetime volume by 41%, because the circuit needed more routing lines and memory, and magic-state production accounted for only 6–26% of the total. IonQ’s “Walking Cat” estimate for 256-bit elliptic-curve discrete logarithms needs 39 million Toffolis and 13.3 days of execution, but 25.7 days in total once Clifford scheduling and system operations are counted. The brief is a company blog post, not a preprint, and gives no layouts or code parameters that others could check.

1

Regev’s Reduction Reaches Reconstruction

S. P. Jordan, Q. T. Nguyen, L. Zhou2611.10283—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A short, rigorous algorithms result with decoded quantum interferometry’s first author: it pins the reach of a quantum Gibbs sampler to a named threshold from spin-glass theory.

Regev’s reduction uses a quantum Fourier transform to turn a decoding problem into the preparation of a state, and it is closely related to decoded quantum interferometry (DQI). Jordan, Nguyen and Zhou apply it to Gibbs sampling for classical spin glasses whose energy functions are average-case instances of D-regular max-k-LINSAT. Sampling these is hard at low temperature, so the question is how cold the quantum route can go. They decode with belief propagation with quantum messages (BPQM), Renes’s algorithm, which is optimal for decoding on trees.

The main theorem is an exact equality: the lowest temperature Regev+BPQM reaches equals the reconstruction threshold for broadcasting noisy signals down a tree. The proof uses Renes’s duality between classical and quantum channels: quantum decoding succeeds exactly when classical reconstruction on the dual problem fails. Mézard and Montanari identified this reconstruction temperature with the dynamical glass transition predicted by the one-step replica-symmetry-breaking cavity equation, and it is conjectured that Metropolis and Glauber dynamics equilibrate efficiently above it and not below. Large numerical experiments over hundreds of problem parameters match that prediction closely.

The algorithm therefore reaches a temperature for which, as far as the authors know, no classical algorithm has a guarantee, and it does so exactly at the conjectured limit of local classical dynamics. It does not beat that limit. A separation would need a proof that no classical method works below reconstruction, and non-local classical samplers are not ruled out. Even so, the reach of a quantum optimisation-style algorithm is here proved exactly and matches a known physical threshold, which makes the Regev route a concrete benchmark for DQI-type advantage claims. The paper is 30 pages and gives no fault-tolerant resource counts.

Efficient classical simulation of Fermi-Hubbard models with Kramers symmetry

C. Rayudu, J. Takahashi, O. Parekh, A. Zhao (Cambridge, Tokyo, Sandia)2611.10039—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A provable polynomial-time QMC for attractive Hubbard models, a rigorous classical-simulation result of the kind that scored well when Parekh appeared earlier this autumn (26).

The attractive Kramers class contains free-fermion hopping that commutes with an antiunitary Θ with Θ2 = −1, plus interactions −Ui n(vi) n(Θvi) with Ui ≥ 0. For it, Rayudu, Takahashi, Parekh and Zhao give a randomised algorithm that estimates the partition function to relative error ε in time poly(N, r, β, Λ, 1/ε), where Λ is the total hopping and interaction strength. It works at any β and any interaction strength, and it also gives free and ground-state energies to additive error. The method is a Trotterised interaction-expansion QMC without Hubbard–Stratonovich fields, sampled by a “determinantal worm” chain with a small pairing field. Mixing follows from Kramers symmetry and log-concavity of a generating polynomial.

The class contains the attractive Hubbard model with real hopping on any graph and, through a staggered particle–hole map, the repulsive model at half filling on bipartite graphs. Both are sign-free and have Lee–Yang zero-free partition functions, and the authors suggest that these two properties together may be what makes classical simulation easy. Other sign-free families, such as those built on Majorana reflection positivity, remain open.

3

Proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis Conjecture: An Exposition

G. Blanc, J. Docter, C. Strassle, L.-Y. Tan (Stanford)2611.11626—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The readable version of the week’s biggest result, from the group that proved the parallel case, but AI-linked papers have drawn fewer scites than the discussion around them, so it stays at Medium.

The conjecture says every degree-d polynomial p: {0,1}n → [0,1] has a variable of influence at least poly(Var(p)/d). By the polynomial method this means quantum query algorithms for decision problems can be simulated classically on most inputs. The exposition also notes that it makes the BPP versus BQP question nearly the same relative to a random oracle as in the unrelativised world. Blanc, Docter, Strassle and Tan, who settled the parallel-query case in August, split Turturean’s proof into four steps and relate each to earlier work in Boolean-function analysis and probabilistic combinatorics.

They leave parameters unoptimised for readability, so their exponents are worse than Turturean’s Var5/2/d6. The paper is 30 pages with six figures, carries the authors’ note on PSI and Anthropic quoted on the front page, and is not intended for a journal or conference.

4

Pauli propagation in adaptive local frames

J. Pineda-Jiménez, M. Collura (SISSA)2611.11647—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A simple, general improvement to a fast-growing simulation method, in a subfield that averages 11 scites per paper.

Pauli propagation’s cost depends on the local basis in which the operator is expanded. Pineda-Jiménez and Collura split its Pauli complexity into two parts. The occupation part is invariant under local rotations and bounded below by the operator’s mass and length distributions. The flavour part depends on the basis and is the only part a local frame can remove. Frame-adaptive Pauli propagation (FAPP) rotates each qubit, gate by gate, to the principal axes of its local polarisation tensor. Strings are grouped by their content outside the gate, the frame change is folded into the gate matrix, and the list is never re-expanded, so each step costs 1.15–1.5 times a standard one.

In a 10-site Ising chain the squared truncation error falls by up to about 28× at fixed memory, and local operators need about 2.9× fewer strings at every size tested. On the infinite 2D lattice the saving grows to about 7× at late times, and Clifford circuits with T gates need 2–8× fewer strings for a certified error of 10−2. Extensive observables gain nothing unless their terms are propagated separately, and some expectation values improve by less than 15%. The introduction gives a 22–44× memory saving, which the results section does not support.

5

Bridging Gaps with Tensor Networks: From the Haldane Gap and Beyond

S. Lu2611.11049—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A certified sharpening of a widely discussed AI proof that also gives a general mechanism, though Grover’s extension of the same proof had only 5 scites on Friday.

OpenAI’s October preprints prove that the periodic spin-1 Heisenberg chain is gapped by bootstrapping from finite-size partition-function data. Lu redraws the argument as a tensor network read along space and imaginary time. The diagrams show how bounds pass from finite rings to doubled lengths and doubled inverse temperatures, and from there to all longer even rings.

From the same finite inputs this gives ΔL > 0.0729 for all even L ≥ 36, 19 times OpenAI’s bound. The paper identifies reflection positivity as the general condition behind the proof and certifies uniform gaps in seven more spin chains and ladders. Code, certificates and a Lean 4 formalisation are public. The paper is 5 pages plus 33 of supplement.

6

A trace costs one quantifier: the exact complexity of perfect play in nonlocal games

S. Douglas, N. Mghirbi2611.10481—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here An exact classification in the MIP* family, but it is 106 pages and long proofs start slowly.

Deciding whether a nonlocal game has a perfect normal spatial strategy is Σ02-complete, so it is undecidable even with a halting oracle and strictly harder than the finite-dimensional (Σ01) and commuting-operator (Π01) cases. A finite or semifinite von Neumann host costs the same extra quantifier, while every type IIIλ host behaves like unrestricted commuting play. The main theorem is formalised in Lean, and the paper also covers zero-error communication with entanglement.

7

Unbounded advantage of adaptivity in entanglement-assisted communication

A. Tavakoli2611.11333—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A short, plainly titled separation from a well-cited author; we have tended to underrate short papers like this.

Tavakoli gives tasks that an adaptive receiver solves with three entanglement-assisted messages, while a receiver that measures before reading the message needs unboundedly many. So no bounded amount of classical communication reproduces every correlation of an entanglement-assisted ternary channel. In the other direction, finite entanglement dimension always allows a finite non-adaptive simulation, and d adaptive messages can be simulated to error ε with O(d log(1/ε)) non-adaptive ones.

8

BP-based qLDPC Decoding under Long-range Correlated Noise: Trapping Configurations and Prior Mismatch

A. Dangi, A. Chatterjee2611.10796—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Decoder papers have done well, but this one is a cs.IT cross-list without a known QEC group.

Correlated errors drawn from a hidden Gaussian Markov random field, with every single-mechanism marginal kept exact, change logical error rates of Relay-BP and beam search by orders of magnitude and can reorder them against BP-OSD. Larger relay memory or beam width does not help. Correlations mainly make trapping configurations more likely, and giving the decoders marginals conditioned on the hidden field recovers most of the loss. The authors conclude that prior mismatch is a major cause of the degradation.

Yesterday’s #2 on SciRate

What are local complementations, really?

K. Goodenough16final

Local complementation, which replaces the induced neighbourhood of a vertex with its complement, is the standard operation on graph states. Following Bouchet, Goodenough argues that it is better understood as switching between matroid bases of a stabilizer state. In that language vertex-minors and the statistics of local Pauli measurements are easier to read off, and several earlier proofs about graphs up to local complementation become shorter; the paper works through examples. It is 26 pages and doubles as an introduction to multimatroids for quantum information. We listed it only in Closest to your work, although short stabilizer papers by known authors have repeatedly reached the top five.

Yesterday’s #7 on SciRate

Exploring topological signatures of quantum Fisher information scaling in graph states

M. Hassani, P. Emonts, J. Tura11final

For graph states the quantum Fisher information has a closed form that counts vertex pairs with identical neighbourhoods. Hassani, Emonts and Tura ask whether the Betti numbers of the graph’s clique complex track whether that QFI beats the standard quantum limit. Starting from balanced complete multipartite graphs, adding edges removes both the metrological advantage and the higher Betti numbers, while deleting edges leaves the topology in place long after the QFI has dropped to the SQL. A Monte Carlo search that maximises a Betti number from Erdős–Rényi starts converges to near-extremal multipartite graphs well above the SQL. Betti numbers alone do not fix the QFI. We did not list it.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
