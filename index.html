Hardware · IBM

IBM plans a 520-qubit Nighthawk r3 and a 1,040-qubit linked system for 2027

IBM says the next Nighthawk will run 10,000 two-qubit operations on 520 programmable qubits, and that two of them will be linked in 2027. The figures are design targets, and the chip-to-chip links are still short of IBM’s own fidelity goal.

IBM Quantum (company announcement)IBM · Quantum Computing Report · The Next Web

At its Quantum Developer Conference on 11 November, IBM announced Nighthawk r3, a square-lattice superconducting processor designed for 10,000 two-qubit operations on 520 programmable qubits. The chip has 2,034 physical elements in all, counting couplers and reset elements, and ten chip-to-chip “l-couplers”. IBM plans to join two r3 chips in 2027 into one system of 1,040 qubits. The current chip, Nighthawk r2, arrived in September with 120 qubits, more than 7,500 operations and up to 100,000 samples per second. IBM says partners have run 34 billion circuits on it, with speedups of up to 19× over its earlier processors.

According to IBM, l-couplers between Nighthawk r1 chips reached 99.3% state-transfer fidelity against a 99.9% target, and two cryogenic modules were joined into one system that cooled below 15 mK in under five days. Quantum Computing Report adds that denser couplers cut the Josephson junctions in a Loon module from about 5,000 to 2,000, and that r3 is described as a step toward Starling, the fault-tolerant machine planned for 2029. The partner results are company figures: E.ON reports a 15× speedup on grid optimisation, ORNL a 12× faster magnetic-materials simulation that agrees with neutron scattering, and Algorithmiq a materials circuit that runs 8× faster than the original Heron experiment and would, by its estimate, need 240× more memory for leading tensor-network methods. These circuits sit on the community Quantum Advantage Tracker, where classical groups can challenge them.

We found no comment yet from researchers outside IBM. The trade coverage (The Next Web, SiliconANGLE) repeats IBM’s numbers without checking them; The Next Web points out that 1,040 means two chips of 520 and that the link fidelity is below target. On Hacker News (54 points, 24 comments) several commenters called the release marketing and asked what the machine can do that classical computers cannot. One raised the power draw and helium supply of dilution refrigerators at data-centre scale. Others replied that fidelity has improved markedly since the r2 systems and pointed to the advantage tracker, and one commenter explained that the non-programmable elements handle reset and leakage removal, not computation.

What to watch: whether the tracker’s circuits hold up against new classical simulations; whether the l-couplers reach 99.9% before two r3 chips are joined; and per-gate error rates for r3, which the release does not give. The release also does not mention Kookaburra, the qLDPC memory module that IBM’s 2025 roadmap scheduled for 2026.

Policy · United States

D-Wave finalises up to $100 million in CHIPS funding, and the Commerce Department takes a minority stake

Quantum Computing Report · The Quantum Insider

D-Wave has finalised a definitive agreement with the US Department of Commerce for up to $100 million in milestone-based CHIPS funding, and the department receives a minority, non-controlling equity stake. The money is for research, fabrication and packaging toward a 100,000-qubit annealer and a 10,000-qubit dual-rail gate-model system, which D-Wave says is designed to give 100 logical qubits running more than a million gates. None of this has been demonstrated yet. Third-quarter revenue was $4.5 million, up 21% on a year earlier, with an operating loss of $53.5 million after the Quantum Circuits acquisition and $503 million in cash and short-term investments. Remaining performance obligations rose to $38.3 million (The Quantum Insider).

Yesterday’s # on SciRate

The energetic cost of loading classical data in quantum computers

J. F. Doriguello, A. Luongo, H. Verma6final

Doriguello, Luongo and Verma estimate the energy of one QRAM query on real hardware. They model a superconducting processor with the metric-noise-resource framework, relating fidelity and power to gate duration, relative drive strength and attenuation across the refrigerator stages, and including thermal photons and gate errors. They compare a linear-depth multiplexer with a bucket-brigade design adapted to a native gate set. For a 12-qubit address register the bucket-brigade query reaches fidelity 0.969 at 3.51 × 10−4 J of dynamic energy, 4.38 W average and 64.4 W peak power. At fixed fidelity, the operating point and attenuation profile change average power by a factor of two or more.

Yesterday’s # on SciRate

Simulating closed- and open-system dynamics of Tavis–Cummings lattices on fault-tolerant quantum computers

S. Sarkar, R. Gharat, N. Nguyen, G. Brennen4final

Sarkar, Gharat, Nguyen and Brennen cost out fault-tolerant simulation of Tavis–Cummings lattices (L cavities, N emitters each, photon cutoff nmax), which are hard to simulate classically in 2D once unequal emitter rates break permutation symmetry. Ladder-operator block encodings give circuits for photon hopping and light–matter exchange. The hopping circuit updates occupations in place, which removes an L⌈log2(nmax+1)⌉-qubit register and saves 4.75–8.11% of Toffolis on 16–256 cavities. Closed dynamics use generalised QSP with bounds for spectral functions; open dynamics use a sampled dilation with run counts for g(2)(0). The paper is 68 pages.

Photonics · Xanadu

Xanadu and HyperLight report 7 mdB loss for a thin-film lithium niobate switch, and Xanadu posts $286 million in cash

Xanadu and HyperLight report 7 mdB insertion loss for a thin-film lithium niobate switch, down from 20 mdB in 2025 and measured across production wafers, which works out to fewer than 2 photons lost per 1,000. The comparison point is PsiQuantum’s published figure of about 100 mdB for barium titanate phase shifters; the 7 mdB number is Xanadu’s own measurement, and no switching speed or paper was released. In its third-quarter results Xanadu reported $1.3 million of revenue, a $32.2 million net loss and $286 million in cash, plus a $140 million loan from the Government of Canada for its Toronto manufacturing site.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
