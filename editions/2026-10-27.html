Deals · Software

Intel agrees to buy Classiq, reportedly for $2.3 billion, to pair circuit synthesis with its spin qubits

Intel will buy the Tel Aviv circuit-synthesis company and fold it into its spin-qubit programme. It gives no price; the Israeli press reports about $2.3 billion, more than three times Classiq’s last private valuation, for a company with revenue in the tens of millions.

Intel; Classiq (N. Minerbi, A. Naveh, Y. Naveh)Intel

After the US market closed on 26 October, Intel announced a definitive agreement to acquire Classiq, whose platform synthesizes circuits from a high-level model and compiles them for several hardware back ends. The release gives no price. Calcalist reports about $2.3 billion in cash and Intel stock, and Globes puts the net cost near $2.2 billion after Classiq’s own cash. Closing is expected in the first half of 2027, subject to regulatory approval; until then the companies run separately. Intel says the deal makes its Quantum Computing Group a full-stack business, likens Classiq to electronic design automation in classical chip design, and promises to keep Classiq’s integrations with other vendors’ hardware.

By the reported figure this is the largest quantum acquisition so far, above IonQ’s roughly $1 billion purchase of Oxford Ionics. Calcalist says Classiq has raised about $200 million, most recently a $110 million round in 2025 that valued it at an estimated $600–700 million, and employs about 150 people; Intel has set aside more than $100 million in retention grants. Ynet lists BMW, Rolls-Royce, HSBC and Citi among its customers. Intel has worked on silicon spin qubits and cryogenic control chips since 2015 but has had little application software of its own. CEO Lip-Bu Tan invested in Classiq personally before taking the job; people close to the company told Calcalist that he recused himself from the talks.

We found no comment yet from researchers or from other hardware companies. Coverage so far comes from the Israeli business press and specialist outlets such as Quantum Computing Report, and its doubts are about price: Calcalist calls the deal a steep multiple of revenue in the tens of millions, in a sector where listed companies have mostly traded poorly. The open technical question is whether Classiq stays neutral. Its value to users has been that one model targets many machines, and its reported investors include IonQ, AMD Ventures and Qualcomm Ventures. For now, Intel’s promise to stay modality-agnostic is the only assurance that competitors’ back ends keep full support once the compiler belongs to a hardware maker.

Watch for the regulatory filings and the final split between cash and stock, which Intel will set at closing; for whether other hardware vendors keep their Classiq integrations during the long wait to close; and for what Intel says next about its spin-qubit roadmap, since software that targets many back ends is worth most to a hardware maker whose own chips are not yet the obvious choice. The deal follows IonQ in assembling a full stack by acquisition, this time from the software end.

Policy · United Kingdom

Britain’s £30 million testbed programme put seven machines at Harwell; the NQCC’s Phase 1 report says delivery ran late but six systems are now under evaluation

The Quantum Insider

The National Quantum Computing Centre’s Phase 1 report, summarized by The Quantum Insider, covers seven systems placed at Harwell across five modalities: Aegiq and ORCA (photonics), Infleqtion and QuEra (neutral atoms), Oxford Ionics (trapped ions), Quantum Motion (silicon spins) and Rigetti (superconducting), with QuEra building its own facility on the campus. For many suppliers it was the first system delivered to, and run for, a remote customer. Delivery slipped: the target of two-thirds of testbeds working by 31 March 2025 was only partly met, though all seven contracts were completed during 2025. Rigetti grew its machine from 24 to 36 qubits and added Riverlane’s Deltaflow for error-correction experiments, and collaborative projects drew £4.6 million in follow-on funding. Suppliers reported trouble getting packaging, electronics testing and cloud services. Six testbeds are now in independent technical evaluation with Edinburgh’s Quantum Software Lab, due to finish in the second quarter of 2027, and the NQCC says it is too early to judge the programme’s wider impact.

AI and proofs

An AI company posts Lean-checked results on five open quantum problems, and a Tasaki paper credits an OpenAI model

PSI

PSI, a company that says it is building physical superintelligence, posted results on 23 October on five entries of the open quantum problems list, produced with its AI research system, each with a paper, a Lean formalization and verification code. It claims full answers to three: local observables of a massive free scalar field in 3+1 dimensions cannot violate CHSH beyond a finite separation, although the regions stay entangled (OQP 12); Fourier-phase measurements are optimal for CGLMP and unique up to unitaries (OQP 27); and a counterexample on causal processes with entangled ancillas (OQP 43). It also claims that 2(d−1) PPT maps always suffice to break entanglement (OQP 38) and that Werner states with 1/d < α ≤ 37/80, d ≥ 3, are three-copy undistillable (OQP 2), leaving the many-copy question open. We found no outside check yet. In today’s listing, Hal Tasaki’s paper on the S=1 J–D chain (arXiv:2610.34330) builds on OpenAI’s proofs of the Haldane gap and credits ChatGPT Astra with substantial parts of the inductive estimates, and Ding, Lin and Wang (pick #4) declare that AI suggested proof strategies for their lower bounds.

Basis-set error bounds for first-quantised quantum simulation of chemistry

D. W. Berry (Macquarie, Queensland), A. Tikku (Sydney), S. Simon (Queensland), R. Babbush (Google Quantum AI)2610.35205—scites●●● High confidence

Why here The model ranks it 17th because it cannot see that this makes rigorous an assumption behind Google’s chemistry resource estimates; Fang and Zhang’s discretization-error paper, which it supersedes for isolated systems, finished second on 20 October with 22 scites.

First-quantised simulations of chemistry on plane-wave or grid bases price their resources on the assumption that the ground-state energy converges to the continuum at O(1/N) in the basis size N. The rate came from numerics and from simpler problems: Dupuy proved it for a periodic one-electron Coulomb Hamiltonian, and Fang and Zhang (arXiv:2610.25260) bounded the energy error under H2 regularity but needed N = O(ε−2) for isolated systems. Berry (Macquarie, Queensland), Tikku (Sydney), Simon (Queensland) and Babbush (Google Quantum AI) prove the O(1/N) rate for the full many-electron Coulomb problem, with clamped or quantum nuclei, on plane waves and on spatial grids with periodic or open boundaries.

The energy result needs no spectral gap, no non-degeneracy and no regularity assumption on the eigenfunction. The Coulomb singularity is controlled by a quadratic-form bound of the potential by the kinetic energy, Hardy-type estimates applied register by register, and on the grid a truncated Fourier kernel that keeps coincident particles finite. For dynamics from initial states in the domain of H2, the state error is weaker, O(N−5/6 + t/N), but observables do better: O(N−5/3 + tN−1) for a smoothed position observable and O((t + log N)/N) for the kinetic energy.

For isolated molecules with clamped nuclei this gives N = O(ε−1 log3(1/ε)) where Fang and Zhang needed ε−2, which feeds directly into qubit counts for first-quantised algorithms. The paper runs to 149 pages and the constants are not optimised. It bounds only the discretization error: Trotter or block-encoding error, phase estimation and the overlap of the prepared state with the target eigenspace still need their own budgets, and the open-boundary grid assumes the true evolution does not leak past the box.

Distillable entanglement is continuous

L. Schmitt (ETH Zurich, IBM Research Zurich), D. Sutter (IBM Research Zurich)2610.35722—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A clean answer to a basic question in entanglement theory from ETH and IBM Zurich; Sutter’s continuity and entropy work is widely read, and the model’s 20th place reflects that it cannot tell a resolution from a routine bound.

Distillable entanglement ED, the optimal LOCC rate of ebits per copy, was not known to be continuous. Vidal had shown continuity only in the interior of the distillable states, leaving open the boundary between distillable and undistillable states, where the bound-entanglement questions sit. Schmitt (ETH Zurich, IBM Research Zurich) and Sutter (IBM Research Zurich) prove that for ½‖ρ − σ‖1 ≤ ε < 1/16, |ED(ρ) − ED(σ)| ≤ 4√ε log d + 4h(√ε), with d the smaller local dimension, the same form as known continuity bounds for conditional entropy, entanglement cost and relative entropy of entanglement.

The proof is constructive. They write a common mixture ω of ρ and σ using the positive and negative parts of ρ − σ, then give an LOCC protocol that turns about n copies of σ plus nκδ ebits into n copies of ρ, with κδ vanishing with the distance. ω⊗n is prepared by asymptotic mixing, with ebits spent to teleport the smaller subsystems, and ρ is extracted by a measurement whose rare outcomes are located by a Grover-type search with a phase oracle run through teleportation. A converse rate bound and the symmetric argument give the inequality. The result is for finite dimensions, and the √ε dependence is probably not tight.

Ancilla-free optimal quantum adder

J. Nie (Shandong), J. Tang (ICT, CAS), X. Sun, W. Zi (Greater Bay Area Quantum Science Center)2610.34446—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Forty-nine places above its prior: the model cannot see that this closes the gap between ancilla-free adders and ones that use ancillas, with matching lower bounds, and arithmetic results with optimal counts have done well this month (T-efficient synthesis, 32).

Adders are the inner loop of Shor-type arithmetic, and the trade-off between ancillas, size and depth has been mapped for two decades. Takahashi, Tani and Kunihiro gave ancilla-free ripple-carry adders with linear size and depth; carry-lookahead adders reach O(log n) depth but use O(n) ancillas (Draper et al.) or O(n/log n) (Takahashi et al.); Remaud and Vandaele reached ancilla-free O(n log n) gates with O(log2 n) depth. Nie, Tang, Sun and Zi build an exact n-bit adder from X, CNOT and Toffoli gates with O(n) gates, O(log n) depth and no ancillas under all-to-all connectivity. Both counts match the Ω(n) and Ω(log n) lower bounds that follow from the top output bit depending on every input bit.

The construction speeds up the controlled ladder underlying addition by grouping bits into blocks and borrowing existing control qubits as conditional workspace, in the spirit of Gidney’s conditionally clean ancillas. Done recursively this gives O(n log log n) gates; coordinating propagation between blocks with two short-path forest operations brings it to O(n). Everything is asymptotic: there are no constants, no separate Toffoli count and no numerics, so whether it beats ripple-carry at cryptographic sizes, or survives nearest-neighbour layouts, is open.

Sublinear thermal state and ground state preparation for free fermions via system-bath interaction

Z. Ding (Michigan), L. Lin (Berkeley, Caltech), K. Wang (Michigan)2610.35370—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Lin Lin’s group on Gibbs and ground-state preparation reliably draws the algorithms readers, which the model, at 32nd, does not track; the result breaks a linear-time barrier but only for free fermions, so Medium.

Dissipative preparation of thermal and ground states, whether by Lindbladian Gibbs samplers in the style of Chen, Kastoryano and Gilyén or by system-bath schemes, generally needs total Hamiltonian simulation time at least linear in the system size N, even when the mixing time is polylogarithmic, because Θ(N) jump operators must act. Ding, Wang (Michigan) and Lin (Berkeley, Caltech) show that the barrier can be broken for quadratic fermion Hamiltonians H = Σ hij ci†cj. They couple the system locally to a bath whose size grows with N, through operators independent of h, and filter in time so that a KMS-type detailed balance holds; decoupling the total Hamiltonian into N independent fermionic modes is what allows the stronger coupling.

With a bath in vacuum and no bath Hamiltonian, thermal states at fixed β and bounded bandwidth are reached in total time Õ(√N/ε) from any initial state. With a bath carrying a random shared frequency, gapped ground states take time polylogarithmic in N/ε. The authors’ earlier single-ancilla scheme needed Õ(N4). Lower bounds show √N/ε is tight for the zero-bath protocols, traced to fourth-order deviations from the Gibbs state, and that random-bath thermal preparation is stuck at Ω(N/ε) unless detailed balance holds for almost every frequency. Interacting systems are left open.

Decoding with alternating weighted graph sparsification for quantum low-density parity-check codes

J. Etxezarreta Martinez, F. Garcia-Herrero, R. M. Otxoa, R. Dastbasteh (Navarra, Complutense Madrid, Hitachi Cambridge)2610.35735—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The model’s eighth, and decoder papers have twice reached the top four this month; the Navarra–Madrid group is less followed than the Relay-BP authors and releases no code, so Medium.

Ensembles of belief-propagation decoders such as Relay-BP and Vibe close most of the gap to BP-OSD on qLDPC codes, but the post-processor still sets the latency tail. DAWG, from Etxezarreta Martinez, Garcia-Herrero, Otxoa and Dastbasteh, runs serial-schedule min-sum BP for some iterations on the full detector error model, then maps the posteriors through a different transfer matrix for each ensemble member onto a sparsified Tanner graph and continues there, with randomized schedules as in Vibe. The sparsified graphs keep roughly 10–30% of the variable nodes.

Under circuit-level depolarizing noise in Stim, DAWG+LSD-0 matches Relay-BP on the [[144,12,12]] gross code and Vibe+LSD-0 on superdense colour-code circuits up to distance 9, with ensembles of at most 15 members instead of 32. On the gross code at p = 0.1%, LSD-0 runs about once in ten million rounds, roughly ten times less often than with Vibe, and on the smaller graph, which eases a hardware implementation. The logical error rates appear only in figures, and the decoder code is not released.

A Control Line Multiplexing Framework for Scalable Quantum Computing

D. Li, N. Zhou, …, X. Zhu, J.-W. Pan (USTC)2610.35227—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A Pan Jianwei and Zhu Xiaobo hardware paper with a clear scaling argument; our notes say big-lab hardware papers with modest headline numbers rarely reach the top five.

Dedicated control lines for every qubit will not fit a million-qubit dilution refrigerator. Li, Zhu, Pan and colleagues at USTC make every gate all-microwave and frequency-division multiplexed, using polychromatic pulses and band-pass filters, which they say cuts the line count by one to two orders of magnitude; two-qubit gates, normally driven by unipolar flux pulses, were the missing piece. In a 1:4 demonstration, parallel multiplexed CZ gates reach (6.4 ± 0.2)×10−3 average error and parallel XY(π/2) gates (10.6 ± 0.2)×10−4, with no statistically significant cost from multiplexing. Whether that holds beyond four qubits per line is untested.

7

Exact Identification of Quantum Correlations in the Minimal Bell Scenario

L.-L. Sun, J. Wang, S. Yu2610.34364—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Forty places above its prior: the abstract claims to settle the quantum boundary in the CHSH scenario, which the model cannot weigh; big claims from smaller groups draw readers but also caution, so Low-Med.

In the minimal Bell scenario, two parties with two binary measurements each, the extreme points of the quantum set are known, but a usable membership test for the set itself was missing. Sun, Wang and Yu prove that a correlation is quantum if and only if it decomposes into an extreme quantum correlation and a classical one, through a theorem that every Bell functional with a quantum advantage has a unique quantum maximizer. They also classify the maximal faces containing each nonlocal exposed point by their local deterministic vertices. A claim to have resolved a long-standing problem will be checked against the NPA-hierarchy literature.

Junction Hook Errors in the Color Code

S. Issa, M. Martonosi (Princeton)2610.33347—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A direct follow-up to Kishony and Fowler’s colour-code schedules from Martonosi’s group; colour-code circuits have an audience, but this is a careful correction rather than a new scheme.

Kishony and Fowler chose CNOT orders for each colour so that hook errors in the triangular colour code no longer shortcut any single anyon type. Issa and Martonosi find a class this misses: junction hooks, whose two data qubits end strings of two different colours where the three strings of a logical operator meet. At d = 7, 9 and 11, every non-corner plaquette admits one that, with d − 2 data faults, produces a logical error from d − 1 faults, so bulk stabilizers still need flags. A minimum-weight logical has at most one junction, which explains why the schedule still reaches distance d − ⌊(d+3)/6⌋.

Algorithms · Quantum Art

Quantum Art proposes preparing wave solutions on the dispersion surface instead of inverting the Helmholtz operator

In a preprint (arXiv:2609.10282) the Israeli trapped-ion company prepares states on the resonant surface k2 = ω2/c2 and encodes source positions as phases, so no linear-system solver, and no condition-number dependence, enters. It claims O(n2) two-qubit and T gates for an n-qubit coordinate register, at most 5n workspace qubits, and post-selection success falling as one over the number of sources; a 3D domain tens of kilometres across at centimetre resolution would fit in about 100 logical qubits. The resource figures are the company’s own, with no error analysis or classical comparison, and runs on its hardware are a stated plan.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
