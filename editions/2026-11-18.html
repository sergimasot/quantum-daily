Theory · Self-correcting memory

Hayden proves the 3D self-correcting memory survives quantum perturbations at zero temperature

In an 84-page single-author preprint, Patrick Hayden proves that a variant of the Balasubramanian–Davydova–Lin code family keeps its thermal stability and is also stable at zero temperature against weak quantum perturbations. The finite-temperature, perturbed case is left to a companion paper that is not yet posted.

Patrick HaydenarXiv:2611.21749—scites

Patrick Hayden has posted the first half of an argument that a three-dimensional, geometrically local, bounded-degree Hamiltonian can protect a qubit passively (arXiv:2611.21749). The starting point is the recursively defined stabilizer family of Shankar Balasubramanian, Margarita Davydova and Ting-Chun Lin (BDL), which earlier this year gave the first 3D model with a memory lifetime that grows with system size below a critical temperature, in the absence of perturbations. BDL left open whether the construction survives generic static perturbations, even at zero temperature. Hayden shows that a slight variant does, on both axes of the perturbation–temperature diagram. With no perturbation it keeps BDL’s thermal stability, with a stretched-exponential lifetime for any local Markovian bath that obeys detailed balance through single-site couplings. At zero temperature, under arbitrary weak perturbations that decay superpolynomially, it keeps a uniform bulk gap, a superpolynomially small ground-space splitting and a superpolynomially long unitary memory time for quasi-locally dressed logical operators.

The question goes back to Kitaev’s proposal of topological quantum memory. The 4D toric code self-corrects, 2D stabilizer models cannot, and earlier 3D candidates such as Haah’s cubic code reach only partial self-correction through a logarithmic energy barrier. The difficulty in BDL’s construction is that it is a multiscale hierarchy rather than a translation-invariant lattice, so the Bravyi–Hastings–Michalakis stability theorem does not apply off the shelf. Hayden establishes local topological quantum order at every level of the hierarchy using what he calls a linearly dilated relaxation of the BHM criteria. A companion paper is meant to combine these tools with finite-temperature worldline cluster expansions and cover the interior of the phase diagram, where perturbations and temperature are both nonzero, which is where a physical memory would operate.

The preprint was submitted on Monday and announced last night, and we found no public comment on it yet from other researchers, on blogs or on Hacker News. The BDL result itself drew specialist coverage in May (The Quantum Insider, PostQuantum), and Davydova presented it at the Simons Institute in April. The headline claim, passive self-correction with perturbations at positive temperature, rests on Part II, which has not appeared. The proved lifetimes are stretched-exponential or superpolynomial, and the codes are recursively defined with non-uniform geometry, so nothing here points to a near-term hardware layout. Eighty-four pages of stability analysis will also take time to check.

What to watch: the companion paper and whether its cluster expansion closes the perturbed finite-temperature case; whether BDL or other groups check the dilated LTQO argument; whether the method applies to other multiscale or fractal codes; and whether anyone proposes a decoder-free memory architecture that uses these Hamiltonians, even as a thought experiment for atom or spin systems with 3D connectivity.

Decoding · SC26

Quantum Machines decodes surface-code syndromes from the controller in under a microsecond per round

Quantum Computing Report · The Quantum Insider

Quantum Machines says it has decoded surface-code syndromes mid-circuit from its OPX1000 controller through NVIDIA’s CUDA-Q QEC server over NVQLink (Quantum Computing Report). No qubits were involved: the test streamed pre-sampled memory syndromes at 0.1% physical error through PyMatching on a GH200 server, at distances 7, 11 and 15 with up to 75 rounds. Per-round decoding took 0.19–0.22 µs at distance 7 and 0.56–0.67 µs at distance 11, under the 1 µs cycle target, but 1.2–1.5 µs at distance 15; the controller-to-decoder round trip was 2.4 µs. Callbacks still go through host CPU threads, with direct GPU dispatch planned, and early access opens in December. The report adds no independent validation. The same week IQM described an upgrade path for its Halocene error-correction line from 150 to more than 1,000 qubits, also decoding over NVQLink, without delivery dates or logical-qubit specifications (The Quantum Insider).

QEDist: Practical Exact Distance Certification for Stabilizer Codes

J. Y. Lee2611.21709—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A QEC tool with public code that certifies exact distances and a circuit fault distance, the kind of DEM-adjacent tool that has led the QEC scites since October; the model agrees (prior 2).

Jong Yeon Lee’s QEDist certifies the distance of stabilizer codes by building candidate logical operators one qubit at a time, using sparse checks, stabilizer equivalence and verified symmetries to cut the tree. Its pruning bound counts both satisfied and unsatisfied checks, since satisfying one check can break another, and drops branches whose lower bound exceeds the remaining weight budget. A completed search with cutoff W either returns a logical of weight at most W or proves none exists.

For lifted-product codes it moves part of the search onto a classical constituent code. Lee reports exact distances for codes with several thousand qubits, including Abelian and non-Abelian LP codes previously known only through upper bounds, distances up to 20 in seconds on one laptop core, and a certified fault distance of 10 for the syndrome-extraction circuit of the [[144,12,12]] gross code. It claims orders-of-magnitude speedups over the exact solvers tested. The code is on GitHub.

Magic state cultivation with lattice-surgery escape on superconducting hardware

Liao, …, Mundada, Hartnett, Baum2611.22035—scites●●● High confidence

Why here QEC on a public IBM chip with record numbers, a category that reached the top three twice this autumn; up from its prior of 11 because the cultivation papers of October drew 58 scites.

Magic-state cultivation grows a T state inside a small color code and then escapes into a larger code. Liao, Schutzman, Munizzi, Mundada, Hartnett and Baum run two escapes to distance 5 on a publicly available IBM Nighthawk: the lattice-surgery escape of Hirano et al., tested on hardware here for the first time, and the grafting escape of Rosenfeld et al., extended with a switch into a matchable code. Decoding uses MWPM or Tesseract on circuit-level models calibrated to the device.

The cultivated T state reaches a logical error probability of 2×10−5 at the injection angle, nearly ten times lower than the previous experiment with about three times the retention. After lattice-surgery escape into the surface code, T-state fidelity stays above 0.99 ten rounds later at about 5% retention, while matchable-switched grafting falls below 0.95 at similar retention. The surface-code logical error per round after escape is about 0.14% at 11% retention. The retention figures depend on postselection on decoder confidence, so throughput is the number to watch.

Local interference algorithms for observables in large random circuits

Suchsland, Ware, Kurilovich, Smelyanskiy, Kechedzhi2611.23207—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Classical algorithms for OTOCs from Google theorists bear directly on OTOC-based advantage claims; ranked ten places above its prior (14) because that question has an audience well beyond the subfield.

Interference is usually expected to give classical Monte Carlo a sign problem in quantum dynamics. Suchsland, Ware, Kurilovich, Smelyanskiy and Kechedzhi argue that this does not hold for observables in random circuits that keep large signals: first-order OTOCs in unstructured random circuits and time-ordered charge correlators in number-conserving ones. Both are approximated by classical stochastic processes, then improved by a series of coherent corrections from clusters of gates, computed with a mix of Monte Carlo sampling and tensor networks.

Numerically the cost scales polynomially in circuit size and target precision. To explain this, the authors map the ensemble-averaged error onto partition functions of replica statistical models, which they analyse perturbatively and numerically for 1D brickwork circuits, and they argue the polynomial cost extends to arbitrary precision and the thermodynamic limit. The results are for random circuit ensembles; how far they reach structured circuits or the specific OTOC experiments behind advantage claims is the open question.

Probing the real-time decoding accuracy bounds in trapped ion architectures

Meinking, Takou, Brown2611.21906—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A decoder paper from Ken Brown’s group with orders-of-magnitude gains on bivariate-bicycle codes; ranked twelve places above its prior (17) because decoder papers keep finishing in the top five.

Trapped ions run QEC cycles in milliseconds, which leaves time that decoders rarely use. Meinking, Takou and Brown run a sliding-window decoder in which a fast, lower-accuracy decoder handles every window and hands a small fraction to a slower, more accurate one. The switch is driven by soft output that they extract efficiently from the fast decoder for any QLDPC code, flagging low-confidence windows.

In memory simulations of bivariate-bicycle codes with noise models informed by trapped-ion hardware, the hybrid lowers the logical error rate by up to three to four orders of magnitude relative to BP-LSD and two to three relative to the beam-search decoder, depending on the code and the physical error rate, while keeping latency low. Spare classical processing time thus buys accuracy that would otherwise need a larger code.

CSS circuits are ICM circuits without magic states

Paler2611.21685—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A short note answering an open question in last Friday’s Gidney–Jones paper (our #1 and the listing’s top paper at 10); short stabilizer results by known names beat long papers three times this month.

Gidney and Jones defined CSS circuits (X, Z, MX, MZ and CNOT), showed they can be sampled at O(1) cost per gate, and suspected the class was not new. Alexandru Paler points out that, up to a normal form, it is the initialisation–CNOT–measurement (ICM) form introduced in 2015 for compiling fault-tolerant circuits, restricted to |0⟩ and |+⟩ preparations. The ICM construction also answers their question about non-CSS-ness as a resource: every Clifford+T circuit compiles to a CSS circuit acting on |Y⟩ and |A⟩ inputs.

Teleportation of Logical Qubits using Lattice Surgery with Superconducting Circuits

Hofele, Song, …, Müller, Hellings, Wallraff2611.23165—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Logical teleportation by lattice surgery from the Wallraff lab on 41 qubits (prior 4); hardware papers without a record number have started slowly this autumn, so it sits below the tools and theory.

Hofele, Song and colleagues in Andreas Wallraff’s group, with Markus Müller, teleport logical states between two distance-3 surface codes on a 41-qubit superconducting device using lattice surgery. They follow the transfer of logical information step by step through the time evolution of logical observables, find that repeated stabilizer rounds after the merge restore fault tolerance and raise the fidelity of the teleported state, and improve it further by trimming gate count and sequence time. The paper also documents the device and gate sequences in detail.

Black holes as black boxes: a query lower bound on python's lunch decoding

Waddell, Xu2611.21723—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here The model’s top pick, a tight Θ(2m/2) query bound linking holography and complexity; the authors have no track record in our data, which kept Monday’s collision bound at 2 scites.

The python’s lunch conjecture ties the cost of bulk reconstruction in holographic tensor networks to an exponential post-selection cost. Waddell and Xu model the lunch as a Haar-random unitary applied to a logical input and ancillas, followed by post-selection on m qubits. Given one copy of the encoded state and coherent, controlled access to U and U†, recovering even one logical bit with constant advantage needs Ω(2m/2) queries, which matches amplitude amplification and gives Θ(2m/2).

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks, showing each event's next deadline: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
