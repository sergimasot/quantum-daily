Experiment · Contextuality

Google plays contextuality games on up to 105 qubits and proposes them as a hardware benchmark

In Science, Google Quantum AI reports magic-square, GHZ-type and hidden-linear-function tasks on 72- and 105-qubit Willow processors, beating classical bounds for bounded-resource strategies. We found no comment yet from researchers outside the team.

S. Kumar, E. Rosenberg, D. Maslov, C. Gidney, M. Devoret, P. Roushan and colleagues (Google Quantum AI)Science · Quantum Computing Report · The Quantum Insider · arXiv

Google Quantum AI published in Science on 19 November a set of contextuality experiments on its Willow processors, with 72 and 105 qubits (Quantum Computing Report; preprint arXiv:2512.02284, first posted in December 2025 with Shashwat Kumar of Princeton as first author). On the Mermin–Peres magic square, the device won 98.3% of rounds against a classical ceiling of 8/9, about 88.9% (The Quantum Insider). A many-player GHZ-type game stayed above its classical bound through 71 players, with the margin shrinking as players were added, and a 2D hidden-linear-function task beat the classical success rate on grids of up to 105 qubits (Interesting Engineering).

The hidden linear function problem is the one Bravyi, Gosset and König used in 2018 to prove that shallow quantum circuits outperform constant-depth classical circuits. The Google paper keeps that framing: its classical bounds apply to strategies with limited resources, such as players who cannot communicate or circuits of fixed depth, and say nothing about simulating Willow on a classical computer. The new elements are scale and the tie to fault tolerance: contextuality is the resource behind magic states, and Google proposes these games as a fixed benchmark that does not move as classical simulation improves (Google Quantum AI on X).

We found no reaction yet from researchers outside Google. Coverage so far comes from the specialist and technology press, which repeats the headline numbers and notes that winning these games is not a useful computation. A DevX commentary raises the obvious experimental question, whether noise or calibration artefacts could imitate a contextual signature (DevX). Tests of this kind on a single device also depend on the measured observables being compatible, which imperfect gates only approximate. The preprint has been public for almost a year, and the team presented the work at the APS Global Physics Summit in March (APS abstract).

What to watch: whether IBM, Quantinuum or neutral-atom groups run the same games, which would make the benchmark comparative rather than a one-company figure; how the GHZ-game margin scales with gate fidelity on the next Willow revision; and whether the authors or others state how much of the 105-qubit success rate survives once the compatibility assumption is relaxed.

Securities regulators find firms and supervisors early in quantum-security preparation

The Quantum Insider · CoinDesk

The International Organization of Securities Commissions has published a survey of its members on quantum computing. It finds most firms and regulators at an early stage in protecting trading and payment systems against quantum attacks, and no surveyed jurisdiction reporting routine use of quantum computing in regulated financial services (The Quantum Insider). CoinDesk reads the report as a call for banks and blockchain operators to begin migrating to post-quantum cryptography now (CoinDesk). The G7 central-bank working group and the EU’s financial supervisors made similar points earlier this year.

Policy · Europe

The EU Quantum Act proposal slips to 24 November

MLex · The Quantum Insider

The European Commission’s Quantum Act, due on 18 November, is now tentatively scheduled for 24 November, according to MLex, which notes that adoption dates stay provisional until confirmed (MLex). The leaked draft would reserve public quantum procurement for EU-controlled suppliers and those covered by reciprocal agreements, target about 100 error-corrected logical qubits per system by 2030 or soon after, and set no dedicated budget, leaving funding to the successor of Horizon Europe and to national and private sources (The Quantum Insider).

Certified Discovery of Quantum Phase Diagrams

Y. Liu, J. Preskill, H.-Y. Huang2611.25064—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Preskill and Huang ask whether a machine can provably discover phases and answer yes with certificates; a day after Preskill’s threshold paper drew 101 scites, we put it 30 places above its prior (31) on the names and the plainly stated question.

Machine-learning approaches to phase diagrams need an order parameter, training data or a guess at the symmetry-breaking pattern, and none of them certify the transitions they report. Liu, Preskill and Huang ask whether a machine can provably discover the phases of a smooth family of geometrically local Hamiltonians, and show that it can under physically motivated assumptions.

Their quantum algorithm takes a classical description of the family and unverified preparations of reference ground states, and needs only that at least one preparation is correct in each gapped region. It transports each reference state through parameter space, quasi-adiabatically or adiabatically, and taps it with a frequency-filtered local probe. Excitations created by the probe signal that the gap is closing, and each reported transition comes with a verifiable certificate. The authors prove that for continuous transitions the gap closing can always be detected with the frequency-filtered gradient of the Hamiltonian. Because the probe is gentle, a single reusable copy of each reference state supports the whole exploration when the ground state is unique, and polynomially many copies suffice when it is degenerate.

The quantum algorithm runs in polynomial time at inverse-polynomial gap resolution. A classical algorithm reaches quasipolynomial time at constant gap resolution, which places the quantum advantage, if any, in resolving small gaps. Numerics on several model systems illustrate the method.

This continues Huang’s programme of provable learning for many-body physics, now aimed at discovery rather than prediction from data. What it leaves open is mainly in the assumptions: correct reference states in every gapped region, and smooth families. Whether first-order transitions are always detectable is not addressed in the abstract.

The Clifford Hierarchy from Clifford Stabilizers in 2D

A. Warman, S. Schafer-Nameki2611.25025—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A short paper that refutes a stated conjecture about 2D codes with Clifford stabilizers, the kind of plainly titled stabilizer result that has topped our listings; close to its prior (9).

The Bravyi–König theorem confines constant-depth logical gates of 2D topological Pauli stabilizer codes to the Clifford group. Codes whose stabilizers are themselves Clifford operators can exceed this, but known qubit examples gain only one level of the Clifford hierarchy, and it was conjectured that one level is the general limit.

Warman and Schafer-Nameki show that the limit does not hold. They give two explicit constructions, built from copies of the quantum doubles of D4 and ℤ2, with k logical qubits and a transversal logical Ck−1Z gate, which sits at level k of the hierarchy. The physical circuits use only Z or CZ gates. The new ingredient is the logical encoding, which depends on non-Abelian group multiplication.

For fault tolerance this puts arbitrarily high non-Clifford logical gates on 2D codes with local Clifford stabilizers, at the price of measuring Clifford stabilizers and decoding non-Abelian quantum doubles. The abstract gives no thresholds or decoders, and no word on how the code distance behaves as k grows.

3

Scaling classical hardware for accurate real-time quantum error correction

C. Jin, S. F. Yelin, A. Gu2611.26447—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A streaming FPGA decoder with headline accuracy numbers on the surface code and the gross code; decoders with hardware numbers have repeatedly reached our top five, so 19 places above its prior (22).

Real-time decoding for superconducting processors forces a trade between speed and accuracy, and the accuracy lost to fast decoders is paid back in larger codes. Jin, Yelin and Gu co-design a streaming neural decoder, Rapids, with an FPGA: the network is built from operations that map cheaply onto the device and is trained in the FPGA’s integer arithmetic.

On the distance-19 surface code, Rapids decodes each round of syndromes in under a microsecond on a single FPGA, fast enough for superconducting cycle times, with low latency, and its logical error rate is 240 times lower than that of existing real-time hardware decoders. The same design runs on the colour code and on the [[144,12,12]] bivariate-bicycle code, where it is 16 times more accurate than the real-time Relay decoder. Spread over several FPGAs or hardened into custom silicon, the authors argue, it becomes faster, more accurate or able to handle larger codes. The abstract does not say which noise model the comparisons use, or how training cost grows with distance.

4

Non-Binary Detector Error Models

S. Tinelli, F. Lázaro, F. García Herrero2611.26395—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The model’s top paper and a DEM tool of the kind that drew strong QEC scites in early November; Medium because the authors have no record in our data.

Stim and nearly all decoders decompose every circuit fault, such as the 16 two-qubit Paulis after a CNOT, into independent binary error mechanisms. Tinelli, Lázaro and García Herrero instead model each fault location as one random variable over its own alphabet, and give a split–merge–regroup transformation that builds a compact non-binary detector error model from any Clifford circuit by merging components that flip the same detectors and observables.

Non-binary DEMs represent any Pauli channel exactly, while binary DEMs are exact only for channels that factorise. For factorising channels the authors prove the two are equivalent under degenerate quantum MAP decoding but not under quantum MAP decoding. Belief propagation still passes one scalar per edge. On surface, colour and bivariate-bicycle codes, the Tanner graphs have four to five times fewer edges, BP and BP+OSD decode more accurately than on the binary model, and BP+OSD runs up to 9.4 times faster on a CPU.

5

Ellis: Extremely Fast Quantum Circuit Simulation under Qubit Loss and Leakage

Y. Hou, G. S. Ravi2611.26173—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A Pauli-frame simulator with an open-source package and a 1,000-fold speed-up on the gross code; near its prior (8) and the sort of QEC tool readers use.

Loss and leakage drop every operation on the affected qubit, so each shot executes a different Clifford circuit, and Pauli-frame simulation, which XORs sampled flips onto one common reference record, breaks. Hou and Ravi show that many fault-tolerant primitives keep a common reference under loss anyway, among them CSS memory, lattice surgery and transversal CNOT. Such circuits can be simulated exactly under Pauli noise and loss by a Pauli frame with per-shot masks for the dropped operations.

They define frame-CSS circuits, CSS up to local single-qubit basis changes given a loss model, prove that every frame-CSS circuit has a common reference, and give a certifier that runs in near-linear time in practice. The open-source Ellis package samples 952,000 shots per second on one CPU core for the gross code under loss and Pauli noise, at least 1,000 times faster than Clifft and PPVM. Circuits outside the frame-CSS class still need slower methods.

Classical Oracle Separation of QMA from the QCMA Hierarchy and Quantum Cryptography Even When P = QCMA

A. Cakan2611.26574—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Answers an open question of Kretschmer, Qian and Tal, in the field where we miss the most top-ten papers; 27 places above its prior (33), though Wang’s related separation drew only 18 yesterday.

Cakan introduces Codeword-Hash pairs in Noise and proves it is in QMA but hard for the whole QCMA hierarchy in the query model. This gives a classical oracle relative to which P = BQP = QCMA = QCMAH, yet QMA is not contained in QCMAH. Relative to such an oracle, pseudorandom states with classical keys and public-key encryption with copy-protected quantum decryption keys still exist, which answers an open question of Kretschmer, Qian and Tal (STOC 2025). The paper is under submission to STOC 2027.

High-temperature Gibbs states are uniformly Markovian and trivial

A. D. Kim, T. H. Yang, J. Y. Lee2611.26626—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Conditional-mutual-information results on mixed-state phases have reliably reached 15–30 scites, and many-body theory is our third-largest source of misses; 39 places above its prior (46).

Kim, Yang and Lee prove that above a constant temperature, Gibbs states of local non-commuting qudit Hamiltonians have exponentially decaying mutual information and conditional mutual information for any spatial tripartition, including ones that do not cover the system: a uniform Markov property. Their tool is a classical representation in an overcomplete basis from a projective 2-design, whose cluster expansion converges with positive weights at high temperature. Separability follows, and the states lie in the same mixed-state phase as the maximally mixed state.

8

When the Noise Gets Noisy: Quantum Error Correction with Disordered Noise and Misinformed Decoding

C. T. Aitchison, L. H. English, B. Béri, D. J. Williamson, C. K. McLauchlan2611.26506—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Known QEC theorists on decoding with imperfect noise knowledge, close to its prior (6); Low-Med because the error-correction picks above it are more concrete.

Aitchison, English, Béri, Williamson and McLauchlan treat physical error rates as random variables to model decoding with incomplete knowledge of disordered noise. The entropy of the noise distribution sets the threshold, and Bhattacharyya coefficients bound the sub-threshold decay of logical failures. Maximum-likelihood and minimum-weight decoding of surface codes agree with the theory under code-capacity, phenomenological and circuit-level noise, including error distributions measured on open-access superconducting devices. The authors suggest devices report these two quantities rather than mean or median error rates.

Optimal Joint Bounds for Sparse Ground-State Energy Estimation and Ground-State Preparation

D. Patel17final

We listed this single-author paper under Also; it finished eighth with 17. Patel gives matching upper and lower bounds in the sparse-access model: Θ(√s log(1/ε)/(γδ)) queries for ground-state energy estimation and Θ(√s log(1/ε)/(γΔ)) for ground-state preparation, where s is the sparsity, γ the overlap of the initial state and δ or Δ the precision or gap. The lower bounds already hold for stoquastic Hamiltonians, so sign-free structure does not lower the query cost. It is a short, tight bound on a central task, the profile our notes say tends to outperform.

Parity Push Post-Selection with Soft Parity Constraints for Quantum LDPC Codes

11final

This was in Closest to your work and finished ninth with 11. Parity push post-selection adds one check node and one variable node per logical observable to the detector-error-model Tanner graph, so the decoder sees soft parity constraints on the logicals and can flag shots whose decision is unreliable. Diraq says its eight-qubit silicon spin quantum computer is installed and running at Equinix’s Sydney data centre, with cryogenics and control electronics in a self-contained unit drawing under 20 kW. How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.
