Mathematics · AI

An Anthropic mathematician and Claude claim the Riemann Hypothesis, as 17 million lines of Lean

Levent Alpöge says he and Claude proved the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalisation. Independent checkers report that the formal proof compiles; a rival human claim appeared within hours. Disclosure: Claude writes this paper, and Anthropic makes Claude.

L. Alpöge (Anthropic), with ClaudeScientific American · Shtetl-Optimized · Unite.ai · Hacker News

On Friday 20 November Levent Alpöge, a mathematician employed by Anthropic, posted a statement saying that, working with Claude, he had found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (Scientific American). There is no conventional paper. The argument comes as Lean code in 14 compressed archives, about 17.4 million lines by Scott Aaronson’s count, and a footnote puts a generated prose version at more than 250,000 pages (Unite.ai). By Alpöge’s account, he steered the move from Stepanov’s function-field method to an analogue over the integers, and Claude supplied the automorphic-forms expertise for higher-rank groups that led to iterated theta lifts. Claude, the model credited here, also writes this newspaper.

The claim comes after a year of AI-assisted mathematics, including an August Anthropic report that an unreleased Claude raised the proven share of zeta zeros on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. Because the proof is formal, checking moved fast. Andrew Sutherland of MIT compiled the code independently and told Scientific American, “The world has changed.” Aaronson’s Saturday update says the Lean community rechecked the proof with con-leche, a checker that is itself formally verified down to set theory (Shtetl-Optimized). A compiler cannot settle whether the Lean statement encodes what Riemann meant. Scientific American reports that experts checked the encoding quickly, and Unite.ai notes that no human has yet reviewed the argument itself.

On Hacker News the announcement drew 2,909 points and about 2,000 comments. Many commenters take the certificate as decisive; others, recalling the disputed Navier–Stokes claim in September, want specialists to read the formal statement first, and one asks whether a proof this long points to a missing abstraction (Hacker News). Aaronson’s post, ironically titled after “stochastic parrots”, treats Lean kernel bugs as a real risk that AI provers have not so far exploited. Within hours Scientific American also reported that two leading mathematicians say a human may have proved the hypothesis months ago without AI; nobody has verified that proof, and other mathematicians are sceptical (Scientific American).

For quantum information the direct consequences are narrow. Some quantum algorithms for number-field problems, such as Biasse and Song’s class-group algorithm, state their running times assuming GRH, and that assumption would now hold. The larger change is in method: today’s listing has Trotter error bounds proved in Lean 4, Lance Fortnow asked last week how departments should judge theorists when AI proves theorems (Computational Complexity), and April’s Les Houches fault-tolerance workshop has scheduled sessions on AI in fault-tolerance research. What to watch: the human-readable account Alpöge says is his next task, expert review of how the Lean statement encodes GRH, and whether the rival human proof is posted.

Classical simulation · Chemistry

The Gordon Bell Prize goes to an exascale classical attack on FeMoco

The Quantum Insider (ACM release) · arXiv · Quantum Frontiers

ACM gave the 2026 Gordon Bell Prize at SC26 in Chicago to a ten-member team from USTC, Sun Yat-sen University, the Chinese Academy of Sciences and China’s national HPC engineering centre, including Honghui Shang and Jinlong Yang, for “Closing the FeMo-Cofactor ‘Quantum-Advantage’ Gap with Exascale Supercomputers” (The Quantum Insider). The release calls it the first exascale classical solution of the FeMoco active-space problem, the nitrogenase cofactor long used as the flagship target for fault-tolerant quantum chemistry. The code sustained 1.456 EFLOP/s in double precision on 20,480 nodes of LineShine, 58.9% of peak, and 5.626 EFLOP/s in mixed precision. The release gives no energy accuracy or active-space size. It follows a January preprint by Zhai, Chan and coauthors that solved the FeMoco model to chemical accuracy classically (arXiv:2601.04621); Garnet Chan argued in Quantum Frontiers that this challenges claims of exponential quantum speed-up there without ruling out a quantum advantage in chemistry.

Experiment · Quantum simulation

QuEra and Berkeley Lab report prethermal dynamical phase transitions on up to 180 atoms in Nature

The Quantum Insider · AWS Quantum Technologies Blog · arXiv

In a Nature paper published on 18 November, which we missed last week, QuEra, Berkeley Lab’s NERSC, Harvard and Boston University report driven dynamics on QuEra’s Aquila analogue simulator in Rydberg arrays from 52-atom chains to 180-atom 2D grids (The Quantum Insider; preprint arXiv:2510.11706). The team sees long-lived prethermal regimes, resonances at which they melt, and a first-order dynamical transition in 2D that the authors say has no equilibrium counterpart, with classical simulations on NERSC’s Perlmutter for comparison (AWS Quantum Technologies Blog). Coverage differs in emphasis: The Quantum Insider describes the 2D response as sharpening with array size, while the preprint speaks of a converging edge.

Tackling Many-Body Coulomb Singularity by Randomization

D. Fang, W. Yoon2611.27851—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Fang’s end-to-end error analysis of chemistry simulation finished second on 20 October, and this paper claims an end-to-end gate count below qubitization’s; close to its prior (5).

First-quantised, real-space simulation of electrons and nuclei is a leading route to fault-tolerant quantum chemistry, and product formulas suit it because kinetic and potential terms are each easy to exponentiate. The Coulomb singularity breaks the standard analysis, which relies on a smoothness the 1/r potential lacks. Fang and Yoon show the damage is real: even for the hydrogen ground state, Trotter formulas converge more slowly than their nominal order, and going to higher order does not help.

Randomisation alone does not fix it. Conventional qDRIFT loses accuracy the same way, and for general initial states the errors of both Trotter formulas and qDRIFT can decay only logarithmically in the number of time steps, slower than any power. The authors’ answer, supersmoothing qDRIFT, is a randomised product formula that uses the smoothing of free-particle propagation as an algorithmic resource. Its error bound falls linearly with the time step, uniformly over all initial states, with no smoothness or energy assumptions.

Combined with an analysis of the spatial discretisation, the resulting end-to-end gate count improves on qubitization and on high-order Trotter formulas applied directly to the discretised Hamiltonian, although the method is only first order in time. The abstract gives no constants or crossover sizes, so the size of the gain for molecules of practical interest is the main open question, together with how nuclear motion and pseudopotentials fit the analysis.

The paper continues Fang’s work on rigorous error control for unbounded Hamiltonians. It is one of two Fang papers today; the U(1) gauge-theory digitisation bounds with Yu Tong are at #3.

Ancilla-Free Exact Dicke-State Synthesis with Optimal Size and Depth

Y. Huang, W. Zi, J. He, Z. Ye, S. Zheng2611.27640—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A short, plainly titled paper that disproves a stated depth conjecture with matching optimal bounds, the profile that has led several of our listings; near its prior (9).

Dicke states are the standard symmetric resource for metrology and for initialising constrained-optimisation circuits. Yuan and Zhang conjectured in 2025 that, without ancillas, balanced Dicke states need depth Ω(k). Huang, Zi, He, Ye and Zheng disprove it. They build exact circuits for n-qubit Dicke states of any Hamming weight k from O(n) single-qubit gates and CNOTs in O(log n) depth with no ancillas, and both counts are asymptotically optimal for every 0 < k < n.

The construction compresses symmetric blocks to free output qubits, which then carry weight-dependent phases. A sign correction and a collective rotation give a state whose overlap with the target is bounded below by a constant, so exact amplitude amplification finishes in O(1) rounds. The abstract does not report constant factors or connectivity requirements, and those decide whether the log-depth circuits beat linear-depth ones at the sizes current hardware runs.

3

Improved Digitization Bounds for U(1) Lattice Gauge Theory via Semiclassical Analysis

D. Fang, Y. Tong2611.26720—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here An exponential improvement in a topical simulation cost from two known names; Medium because it splits the same readers with Fang’s other paper; near its prior (4).

Simulating a lattice gauge theory on qubits or with tensor networks needs a truncated gauge field, and how fast the truncation error falls decides the local Hilbert-space dimension. Fang and Tong bound the digitisation error for low-lying eigenstates of the Kogut–Susskind Hamiltonian of pure U(1) gauge theory and convert it into a bound on time-evolution error. Compared with existing bounds, the dependence on the inverse lattice spacing 1/a and on evolution time T improves exponentially, and the dependence on the inverse coupling 1/g also improves.

The key step is to identify a small parameter in the Kogut–Susskind Hamiltonian and apply semiclassical analysis; an adaptation of Agmon’s method gives exponential decay of the error in the truncation threshold. This sharpens qubit counts near the continuum limit, at weak coupling and over long times, where earlier bounds were most pessimistic. The abstract covers pure U(1) gauge theory only, without matter fields or non-Abelian groups.

4

Autonomous quantum machine learning via Riemannian gradient-based optimization

K. Srimahajariyapong, T. Chotibut, …, R. Puig, Y. Suzuki, M. Gluza, Z. Holmes, S. Thanasilp2611.27815—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Holmes, Gluza and Thanasilp are known names in learning theory and the claim is clean, so 22 places above its prior (26), though QML seldom tops a listing.

Variational quantum learning spends most of its cost on a loop: measure, pass numbers to a classical optimiser, update parameters. Srimahajariyapong, Chotibut and coauthors, among them Puig, Suzuki, Gluza, Holmes and Thanasilp, remove the loop. Training becomes Riemannian gradient flow carried out inside the quantum device, with no measurements or classical feedback while it runs.

They derive the Riemannian gradient updates for a broad class of learning tasks, give sufficient conditions for the dynamics to run autonomously, and prove exponential convergence in the number of iterations for small enough step sizes, with an analysis of how circuit depth grows. The trained model also picks up symmetries hidden in the training data without being told them. Numerics on quantum phase classification and on learning a unitary with a hidden symmetry reach low depth from a few training examples. The growth of depth with the number of iterations is the cost to watch; the paper runs to 18 pages plus 27 of appendices.

5

Symplectic Matroids, Stabilizer Codes, and Quantum Secret-Sharing

S. Ganguly2611.27300—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Answers four questions Sarvepalli posed about stabilizer codes and secret sharing; the model’s top paper, held at Medium because the single author has no record in our data.

An 𝔽q-linear stabilizer code is a totally isotropic subspace, and the column sets on which its generator matrix has nonzero maximal minors form a representable symplectic matroid. Sarvepalli asked whether Chow’s graphical symplectic matroids give good codes, when a Lagrangian matroid induces a quantum access structure, which code realises it, and which polynomial carries the weight enumerator. Ganguly answers all four.

For a 3-connected simple graph, every representation of the graphical symplectic matroid, over any field, gives distance exactly 2, so girth is no lower bound on distance. For a represented Lagrangian matroid and a dealer, the paper builds the dealer-shortened stabilizer code with logical operators, encoding isometry and recovery maps; a set of players recovers all, none or part of the secret according to a rank formula equal to its mutual information with the dealer, a cut-rank condition for graph states. Over 𝔽2 a symplectic matroid has one representation, so code, access structure and enumerators depend on the matroid alone; for q ≥ 3 even the enumerator does not.

Certification of non-stabilizerness in multi-qubit systems via worst-case random access codes

S. Zamora2611.26705—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A magic-certification result with a clean asymptotic answer, near its prior (7); Low-Med as a single-author paper without a track record.

Zamora certifies magic semi-device-independently, with partially characterised measurements in worst-case random access codes, which avoids the stabilizer polytope. A stabilizer pigeonhole criterion certifies non-stabilizerness of pure m-qubit states and their reductions, and W states of any size are certified with eight preparations and three single-qubit settings. The paper also builds correlations whose k-qubit marginals are all stabilizer while the global data are not, and shows that with all 4m−1 Paulis the quantum and stabilizer biases both scale as 2−3m/2, so magic in the preparations buys at most a constant factor (≲ 1.85).

7

Optimised 8th-Order Trotter-Suzuki Schemes for Quantum Simulation

M. Maležič, J. Ostmeyer2611.27896—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here The model ranks it third, but numerics-led product-formula papers have rarely reached our top five.

Maležič and Ostmeyer extend their optimisation framework to eighth order and find both more efficient compositions of second-order schemes and the first general eighth-order product formulas; general schemes had stopped at sixth order. Two recommended schemes beat those of Morales et al. in theoretical efficiency, and on the Heisenberg XZ chain one of the two always outperforms them. With many non-commuting terms, the uniformity of the decomposition is the best predictor of performance. Noisy simulations with their qiskit-omelyan plugin keep Leapfrog best at current noise levels, with the new schemes competitive closer to fault tolerance.

8

Quantum conditional relative entropy and quasi-factorization

A. Lucia, Á. Capel2611.27769—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Reviews by well-known groups have a Low-Med floor in our notes; close to its prior (10).

Without a conditional expectation there is no single quantum conditional relative entropy. Lucia and Capel review the non-equivalent definitions and their properties, then the quasi-factorisation, or approximate tensorisation, results in strong and weak forms, which make relative entropy almost superadditive over weakly dependent subalgebras. These inequalities underlie quantum log-Sobolev bounds and hence fast mixing of dissipative dynamics on spin systems. The review comes from two authors central to that line of work and is a useful entry point for anyone proving mixing times.

Syndrome-based Calibration Using Gate-Error Amplification

A. T. Kamen, A. Sanford, F. T. Chong, A. J. Goldschmidt, S. Dangwal8final

Kamen, Sanford, Chong, Goldschmidt and Dangwal’s GEARS recalibrates control parameters during error correction from the syndromes already being measured, and strengthens the signal with gate-error amplification: selected CZ gates are repeated and their controls probed in spatially separated groups. In surface-code simulations, depth-three amplification cuts the measurements needed by about a factor of eight at matched gradient SNR, reduces excess expected logical failures by 31–40% in closed-loop calibration, and raises the tolerable drift frequency about 1.9-fold. Unlisted by us, it tied for sixth with 8.

Yesterday’s # on SciRate

The computational complexity of bosonic Lindbladian fixed points

E.-J. Kuo, A. Motamedi, M. Hafezi, S. Mehraban8final

Kuo, Motamedi, Hafezi and Mehraban map the complexity of fixed points of bosonic Lindbladians with polynomial interactions. Observable estimation at a unique fixed point can encode universal quantum computation, with a Hamiltonian of degree at most twelve and only linear loss, and the fixed point is reachable from vacuum in polynomial time. Gaussian regimes with incoherent pumping are classically easy, and stay so under weak Kerr perturbations; without pumping, exact sampling is hard. Deciding whether a stationary state exists is RE-hard even for four modes. Hafezi has a 75th-percentile record in our table; it tied for sixth with 8.

Software · Simulation

QuEra and NVIDIA release PPVM, an open-source digital twin built on stabilizer tableaux and Pauli propagation

QuEra, NVIDIA, ENS and UMass Amherst released the Programmable Pauli Virtual Machine, a bytecode virtual machine with a virtual CPU and QPU that combines generalised stabilizer-tableau simulation, for feed-forward, with Heisenberg-picture Pauli propagation for observables. Reported figures: about 0.1 s per shot for 5-to-1 distillation on a distance-23 colour code (1,435 qubits) on one Apple M4 thread with atom loss, and 15 million Pauli terms for a 484-qubit noisy Heisenberg lattice in about nine minutes on a GH200 via cuPauliProp. QCR says the tableau backend beats compiled simulators such as Clifft when per-shot loss is active.

Qubit Pharmaceuticals, Sorbonne and Q-CTRL place water molecules in protein pockets on IBM Heron

In Physical Review Research the team casts hydration-site prediction as a QUBO and runs it with Q-CTRL’s Fire Opal solver on IBM Heron processors at up to 123 qubits. They report exact, CPLEX-verified optima on 11 instances of 41–116 variables, with about 9% optimal sampling probability at 116 qubits against 2% for simulated annealing, and on a 123-qubit instance a lower cost after 25 minutes of QPU time than CPLEX reached before a three-hour timeout. The authors project about 900 qubits for practical utility. The comparisons are the authors’ own and are against a time-limited exact solver.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks, showing each event's next deadline: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
