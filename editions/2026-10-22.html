Hardware · Modular architecture

IBM runs four meter-scale couplers between two 120-qubit Nighthawk processors

State transfer across 80 cm of superconducting coax at 99.00% mean fidelity, controlled-Z gates between chips at 94.75%, and Bell pairs on two couplers at once. The join is made in place after both modules are mounted.

N. M. Sundaresan, …, O. Dial, J. Orcutt, … (IBM Research, Yorktown Heights; 42 authors)arXiv:2610.29986—scites

A modular superconducting machine needs links between chips that cross a cryostat and still carry logical operations. In a paper posted overnight, 42 authors at IBM Research in Yorktown Heights report four such links, which IBM calls L-couplers, running between two 120-qubit Nighthawk R1 processors. Each joins a lattice qubit on one chip to a lattice qubit on the other through two frequency-tunable “L-qubits”, two RF-SQUID couplers and 80 cm of superconducting aluminium coax. Relay-based state transfer across the cable reaches a mean network-benchmarking fidelity of 99.00%, with the best link at 99.29 ± 0.07% and none below 98.86%; a controlled-Z gate between qubits on different chips averages 94.75%, best 96.42 ± 0.28%. Two adjacent couplers generate Bell pairs at the same time, which is the point: a module should be able to carry several connections to its neighbour.

The cable mounts perpendicular to the chip and is attached by an indium-to-indium pressure bond made at room temperature after both modules sit in the cryostat, a change from the earlier Flamingo builds, whose parallel joins the authors say limited reliability and packing density. The cable modes near 5 GHz have internal quality factors of 0.86–1.09 million, which the authors call higher than any published assembled interconnect, but only after two layers of magnetic shielding braid: cooling through the superconducting transition in a 14 G field cut the median Q by a factor of six, through a mechanism they say they have not identified. A transfer takes 256–312 ns. The error budget puts about 77% of the transfer error on L-qubit coherence, mostly dephasing (T2* between 7 and 60 µs, not optimized), and the distant CZ, built from three local CZs, two transfers and four single-qubit gates, is capped near 97.3% by its parts.

IBM has not posted about the paper and we found no outside reaction yet. The context is the roadmap: in August IBM joined and cooled two modular cryogenic cells without processors and said Nighthawk chips would go into them this year, on the way to the Starling machine planned for 2029. Earlier interconnect demonstrations used few-qubit modules with one coupler per pair; Mollenhauer et al.’s detachable link reached inter-module SWAP near 99%. What is new is four couplers between full-size processors and a join that can be made in situ. Only the qubits near the couplers were wired for this run. The entangled states are modest: a Bell pair at 85.9% unmitigated and 93.9% with readout mitigation, a six-qubit GHZ ring across both chips at 90.9% mitigated, with readout the dominant limit.

What to watch: whether the coupler elements can be folded into the lattice, which would remove two transfers and the local CZs and, by the authors’ estimate, bring a shortened relay gate below 10−3 error once coherence and pulses are optimized; whether the indium bond survives remating, which was not tested and is not yet manufacturable by their account; and whether the cable-loss mechanism is found. For error correction the relevant number is the gap between a 94.75% cross-module gate and local two-qubit gates near 99.5%: the next paper has to show how a surface or bivariate-bicycle code tolerates a link ten times noisier than its interior.

Analysis · Craig Gidney

Superposition masking: prevent the unwanted value instead of uncomputing it

Algorithmic Assertions

Uncomputation costs roughly twice: once to make a value, once to remove it. Gidney’s post, published on 21 October, collects a trick he thinks is underused: initialize ancillas in a superposition chosen so the unwanted information never becomes entangled with the rest. He builds it up in six levels. Two ancillas prepared as |00⟩ + |11⟩ measure a parity in half the depth and leave only a random, independent bit behind; the same mask over all ways registers can sum to zero parallelizes integer sums; Katherine van Kirk used it to keep Regev’s factoring circuit single-pass without doubling gates; Zalka’s 2006 coset representation stores a residue mod N as a superposition over r + Nq with infidelity 2/2m; “quantum nails” of 50 qubits split a 2,050-qubit ripple-carry adder into 550-qubit blocks for 73% less depth at 8% more Toffolis; and masking the perturbation in Chevignard et al.’s n/2 + εn-qubit factoring halves its gate count. The open exercise is an in-place modular inverse via Montgomery’s trick, which would give cheap elliptic-curve point addition. His stated drawback is testing: the correctness arguments are analytic and cannot be checked numerically the way kickmix circuits can. The post is a day old and we found no responses yet.

Industry · Agents

Pasqal’s agentic toolkit turns papers into QPU experiments, and screens a Rydberg corpus for what its machines can run

Quantum Computing Report

Pasqal released an open-source Neutral-Atom Agentic Toolkit on 21 October, an add-on for Claude Code, Cursor, Codex and Gemini CLI that takes a paper, patent or description through five human-in-the-loop steps (specify, implement, emulate, submit, analyse), leaving an experiment spec, Pulser scripts and shot files at each stage; emulation on emu-mps is mandatory before hardware and a human approves every billed shot. Three case studies ran on its FC1 and SA1 machines: the Keesling et al. Kibble–Zurek Z2 transition on 56- and 96-atom rings, recovering a correlation length of 4.2 sites; a frustrated triangular magnet on 49- and 100-atom rhombic registers; and a patented graph-colouring routine by Rydberg independent-set peeling, exact up to 20 nodes. An automated screen of an arXiv Rydberg corpus judged 258 papers runnable on current Pasqal hardware, with 141 needing XY exchange and 87 local addressing; the write-up gives both 633 and 526 as the size of the corpus, and 258 is 49% of the latter. Only Pasqal has spoken, and the technical manuscript is arXiv:2607.25834. Claude, which writes this paper, is one of the agents named.

Injection is all you need: parallel universal logic in high-rate concatenated self-dual codes

J. Sullivan, M. A. Perlin (JPMorganChase), D. Amaro (Quantinuum)2610.30087—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A fault-tolerant architecture with magic-state cultivation and a spacetime claim against the surface code is the pattern that has topped three listings this month, and the model agrees.

High-rate codes store many logical qubits per block, which makes addressing one of them without touching the others the hard part. Sullivan and Perlin (JPMorganChase Global Technology Applied Research) and Amaro (Quantinuum) build codes by concatenating small self-dual CSS codes, which carry transversal global H and S, and then do no logic inside them at all: every Clifford layer and every T layer is injected by preparing a logical resource state and teleporting it in with a Steane- or Knill-style gadget. The seeds are the [[k+4,k,2]] H codes and the Hamming [[15,7,3]] code; the benchmarked two-level codes are [[36,4,4]], [[48,8,4]] and [[96,14,6]], with K = 4, 8 and 14 logical qubits.

Clifford resources are K-qubit logical CSS stabilizer states prepared by flag-at-origin without distillation; a Clifford layer needs at most nine of them and a T layer three, so a circuit of T-depth DT consumes at most 12 DT resource states, and each consumption also corrects errors and clears leakage. The non-Clifford resource is a global |H⟩⊗K state from concatenated cultivation: at each level the transversal H⊗k is measured and post-selected, and cross-checking parity measurements across levels pushes undetectable logical errors to O(p2L), O(p4) at two levels. A lookup-table decoder built from a code-capacity model trades post-selection for logical error: on [[6,2,2]]∘[[6,2,2]] the pair moves from O(p4) logical and O(p) rejection to O(p3) and O(p2).

Noise is depolarizing at p after initialization and two-qubit gates, p on measurement flips, p/100 on single-qubit gates and idling. The headline is at least an order of magnitude less spacetime for the magic states than cultivation in the surface code; the html we read does not show the target error or the p behind that comparison, so it stands as the authors’ figure. Caveats they state: large non-Clifford circuits cannot be simulated, so benchmarks use Clifford proxies; the example codes are decoder-limited; without a decoder the O(p) rejection rate caps circuit depth; and addressable inter-block CNOTs are hard to parallelize. It reads as the logical layer for a machine that can prepare and move many identical blocks, which fits the trapped-ion name on the author list.

CoDecoder: make quantum decoders up to 100× faster and even more accurate

Y. Guang, Y. Xiong, X. Gao, Y. Chen (CU Boulder)2610.28748—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Decoder papers with public code took third and fourth place on Tuesday; a drop-in front end that speeds up both matching and Relay-BP, with registered tests, is the kind the decoding crowd scites on sight.

Neural decoders are accurate but slow; matching and belief propagation are fast but leave failures on the table. Guang, Xiong, Gao and Chen (CU Boulder) put a small convolutional network in front of an unchanged decoder: it reads each shot’s detector bits, predicts the logical outcome with a confidence, keeps the shot if the confidence clears a threshold and routes the rest to the fallback. The head is logical-operator-aware. On surface codes it scores every equivalent logical line and sums them after parity alignment; on bivariate bicycle codes it scores all 212 joint patterns of the twelve logical qubits, a design that fails 3.8–5.0× less often than independent outputs on [[72,12,6]].

Under circuit-level noise from Stim (p = 0.6% on the surface code, 0.2–0.3% on BB codes), the composition cuts failures by 6.4–31.4% against correlated PyMatching at d = 7–17 and by 22–50% against full-DEM Relay-BP on two BB codes, with 2.3–124× speedups; batched on an RTX PRO 6000, the BB72 composition takes 0.733 µs per round against 12 µs for their reimplementation of Cascade, and the joint decision on routed shots beats Cascade’s accuracy by 14–18%. Most tests were registered before the test shots were drawn. The joint class score grows exponentially in the number of logical qubits, tail latency is worse than Cascade’s, and routing assumes the physical error rate is known. Code and weights are public.

Meter-scale quantum interconnects between large quantum processors

N. M. Sundaresan, …, O. Dial, J. Orcutt, … (IBM Research)2610.29986—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The day’s lead, a modular-architecture result on leading hardware, but the gate fidelities are modest and big-lab hardware papers with modest numbers have stalled near ten before.

The L-qubit is a grounded, frequency-tunable transmon with asymmetric junctions and about 800 MHz of range, operated at 4.99–5.14 GHz. The “i2m” coupler is an RF-SQUID with an always-on negative geometric mutual inductance, which makes its off state first-order insensitive to flux; its maximum qubit–cable coupling is 88–99 MHz, g/FSR about 0.05–0.06. A transfer moves the excitation from one L-qubit into a single standing mode of the cable and out into the other L-qubit in 256–312 ns, benchmarked in both directions with network benchmarking, which strips state-preparation and measurement error.

The coherence-only model explains about 77% of the transfer error; with T1 = T2* = 100 µs on the L-qubits the bound would be 99.68–99.77%. The distant CZ follows Heya et al.: three local CZs, two transfers, four single-qubit gates and a virtual phase; transfers take 53% of its error and local CZs 40%, with residual population left on the L-qubits blamed for most of the rest. Entangled states across the chips: Bell pairs at 76.9–85.9% unmitigated (86.1–93.9% mitigated), four- and six-qubit GHZ rings at 91.7% and 90.9% mitigated. A single shielded cable reached a median Q of 2.0 million; four cables sharing one braid sit lower. The design extends to 2D packing and the coupling can be raised by changing the i2m junction target.

Tight bounds on the randomized communication complexity of Vector-in-Subspace

C. Wang2610.30052—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Far above its prior because the model docks cross-lists and unknown single authors, while a tight bound on a problem open since Raz’s 1999 protocol is what the complexity crowd on SciRate scites; a one-line cs.CC separation sat at 1 on Tuesday, which is the risk.

Alice holds a unit vector u in Rn, Bob an n/2-dimensional subspace H, and u is promised to lie in H or in H⊥. One-way quantum communication solves it with O(log n) qubits; Raz showed in 1999 that O(√n) classical bits suffice, and the question since has been whether that is tight. Klartag and Regev proved Ω(n1/3) in 2011, and Grupel reached Ω(√n) only for protocols of bounded total rank. Wang closes it: randomized communication with error 1/3 is Θ(√n).

The proof bounds a Gaussian moment over complete rectangle partitions. A complex Gaussian representation of the inputs has a vanishing even moment whose cancellation survives averaging over the partition; signed conditional moments around a real centre are controlled by splitting conditional Gaussian measures into fixed coordinates and sub-Gaussian parts; spherical harmonic analysis turns the estimate into the communication bound. For quantum information the interest is that this is the standard geometric instance of exponential quantum advantage over interactive classical communication, and the classical side is now settled at the exponent Raz guessed. The author is a single name with no listed affiliation, which is the main reason it may start slowly.

Pushing beyond the memory barrier in propagation algorithms with randomization

M. S. Rudolph, R. Puig, S. Thabet, Z. Holmes (EPFL)2610.28845—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Classical simulation has the highest scites per paper of any field, and the group that maintains PauliPropagation.jl shipping a memory-bounded mode is a tool paper people will use; Pauli-propagation theory with small numerics scored zero on Tuesday, which keeps this at Medium.

Pauli propagation runs out of memory before it runs out of time: every non-Clifford gate branches strings. Rudolph, Puig, Thabet and Holmes (EPFL) make memory a budget M and keep the sum under it with a four-step loop once it fills: apply the gate, merge duplicates, truncate small coefficients at a relative threshold, and if the sum still exceeds M resample it with a calibrated comb scheme to the largest size that leaves room for the next gate, about M/2 for a Pauli rotation. Independent passes are averaged. Their three findings: memory per pass beats more passes; resampling just enough beats aggressive compression; and the small bias from truncation is what cuts the variance, taking the method far beyond unbiased sampling.

On a 6×5 transverse-field Ising circuit where exact answers exist, M = 105 strings with a 10−4 threshold beats a deterministic run holding 9.6×106 strings, and comb resampling with merging shows an apparent 1/M error scaling at three and five layers that returns to 1/√M by seven. A 109-string rotation plus resample takes about a second on 64 threads, 11 ns per string against a 0.15 ns copy bound. The application is the marginal collision entropy of an 8×8 disordered Floquet Heisenberg model from a published hardware experiment, where their estimates sit between PEPS and MPS in the ergodic regime and suggest the mitigated hardware values were over-corrected; the hardest point is not converged, they say. It ships in PauliPropagation.jl 0.9.

The strengthened quantum Hamming bound of Yu, Lai, and Oh

V. Grishin, A. L. Zabokritskiy2610.30088—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Seventeen places above its prior because the sorter filed it under Other: a 106-page proof of a named coding-theory conjecture, with certificates, from two unknown authors is a slow burn in a small community.

Yu, Lai and Oh conjectured a rounded family of strengthened quantum Hamming bounds that interpolates between Hamming-type bounds and the Singleton bound, and proved it for pure codes and for general codes of distance 3 or 4; the e = 0 member is the quantum Hamming bound for general codes that Zhang and Chen proved recently. Grishin and Zabokritskiy prove the whole family for every exact code with K > 1, every local dimension p ≥ 2, every t ≥ 1 and both distances 2t+1 and 2t+2. The engine for p ≥ 3 is the inequality K f(0) ≤ pn E f(X), X ~ Bin(n, 1 − p−2), for polynomials f of degree below d that are nonnegative on {0, …, n}. Computational certificates and code are on Zenodo.

7

Finite classification of transversal diagonal gates beyond stabilizer codes

X. He, C. Zhang, S. Huang, B. Zeng2610.29955—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here The model’s #2, but a finiteness theorem for non-stabilizer codes with one ((6,2,3)) worked example is for the code-theory specialists; Zeng’s name keeps it in the top ten.

Which diagonal logical gates can a non-stabilizer code implement transversally? He, Zhang, Huang and Zeng show that for exact qubit codes of distance at least 2, any projective logical action of a transversal diagonal gate is realized by a physical gate of finite order, with the order bounded by the code parameters alone. That makes the search finite: the subset-sum linear-programming framework of arXiv:2504.20847 enumerates every candidate discrete symmetry and the Knill–Laflamme equations fix the continuous parameters. For ((6,2,3)) codes the method finds 66 candidate symmetry classes, 15 of them realizable, with projective diagonal cyclic groups of order 2, 3 and 5; the order-5 code is unique up to local unitaries and permutations and is the shortest ((n,2,≥3)) code whose transversal group cannot be conjugated into the Clifford group.

8

Local Clifford equivalence to CSS codes: polynomial-time classification and conversion

M. Wang, S. Krastanov (UMass Amherst)2610.28737—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A clean algorithmic answer to a question code designers meet in practice, from the QuantumClifford.jl maintainer; useful tools draw steady scites without reaching the top.

Wang and Krastanov decide in O(mn2 + n4) binary operations whether a stabilizer code given by m possibly redundant generators on n qubits is locally Clifford-equivalent to a CSS code, and construct the single-qubit transformation and the CSS checks whenever it is. The reduction is to affine linear systems through local projectors that preserve the span of the binary stabilizer labels; code parameters and check sparsity survive, so CSS decoders and fault-tolerant gadgets can be reused on qLDPC codes that only looked non-CSS. For graph states the question becomes whether the local-complementation orbit contains a bipartite graph, with an adjacency-matrix criterion and a construction. They settle open LC-to-CSS questions for published code constructions.

Cooling more while dissipating less: anomalous tradeoffs from thermodynamic geometry

A. Canzio, V. Cavina, S. Raia, V. Vetrano, M. Scandi, P. Abiuso15final

Slow driving of an open quantum system is supposed to trade dissipation against speed and against how far the state moves. Canzio, Cavina, Raia, Vetrano, Scandi and Abiuso find cases where optimal driving reaches a colder or hotter target in the same time while dissipating less, or dissipates the same in less time, and name the effect NEAT, near-equilibrium anomalous tradeoff. They give necessary conditions, locate its origin in the thermodynamic metric, separate it from the Mpemba effect, show it in an exactly solvable qutrit and in d-level birth–death dynamics with independently controlled levels, and use it to improve a Carnot engine. Eight pages plus supplement; we did not list it, and it was second at 8 h: Abiuso’s thermalization speed limit was on Tuesday’s front page, and the thermodynamics crowd followed.

Yesterday’s #6 on SciRate

Quantum algorithm for high-dimensional nonlinear stochastic differential equations based on Carleman linearization

K. Miyamoto9final

Carleman linearization turns a nonlinear ODE into an infinite linear system truncated at order J; Miyamoto extends it to stochastic differential equations in N dimensions. Auxiliary processes approximate the tensor powers of the state, a linear system for their expectations is solved by a quantum ODE solver, and the target expectation is read out by overlap estimation. When the nonlinear and noise terms are small against the linear part, the error decays exponentially in J and the query count in the coefficient-matrix unitaries is polylogarithmic in N. A Langevin-type test confirms the decay. Forty-eight pages, one figure, one author; we skipped it, and it finished sixth. Quantum-ODE papers with explicit error bounds keep scoring like QEC.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
