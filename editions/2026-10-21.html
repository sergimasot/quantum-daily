Classical simulation · BosonSampling

Lossy boson sampling is classically simulable whenever a constant fraction of photons survives

Go and Jeong give a polynomial-time sampler for Fock-state BosonSampling at any fixed loss fraction, for most Haar interferometers in the dilute regime. The provable boundary moves from √N surviving photons to a linear fraction.

B. Go, H. Jeong (NextQuantum Center, Seoul National University)arXiv:2610.27065—scites

How much photon loss can a boson-sampling experiment absorb before a classical computer reproduces its statistics? For single-photon inputs the provable answer has stayed small. Oszmaniec and Brod and García-Patrón, Renema and Shchesnovich showed efficient simulation when only about √N of N photons survive, and the paper quotes the rigorous regime to date as n = O(√(εN)) surviving photons, or transmission η = O(√(ε/N)). Experiments lose a constant fraction, not everything but √N. Go and Jeong now give an algorithm running in time poly(M, N, 1/ε, 1/δ) for any fixed survival fraction n/N in (0, 1), which samples within total variation distance ε of the fixed-loss distribution with probability 1 − δ over Haar-random M-mode interferometers, and they extend it to uniform transmission loss at any fixed η.

The method is not a tensor network. Under fixed loss the output probability is an average of |Per|2 over which n of the N input columns survived, and the authors expand that average in a Hoeffding decomposition over input subsets, where the order-j term carries weight C(n, j)/C(N, j). For Gaussian matrix entries the terms are orthogonal, so truncating at degree d leaves a mean-square error at most (n/N)d+1/(1 − n/N), and a density-domination lemma transfers the bound to Haar submatrices when M ≥ C0n2N. Color coding replaces subset enumeration, bringing a marginal from NO(d) to 2O(d), and a signed sequential sampler draws outputs one mode at a time. The degree needed is d = O(1 + log(1/εδ)/log(N/n)), so the runtime is M·n·N3·ε−2·δ−1·2O(d).

The paper appeared overnight and we found no reaction yet, so the caveats come from the authors themselves. The guarantee holds for most interferometers, not all; the dilute condition M ≥ C0n2N asks for far more modes than the M ~ N2 of the standard hardness arguments; the main theorem targets the collision-collapsed distribution, and the full Fock output needs M = Ω(n2/εδ); and the input is single photons, where Jiuzhang and Borealis run Gaussian boson sampling with squeezed light. The authors say earlier attempts at the linear-survival regime either lacked guarantees (a Metropolis sampler) or ran in exponential time.

What to watch: whether the Hoeffding truncation extends to squeezed-state inputs, where the loss results of Oh, Liu, Alexeev, Fefferman and Jiang are numerical rather than provable; whether the mode condition can be brought down toward M ~ N2; and how the photonic groups answer, since a constant-fraction result removes the argument that the loss in current chips is too mild to matter. Today’s listing also has Facchi, Parisi and colleagues computing moments of random permanents and hafnians by replica methods (also), the anticoncentration input that hardness arguments need.

Industry · France

Alice & Bob reports PROQCIMA Phase 1: cat-qubit demonstrators run a distance-3 repetition code, still above threshold

The Quantum Insider

Alice & Bob says the first phase of PROQCIMA, the French defence-led programme that aims at two 1,000-logical-qubit prototypes by 2032, produced three demonstrators: two 6-qubit chips and one 12-qubit chip. On them it ran a distance-3 repetition code over several cycles, noise-biased CNOT gates between cat qubits that keep bit-flip protection, a measure-and-reset sequence for auxiliary qubits that it describes as broadly in line with programme targets, and a non-Clifford gate on a cat qubit. The logical error rate measured across the register stayed above threshold; no value is given, and the company’s digital twin of the chip points to qubit coherence across the device as the first thing to fix. Only the company has spoken so far: we found no release on its own site and no independent assessment. Five companies are in the programme (Alice & Bob, Pasqal, Quandela, Quobly, C12); three continue after year four and two beyond year eight.

Thermodynamics

A Planckian speed limit on thermalization, proved from Hamiltonian estimation

Physics World

Abiuso, Rolandi, Calsamiglia, Sekatski and Perarnau-Llobet (IQOQI Vienna, TU Wien, UAB Barcelona, Geneva) prove in Nature Physics (arXiv:2506.19188) that any process which brings a nontrivial class of Hamiltonians close to their thermal states takes at least ħ/(2kBT), half the Planckian time, and at least ħ/Δ at low temperature, where Δ is the gap. The move is to define a thermalization machine as one that also works for neighbouring Hamiltonians, which rules out shortcuts tuned to one system, and then to read the thermal state as an estimate of the Hamiltonian, so that the Heisenberg limit on estimation becomes a bound on time. Physics World (15 Oct) and phys.org (18 Oct) covered it; neither carries an independent quote, and the paper’s own example is a model of interacting particles rather than an experiment.

Complete modified logarithmic Sobolev inequalities for one-dimensional quantum spin chains

L. Gao, J. Guo, S. Wang (Wuhan University)2610.28600—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Gibbs-sampler mixing is the theory topic of the year, and a system-size-independent CMLSI for the Chen–Kastoryano–Gilyén sampler at every temperature in 1D is the kind of bound the algorithms crowd scites on sight.

Chen, Kastoryano and Gilyén’s Lindbladian Gibbs samplers are the leading candidates for preparing thermal states on a quantum computer, and the open question is how fast they mix. For one-dimensional chains with bounded finite-range interactions, noncommuting and nonuniform allowed, the authors prove a complete modified log-Sobolev inequality at every fixed β > 0: for the Gaussian or Metropolis version of the sampler, any finite reference system R and any faithful joint state ρ, 2α D(ρ‖ρR ⊗ σ) ≤ IL(ρ), with α independent of N and of the reference dimension. Relative entropy to the Gibbs state then decays as e−2αt, giving trace and diamond mixing times O(log N + log 1/ε), which they call optimal for the class. The previous bound in this setting, from Bergamaschi and Chen’s spectral gap, was O(N + log 1/ε); logarithmic mixing was known for commuting 1D Davies dynamics and for the Gaussian CKG sampler at high temperature.

The proof has two halves. A complete spatial factorization bounds the global relative entropy by a constant times the sum of single-site conditional relative entropies with no additive error, using 1D Gibbs locality, stability of conditional relative entropy under local filtering and an interval recursion; then each single-site term is bounded by its entropy production plus regional errors with summable coefficients, which are absorbed. The static estimate also yields a size-independent CMLSI for single-site Petz heat-bath dynamics. Fifty-seven pages and no numerics; two dimensions is the obvious next target.

Parallel omnidirectional atom rearrangement with dynamic holograms

S. A. Engel, D. Gong, Z. Lin, L. Christakis, K.-K. Ni, N. Y. Yao (Harvard)2610.27053—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A 2,078-site ytterbium array at 99% filling from the Ni and Yao labs is the largest tweezer-assembly number in months, but it is a physics.atom-ph cross-list, and cross-lists collect fewer quant-ph scites.

Rearranging a tweezer array with a spatial light modulator is slow because the liquid crystal has to settle between holograms. Engel, Gong, Lin, Christakis, Ni and Yao drive a kHz-rate SLM (minimum frame period about 0.7 ms, settle time 1.6 ms) with new frames before the crystal has settled, and show by measuring survival and heating that ytterbium atoms stay trapped: radial speed rises threefold to 0.75 µm/ms as the frame period drops from 2.8 to 0.7 ms, axial speed to 3.86 µm/ms, and 0.2 µm radial steps show no loss over about 2,000 steps. Holograms are computed in real time by a warm-started weighted Gerchberg–Saxton algorithm that converges in about three iterations at 2048×2048 in 0.65 ms, matching their trained SLMnet network without any training.

With that they rearrange 2,071 atoms into a 2,078-site kagome lattice, 99.0% mean filling over 195 shots, in a median 56 ms, and move atoms between the layers of a 536-site bilayer (96.7% filling). One rearrangement round only; fidelity is limited by imaging loss, array size by laser power, and the modified 532 nm liquid crystal sends about 7% of the light into other diffraction orders. The authors say the method ports to any SLM-based tweezer platform.

NeST: ultra-high-rate qLDPC codes with sub-50 µs stabiliser cycles via spin shuttling

A. Siegel (Quantum Motion)2610.28538—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A qLDPC architecture with circuit-level thresholds and a 10−14 extrapolation fits the fault-tolerance-architecture pattern that out-scites everything but headline experiments, though a single author at a startup and a 30-page read will slow it.

High-rate qLDPC codes need long-range, block-wise qubit transport, which tweezers supply in milliseconds. Siegel asks which group actions electron shuttling on fabricated tracks can perform without collisions, and builds codes around those groups. A looped conveyor belt implements a cyclic group as a rigid global motion; nesting loops gives semidirect products, with a reflection obtained by entering the destination loop backwards, a family G(n, σ) that covers all finite abelian groups and some non-Abelian ones. The codes are “mitten” codes, lifted products with 1×2 base matrices of weight-3 entries, rate 1/5 and check weight 9, searched for base groups that fit; the largest found is [[1400, 280, ≤28]].

Data qubits stay still and only ancillas move, at 50 m/s on a 1 µm grid, with shuttling dephasing psh per µm on top of uniform depolarizing noise at p and quasi-static dephasing with T2* = 20 µs on idling ancillas. Circuit-level thresholds exceed 10−3; syndrome cycles take 16–30 µs for the smaller codes and an estimated 42 µs for the largest, and the mitten codes match or beat same-distance surface codes up to psh = p/20. A [[150, 30, 10]] surgery gadget keeps rigid belt motions at no qubit cost and 29% more layers.

The 10−14 figure is a straight-line extrapolation the author calls rough: no circuit distance was computed for the 1,400-qubit code, noise grows with code size, and inter-cell scheduling is left open.

Local-channel stability of classical Z2 lattice gauge models

G. Xun, W. Cao, H. Watanabe (Tokyo)2610.27939—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Local-channel stability of classical gauge models is the thread Monday’s stable-spin-model paper (19 scites) pulled on, and Watanabe’s name carries in the many-body crowd.

Which classical gauge models keep their low-temperature phase under local perturbations, when the comparison is made with local quantum channels rather than with a symmetry? Xun, Cao and Watanabe take Z2 models with spins on m-cells of a d-dimensional lattice and interactions on (m+1)-cell boundaries, on the full Hilbert space with no Gauss law, so the family includes the Ising model (m = 0) and the magnetic sector of the toric code (m = 1). For 1 ≤ m ≤ d − 2, sufficiently weak local classical perturbations at low enough positive temperature leave the state two-way equivalent to the canonical flat ensemble under local channels with product ancillas, polylogarithmic causal radius and any inverse-power trace-norm error. No symmetry of the perturbation is needed; the ingredients are locally indistinguishable flat sectors and extended magnetic defects of dimension d − m − 1.

Weak noncommuting perturbations get the same conclusion in a smaller window, by reducing the diagonal to a classical interaction and restoring the coherences with a local channel. High temperature is two-way equivalent to a product state, and an optimized covariance between distant parallel m-tori, nonincreasing under one-way conversion, takes the values 1 and 0 in the two proven regimes. The authors say the sufficient domains do not fix the full phase diagram; it reads as the classical companion to Monday’s stable spin model from Balasubramanian, Davydova, Lin and Thorngren.

Trapped-ion-based quantum repeater node across a 17 km metropolitan fiber

K. Fabitha, …, T. E. Northup, V. Krutyanskiy, B. P. Lanyon (Innsbruck)2610.27854—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Networking experiments draw steady but modest scites; a city-fibre repeater node from Lanyon’s group with an 81.6% Bell fidelity should sit near ten.

Two ions in an optical cavity act as a repeater node whose photons travel a 17 km deployed fibre across Innsbruck. The paper first characterizes the channel and the ion–photon entanglement distributed across it, then runs the node protocol, which leaves a photon at the node entangled with one that has crossed the fibre, at a Bell-state fidelity of 81.6 (+1.9/−1.4)%. It is one node rather than a concatenation, and rates are not in the abstract, but it is the first trapped-ion repeater step on city fibre, moving the earlier lab demonstrations out of the building.

Floquet ground state order

S. Mondal, P. M. Schindler, M. Bukov (MPI-PKS Dresden)2610.28336—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Bukov’s geometric Floquet theory has a following and “ground-state time crystals” is a phrase that travels, but 42 pages of dynamics theory is a slow burn.

Driven interacting systems are supposed to heat to infinite temperature. Mondal, Schindler and Bukov split Floquet evolution into geometric and average-energy parts and define the Floquet ground state as the ground state of the average-energy operator, with a variational principle and an MPS algorithm to find it. In driven nonintegrable chains of more than 100 spins the state is gapped, with area-law entanglement, over extended parameter regions, hosting Floquet antiferromagnets and ground-state time crystals, with transitions where the average-energy gap closes. Stability against heating is attributed to quasilocal counterdiabatic driving. The claim that these states evade ETH will get the sharpest reading.

Certified non-degenerate maximum-likelihood benchmarking of quantum LDPC decoders

M. Banfi, M. Ferrari, A. Favano, A. Tarable, L. Barletta (Milan)2610.28008—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Two decoder papers took third and fourth place yesterday; a shot-level maximum-likelihood certificate is the kind of benchmarking tool decoder people adopt, though it is a cs.IT cross-list.

A decoder’s logical error rate only means something against the best possible decoder. Banfi, Ferrari, Favano, Tarable and Barletta certify, per sampled shot under circuit-level noise, whether non-degenerate maximum-likelihood decoding would succeed: upper and lower bounds on the minimum correction cost in the true and competing logical classes label each shot success, failure or undecided, with branch-and-bound tightening the bounds under a resource limit. Counting gives an interval on the ML error rate whose width is the undecided fraction. On two bivariate bicycle codes, best-first OSD tracks the benchmark closely but leaves some ML-correctable shots on the table. Degenerate ML is not addressed.

Yesterday’s #2 on SciRate

Spatial discretization error for quantum chemistry simulation

D. Fang, J. Zhang22final

Quantum chemistry algorithms are costed in a finite basis, but the continuum Schrödinger equation is the target. Fang and Zhang give a Sobolev–Galerkin framework for the spatial discretization error of unbounded many-body Coulomb Hamiltonians, with periodization and Galerkin bounds for both dynamics and ground-state energies. The rates differ: the ground-state energy converges as M−1 in the number of one-particle basis functions, sharply, while no uniform M−1 rate exists for the real-time wavefunction with general H2 data, because a dynamical projection error enters at its own rate. Combined with current algorithms, they claim the first end-to-end complexity bounds for molecular dynamics with every spatial error controlled. We gave it a trending link; 22 scites says the algorithms community wants rigor at the basis-set step.

High-accuracy propagation-assisted local decoding for fast transversal Clifford circuits

M. Webber, S. Sadana, M. A. Lane, L. J. Robson, A. O’Rourke18final

An iterative redecode scheme for fast transversal Clifford circuits: local MWPM on memory-equivalent detector error models, alternated with Pauli-frame propagation and detector updates across transversal CNOT layers, so that local decoders agree with the global detector record without ever building a global hypergraph. At d = 11 on deep random Clifford circuits it fails about 4× less often than the ghost protocol and 15× less than logical-observable matching; three-block GHZ preparation gains 2× (25× at d = 19), repeated logical S gates 4–10×. Error rates saturate after a few iterations, buffers of ⌊d/4⌋ suffice, and circuits and data are on Zenodo. One line in “also” was too little for a decoder paper with public data; it finished third.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
