AI and proofs · Quantum complexity

OpenAI’s unverified unitary-synthesis algorithm is already a building block on quant-ph

A 102-page paper uses the claimed algorithm for one half of unitaryQIP = unitaryPSPACE, and a UT Austin note cites it in support of an NP-oracle conjecture. Nobody has published an independent check, and mathematicians spent the weekend on Terence Tao’s blog arguing over how to handle AI proofs.

E. He, F. Ou, M. Zhao; A. Sen (UT Austin); F. Calegari; S. AaronsonarXiv · Terence Tao’s blog

OpenAI’s claimed solution of the unitary synthesis problem, posed by Aaronson and Kuperberg in 2007, was part of the company’s 5 October batch as “Polynomial-Time Unitary Synthesis from a Boolean Oracle”: every n-qubit unitary would be implementable in polynomial time with queries to a suitable Boolean function. Today’s listing has the first quant-ph papers to use it. Entong He, Fengning Ou and Mingnan Zhao prove unitaryQIP = unitaryPSPACE, the unitary analogue of QIP = PSPACE. One inclusion comes from a rigidity lemma applied to Metger and Yuen’s polynomial-space prover; the other uses OpenAI’s algorithm to synthesize unitaryPSPACE families in polynomial time with a PSPACE oracle. Aniruddha Sen (UT Austin) prepares arbitrary states with an NP oracle instead of PP and cites the OpenAI claim only as support for his conjecture that an NP oracle also suffices for unitaries; his own result does not depend on it.

Aaronson and Kuperberg had conjectured a negative answer. Days after the release, Dakshita Khurana wrote that she had long expected a positive one, recognized ingredients she and others had tried, such as recursive constructions and near-isometries with sparse rows, and was still reading the manuscript. John Bostanci gave an informal Cambridge seminar on his “current understanding” of the algorithm on 13 October. Huang, Ramkumar and Wright separately showed a random-oracle version impossible, which leaves the general question untouched. We found no published independent verification of OpenAI’s proof, so the second inclusion in the new paper is, for now, conditional on it.

The weekend’s discussion was about AI-produced mathematics in general. On Terence Tao’s blog, Frank Calegari argued that mathematicians should adapt rather than despair. He called OpenAI’s papers “atrociously written”, said some are wrong, and proposed recording AI results, Lean-checked where possible, as shared knowledge; the 47 comments mix support with worries about careers and the influence of AI companies. A second guest post, on “egoless” mathematics by Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai and Jie Xue, drew complaints that it was vague. On Shtetl-Optimized, Aaronson told a commenter that preparing a fixed state was already known to reduce to a Boolean function; the new claim is about applying unitaries, and its consequences for counterfeiting quantum money rest on unlikely class collapses.

Things to watch: whether quantum complexity theorists post a careful reading of the unitary-synthesis manuscript, as Dana Moshkovitz’s group has done for Unique Games ahead of her UT Austin talk tomorrow; whether the He–Ou–Zhao inclusion can be proved without the OpenAI step; and their constant-round version of the protocol, which they say yields a barrier to proving that EFI pairs exist. The UK Academy for the Mathematical Sciences’ report on AI in mathematics, out on 31 October, lists independent verification among its priorities.

Policy · United States

Quantum Industry Coalition asks Congress to pass the NQI reauthorization in this year’s defence bill

The Quantum Insider

In a 30 October letter to the leaders of the Senate Commerce and Energy committees and the House Science Committee, the Quantum Industry Coalition, whose members include AWS, IBM, Microsoft, Quantinuum, IonQ, Xanadu, Atom Computing and D-Wave, asked for the National Quantum Initiative Reauthorization Act to be enacted this year through the 2027 National Defense Authorization Act once the House and Senate versions are reconciled. The letter puts testbeds and quantum-centre operations first among funding priorities, asks for authorization levels above the proposed FY2027 budget, and wants research-security rules and definitions aligned with the CHIPS and Science Act in technology-neutral terms that cover hybrid quantum-classical systems. It gives no dollar figures. The original authorization lapsed in 2023, and the Senate bill introduced in January would extend the initiative to December 2034. We found no general-press coverage of the letter; the coalition is an interested party.

Early Fault-Tolerant Magic State Synthesis and Spacetime Efficient Analog Rotations with Color Code on a Superconducting Quantum Processor

Garner, Hoyt, Kan, Stein, Liu (PNNL), Li (U. Washington)2611.00404—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Magic-state cultivation and a small color-code factory run on public IBM hardware, the kind of QEC-on-a-known-chip result that has finished near the top in our scored listings.

Color codes have transversal Cliffords and now competitive decoders, so for hardware with local connectivity the open question is how to make magic states cheaply. The PNNL–Washington team runs both main recipes on IBM’s Nighthawk R2 device ibm_phoenix with distance-3 [[7,1,3]] patches and superdense syndrome extraction: a STAR-style analog-rotation injection for early fault tolerance, and a magic-state-synthesis factory built from cultivated T states. They describe these as the first superconducting implementations of either.

The STAR injection uses a native RZZ(θ) gate inside the color code with flag qubits. Its first-order logical error is p/15, against 2p/3 for the surface-code version, about an order of magnitude lower in simulation. On hardware the flagged partial-FT injection has 4.98% mean infidelity at 62% acceptance, and logical analog-rotation states reach about 96% tomographic fidelity. Cultivation follows Gidney et al. with a slimmer seed network (22 entanglers, 4 helpers). Of 500,000 attempts 71,060 were kept (14.2%), and kickback tomography, run at distance 2, gives a correct-sign probability of 99.9859%.

The factory joins two factory patches and a compute patch by lattice surgery and synthesizes TLHLTLHLTL|+⟩L from three cultivated T states and transversal Hadamards, with real-time feed-forward SL corrections. Final fidelity is 91.3% with post-processed corrections (4.3% acceptance) and 91.0% in real time (0.9%). One-round teleportation has 26.1% process infidelity without decoding, 19.2% with concatenated matching and 14.2% with Tesseract.

Everything rests on heavy post-selection, decoding is offline, the noise model leaves out leakage and coherent errors, readout dominates the error budget, and three surgery rounds did worse than one. The paper runs the whole color-code pipeline end to end on a cloud device, at fidelities still far below what a factory needs.

unitaryQIP = unitaryPSPACE

He, Ou, Zhao2611.02819—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A clean complexity equality with a plain-language title, a type we have under-ranked seven times in algorithms and complexity; 38 places above the prior because its link to the OpenAI claim will draw readers.

Rosenthal and Yuen defined unitaryQIP and unitaryPSPACE for unitary families implementable by interactive proofs and in polynomial space, and the state versions were shown equal by Rosenthal–Yuen and Metger–Yuen. He, Ou and Zhao settle the unitary case. For unitaryQIP ⊆ unitaryPSPACE they derive an acceptance-rigidity lemma from the soundness condition, which shows that Metger and Yuen’s polynomial-space prover for a fixed input in fact implements the target unitary on every input, so the protocol can be simulated in polynomial space.

For the reverse inclusion they use OpenAI’s claimed solution of the Aaronson–Kuperberg unitary synthesis problem to synthesize any unitaryPSPACE family in polynomial time with a PSPACE oracle, then answer the oracle queries with auxiliary states from the statePSPACE ⊆ stateQIP protocol. With polynomially many copies of the input the protocol parallelizes to constant rounds, and they extract a complexity-theoretic barrier to proving that EFI pairs exist. The paper runs to 102 pages, and the second inclusion is only as solid as the OpenAI manuscript, which nobody has independently checked in public (see the front page).

Boson sampling is fragile to loss

Y. Lim (Chungbuk National U.)2611.01434—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A short single-author result that makes any fixed loss fatal to boson-sampling hardness, Gaussian inputs included; 85 places above the prior because the 21 October lossy boson-sampling paper finished third.

Hardness arguments for lossy boson sampling have needed the transmission η to fall with system size, and earlier efficient samplers covered either vanishing η or ran in quasi-polynomial time. Lim shows that any fixed uniform loss is enough. For Fock-state inputs (n single photons in distinct modes plus any number of vacuum modes, every M ≥ n) and for Gaussian boson sampling with equal bounded squeezing in every mode, there are classical samplers polynomial in M, n and 1/ε, with total-variation error averaged over Haar-random interferometers, even when the mean detected photon number grows linearly.

Loss suppresses high-order photon correlations, so keeping correlation orders up to L = O(1 + log(1/ε)) leaves an exponentially small truncation error, and rejection sampling turns the truncated marginals into samples. The polynomial’s degree depends on η and diverges as η → 1, so the paper does not show that a given experiment can be spoofed in practice, and it covers only uniform loss and Haar averages. Hardness would need loss to vanish faster than roughly (log n)/n. The author says the work is independent of a related preprint that needed a dilute-mode condition.

Quantum algorithm for weakly correlated molecular electronic ground states with sub-quintic scaling in the system size

Leimkuhler, Kronenberger, …, Solomon, Christandl, Reiher2611.00751—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here End-to-end chemistry cost bounds with explicit error control, which have scored like QEC papers for us (Fang–Zhang, 22), from the Christandl and Reiher groups.

Most molecules are weakly correlated and are handled classically by CCSD(T), which costs O(n7) in the number of orbitals and gives no guarantee of chemical accuracy. The authors give a quantum algorithm for these ground-state energies with end-to-end cost Õ(n4.8/ε2) as a low-depth, shot-noise-limited method in the high-order Trotter limit, or Õ(n4.8 + n3.8/ε) when the simulation time scales as 1/ε. It combines adiabatic preparation along a smoothed Gevrey schedule with eigenstate filtering by quantum prolate diagonalization (QPD).

Both steps assume a spectral gap that does not shrink with system size. The authors support this numerically on alanine chains of up to 64 monomers, but it remains an assumption. On 28 molecules QPD gives state-of-the-art cost trade-offs against earlier phase-estimation methods, and they estimate physical resources for Trotter-step circuits of up to 40 spin-orbitals. The comparison is with CCSD(T)’s formal scaling, which comes with no accuracy guarantee; the quantum method’s guarantee holds only under the gap assumption.

5

Full-Rank Constructions of Explicit Lossless Expanders and Good Small-Set qLTCs

Aharon-Holzman, Ilan, Reiner, Ta-Shma2611.02963—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Supplies the code family a self-correcting-memory proposal was waiting for; qLDPC constructions have done well with us, though a 32-page combinatorics paper may climb slowly.

Hong, Guo and Lucas showed that good qLDPC codes with full-rank check matrices and small-set soundness (confinement) would give quantum memories with long thermal lifetimes, but no explicit family had all of these properties. The authors build one. They modify the lossless expanders of Hsieh, Lubotzky, Mohanty, Reiner and Zhang so that the graphs carry a free action of a finite 2-group. Under that action, injectivity and surjectivity of the adjacency matrix can be checked on a fixed-size matrix indexed by vertex orbits, and a sparse factor added at one type of middle vertex forces full rank at a controlled loss of expansion.

The 2-groups come from a new observation: reducing the Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak generators modulo powers of 2 instead of an odd prime gives Ramanujan Cayley graphs on 2-groups. Two such expanders in the Lin–Hsieh balanced product give asymptotically good codes whose X and Z checks have full row rank and confinement on a linear range. Under Hong–Guo–Lucas’s dynamical assumptions this means a free-energy density analytic at every positive temperature and a guaranteed lifetime of exp(Ω(N)) at low enough fixed temperature, up from stretched-exponential.

Correlated Catalysis Removes the Strong Converse for Entanglement Distillation

Masajada, Philip, Streltsov2611.03148—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A crisp information-theory theorem the prior ranks second, but resource-theory papers have rarely reached our top five.

Above the distillable entanglement ED, distillation fidelity goes to zero for many states, a strong converse. Masajada, Philip and Streltsov show that correlated catalysis removes the strong converse for every distillable bipartite state: at any rate R > ED the optimal asymptotic fidelity is exactly ED/R. The catalyst is returned exactly at every blocklength and its correlations with the output vanish asymptotically, so the reliable rate is unchanged and only the fidelity above it improves. They also bound the catalyst size needed.

7

Quantum channel tomography: optimal bounds in the near-boundary regime

K. Chen, A. Oufkir2611.03639—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here It settles the query complexity of channel tomography; 17 places above the prior because tight learning bounds have done well with us, though Monday’s Oufkir–Berta paper had only 4.

For channels with input dimension d1, output dimension d2 and Kraus rank r, the dilation rate τ = rd2/d1 governs a Heisenberg-to-classical transition, and only the near-boundary regime 1 < τ < 1 + o(1) was open. Chen and Oufkir prove Ω(rd1d2/ε2) for diamond-norm error, matching the known upper bounds, and Θ(d12/ε + min{(τ−1)d12, (τ−1)2d12d22}/ε2) for Choi trace-norm error. Heisenberg scaling disappears abruptly in diamond norm and fades gradually in Choi norm.

8

Belief Propagation-Informed Tesseract Decoding of Quantum LDPC Codes

Javed, Garcia-Herrero, Vasić, Flanagan2611.03128—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Decoders have been our most dependable subfield, but a speed-up with no new accuracy record keeps this at Low-Med.

Tesseract finds a minimum-weight error set by A* search, and its effort grows quickly with error rate and code size. The authors run damped sum-product BP first. If BP’s hard decision reproduces the syndrome it is accepted; otherwise BP marginals, scaled by a trust coefficient, reorder the search while path costs keep the channel priors. An optional BP initialization starts a bounded residual search that can undo wrong BP choices. On the [[72,12,6]], [[90,8,10]] and [[144,12,12]] bivariate bicycle codes under circuit-level noise, expanded nodes fall by more than two orders of magnitude at low error rates, with logical error rates close to Tesseract’s.

Witnessing and quantifying stellar rank with the Wigner function

O. Hahn, A. Maltesson, V. Upreti, M. Ellerbroek, A. Ferraro, S. Rahimi-Keshari, U. Chabaud, G. Ferrini18final

Stellar rank counts how many photon additions a bosonic state needs beyond Gaussian operations, a measure of its non-Gaussian resource. Hahn and colleagues estimate fidelities with target pure states from displaced-parity measurements, which certifies stellar rank, and add witnesses that need the Wigner function at only a few points. Applied to existing superconducting-cavity data for Fock, binomial, cat and GKP states, the method certifies stellar rank 8 for a GKP state, which they call a record across platforms. We listed it only under “also”; it finished third with 18, ahead of every pick but two.

Engineered Learning Problems on Noisy Quantum Processors

E. Payares, N. Milazzo, G. Ben Slama, T. Cope10final

Payares, Milazzo, Ben Slama and Cope design an artificial classification task for a projected quantum kernel and run it on hardware at 15, 30 and 45 qubits, reproducing the results on several processors. They report a significant gap over the classical methods they tested at each size, and find that error mitigation widens it in two ways: it improves the hardware output and makes the problem harder for classical methods. How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks, showing each event's next deadline: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
