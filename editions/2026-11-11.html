Policy · European Union

A leaked draft of the EU Quantum Act would reserve public quantum contracts for EU-controlled suppliers and aim for 100 logical qubits per system around 2030

The Commission is due to propose the Act on 18 November. The draft would also make critical-sector purchases post-quantum-ready, and it carries no budget of its own.

European Commission (draft, as reported by The Quantum Insider and MLex)The Quantum Insider · MLex

The Quantum Insider has obtained a draft of the EU Quantum Act. Under it, designated strategic quantum projects and public purchases of quantum products and services would generally be limited to EU-controlled companies. Firms from partner countries could take part where a strategic partnership gives reciprocal access, or with Commission authorisation. Six months after the Act takes effect, equipment bought for critical sectors would have to be ready for post-quantum cryptography and able to switch algorithms, and the draft says cost or supplier preference alone would not justify an exception. It sets computing targets of about 100 error-corrected logical qubits per system by 2030 or soon after and thousands per platform by 2035, and asks that quantum machine learning be compared reproducibly with leading classical AI. For networks it targets 50 km metropolitan pilots by 2030, 200 km links by 2035 and an operational EuroQCI from 2031.

MLex reported in October that the Commission plans to propose the Act on 18 November and that the text could still change; the copy The Quantum Insider saw still has unfinished references. It sets no overall budget. Money would come from the successor to Horizon Europe, the proposed European Competitiveness Fund and national and private sources, and strategic designation would not guarantee funding. That fits what DG CNECT’s Thomas Skordas told a Parliament event in September, as reported by The Next Web: “The act is not about budget.” He also said the Commission expects some consolidation among Europe’s quantum startups.

We found no public reaction yet to the leaked text from companies, researchers or member states. At the September event, Tommaso Calarco of the EU’s quantum advisory board described the Act as Europe’s last opportunity, and QuIC’s Cecile Perrault said 80% of the components in quantum computers found in Europe are made in Europe. Neither report says how the ownership test would treat groups in countries associated with Horizon Europe, such as Switzerland and the UK. That question has come up before: a 2024 draft Horizon work programme proposed keeping associated countries out of some quantum calls.

Things to watch on 18 November: whether “EU-controlled” survives or becomes “EU-headquartered”, as one trade outlet put it; which partnerships count as reciprocal; how the logical-qubit target will be benchmarked; and whether any money is attached before the next multiannual budget.

Policy · United States

A bipartisan US commission says research strength alone won’t win quantum and asks for an Exascale-style co-design programme

SCSP · The Quantum Insider

The Commission on U.S. Quantum Primacy, convened by the Special Competitive Studies Project and co-chaired by Senators Todd Young and Ben Ray Luján with SCSP’s Ylli Bajraktari, released its preliminary report on 10 November. It argues that US research leadership will not by itself carry over into deployment and manufacturing, and it points to China’s public investment and supply chains. Its recommendations, summarised by The Quantum Insider, include steady funding for basic research with dedicated tracks for new algorithms, a national hardware–software co-design programme that could follow the Exascale Computing Project, a NIST-led benchmarking and interoperability effort, a National Quantum Foundry Access Program and a faster move to post-quantum cryptography in state and local government. The report gives no dollar figures. A final report with an implementation plan is due in spring 2027.

Hardware · Japan

Quantinuum and RIKEN sign up for a fault-tolerant machine “made with Japan”, and SoftBank will study linking quantum hardware to AI data centres

Quantum Computing Report

RIKEN has commissioned the upgrade of its on-site Reimei machine to a 56-qubit Quantinuum H2, and the two have expanded their memorandum to build a fault-tolerant computer with Japanese universities and component makers, combining Quantinuum’s QCCD trapped-ion architecture with Japanese cryogenics and precision manufacturing. A separate agreement with SoftBank covers connecting quantum hardware to commercial AI data centres, including optical interconnects. The partners describe two validated workflows that use Reimei, the ROQUO GPU system and Fugaku together: spin-lattice data from a 6 × 6 lattice used to train a generative model of phase diagrams, and an excited-state spectrum of zinc porphyrin. These are company descriptions. No timeline, qubit target or budget for the fault-tolerant machine has been published, and QCR adds no independent assessment.

Research · EPFL

EPFL converts 263 GHz millimetre waves to telecom light with 67% internal efficiency

arXiv · Quantum Computing Report

EPFL’s Hybrid Photonics Laboratory couples a thin-film lithium niobate photonic molecule to a sub-millimetre gold resonator and converts photons between 263 GHz and 1561 nm (arXiv:2611.00279, QCR). The vacuum electro-optic coupling is about 60 kHz, roughly sixty times typical microwave-domain values, which gives a cooperativity up to 0.27 and 67% internal conversion efficiency, with a resolved thermal occupation of 0.21 photons. Off-chip efficiency is only 1.6 × 10−4, limited by free-space coupling into the antenna. Millimetre-wave qubits can run warmer than microwave ones, so an efficient optical link would ease cooling for networked processors; the authors expect superconducting NbTiN resonators to help at liquid-helium temperatures.

PriorTilt: Decoding quantum LDPC codes with sparse perturbations of belief propagation priors

M. Ye, N. Delfosse (IonQ)2611.1337315Wed 10:17●●● High confidence

Why here A short decoder paper from a known QEC name, with public code, that beats Relay-BP on the gross code. Decoder papers with public code have repeatedly reached the top five this autumn.

Belief propagation is the cheapest decoder for quantum LDPC codes, but degeneracy and short cycles stop it from converging. Recent fixes either restart BP with modified memory (Relay-BP) or run many copies (beam search). Min Ye and Nicolas Delfosse keep BP’s message-passing rules and change only a small number of priors. After 60 ordinary iterations PriorTilt runs rounds of six. At each round boundary, error nodes whose summed posterior log-likelihood over the last round falls below a threshold become eligible, and each eligible node gets, at random, a nonnegative or nonpositive offset scaled from the extreme clipped values of its posteriors and outgoing messages. Offsets stay fixed within a round and messages are never reset. Fewer than 0.5% of priors are touched per round.

The benchmarks are circuit-level memory experiments over d rounds on BB144 ([[144,12,12]], the gross code, with Bravyi et al.’s uniform noise) and on BB70 ([[70,6,9]]) and GB102 ([[102,22,9]]) with Tripier et al.’s moving-qubit noise. Against Relay-BP the logical error rate falls 1.7–4.3× on BB144, 1.6–3.5× on BB70 and 1.2–1.6× on GB102, and against beam search 1.4–3.1×. At p = 10−3 on BB144 it reaches 2.1 × 10−8, against 1.3 × 10−5 for min-sum BP with the same 18,000-iteration budget. On one laptop core the mean decoding time is 0.91 ms, against 1.04 ms for Relay-BP and 2.38 ms for beam search, though the 99th percentile on BB144 is worse than Relay-BP’s (4.5 against 3.3 ms). For XYZ decoding at p = 0.003 it matches Relay-BP-5 and is 1.8× faster.

The code is public. The schedule has 14 parameters, tuned by coordinate descent for each code and error rate, so it remains open how well one setting transfers across noise levels and devices. Rounds depend on each other, which limits parallelism compared with BP-SF. There is no FPGA or ASIC implementation yet, though the authors argue that staying close to plain BP should make one easier.

FusionQ: A Generic and Optimal Decoding Framework for Ultra-Low-Latency Quantum Error Correction

J. Zhou, S. Garner, A. Sundaram, G. Li, Y. Wu (Microsoft Quantum, Penn, UW)2611.1311112Wed 10:17●●● High confidence

Why here A Microsoft decoding framework that works directly on detector error models and comes with an exactness theorem; tools that touch DEMs were among last week’s highest scorers.

FusionQ does not hand a whole space-time DEM, or a fixed window, to one decoder. It groups fired detectors into clusters, builds an open-boundary local DEM around each, and grows a footprint by one hop only when its local solution uses a virtual boundary error. Overlapping footprints are merged and solved again. The theorem: with an exact minimum-weight local backend and results published only when they are free of boundary errors, the fused local solutions give a global most-likely error. The scheduler needs only a local solution and a flag from the backend, so MWPM, Tesseract and Relay-BP all run under it.

Under SI1000 noise at p = 5 × 10−4, post-acquisition latency for a d = 11 surface code over 33 rounds falls from 56.7 ms to 0.12 ms with MWPM. On BB144 with Tesseract it falls from 3.3 to 0.7 ms, and for surgery across six BB blocks it stays under 5.9 ms while the monolithic decoder needs 196 ms. The latencies come from trace-driven replay on one core per job under an idealised parallel model. A monolithic fallback finishes first on 2.3% of shots, and the scaling runs use only 2,000 shots.

Surface codes with coherent errors: optimal decoding and finite-size crossovers

X.-D. Dai, Z.-Q. Wan, X. Dai, G.-Y. Zhu2611.130984Wed 10:17●●○ Medium confidence

Why here It settles a long-running puzzle about coherent-error thresholds and proves Pauli decoding optimal for even d; theory with proofs has done well in QEC, but at 47 pages it may start slowly.

Under uniform coherent rotations exp(iθZ) with perfect syndromes, numerics for the surface code have drifted with system size, which made the threshold hard to read off. Dai, Wan, Dai and Zhu find two length scales: a ballistic crossover dba ~ 1/βRα (α = 1 for odd d, 2 for even d) and a far larger renormalisation scale dRG ~ exp(C/βR4), with βR = −½ ln tan θ. Near the SWAP point θ = π/4 the two separate, leaving a wide window of drift and false plateaus. A quartic interaction in the replicated Majorana theory leads to the class-D sigma model, and free-fermion simulations collapse in X4 = βR4 ln d. Everything is consistent with a Pauli-decoding threshold at θc = π/4.

For even d they prove that maximum-likelihood Pauli decoding maximises entanglement fidelity over all deterministic syndrome-conditioned recoveries, so its threshold equals the coherent-information limit and treating these errors as Pauli costs nothing. The setting has no measurement errors and uses uniform angles only.

Machine-learning decoders for fusion-based quantum computing in real time

D. Barter, …, M. Gimeno-Segovia, …, S. Roberts (15 authors)2611.1363023Wed 10:17●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A real-time decoder with FPGA latency numbers from a team that includes PsiQuantum’s Mercedes Gimeno-Segovia and Sam Roberts; it sits well above its prior because decoders have been our most reliable scorers.

In fusion-based architectures, photon loss and failed fusions produce erasures whose locations are known. PRISM is a transformer decoder that takes those erasure locations as input alongside the syndrome. Across surface, colour and qLDPC protocols up to distance 24, with mixed Pauli and erasure noise, it matches the logical error rates of integer-programming decoders and runs much faster, including at error rates up to half the threshold.

The team then optimised the model and implemented it on an AMD VP1902 FPGA. For distance-8 blocks in space and time it reaches 6.59 μs latency and 1.56 μs amortised per logical block. The hardware result covers only distance 8, and the abstract names integer programming as the only baseline. The paper is 43 pages including the supplement.

5

Parametric Proof-Carrying Compilation for Quantum Error-Correction

J. Z. Ye, J. Palsberg (UCLA)2611.12985—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Lean-checked distance guarantees for whole code families; formal QEC tools such as QECert drew 30 scites last week, which is why it ranks well above its prior despite being a cs.PL cross-list.

PC-QEC compiles syndrome-extraction circuits together with a proof that they keep the code distance at circuit level. The fault model allows single-wire Pauli faults on every operand, which covers data, readout and ancilla faults that spread into hook errors. For each code family and each extraction scheme (Standard, Flag, Shor and Knill), code-algebra proofs and fault-aware Hoare proofs combine into a finite symbolic certificate whose size does not depend on the distance.

One certificate proves distance d for every odd d ≥ 3 of the rotated surface code with standard extraction, and it checks in 214 s on average. Five code families are covered, and Lean 4 formalises the metatheory and the exact circuit-level distances of the surface, hypergraph-product and toric families. The guarantees hold under the stated fault contract. Correlated or multi-qubit faults are outside it.

6

Dynamical GKP codes

J. Pelletier, B. Royer (Sherbrooke)2611.140811Wed 10:17●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here It brings Floquet-code machinery to oscillators from a known bosonic-codes group, but CV codes draw a smaller audience than qubit QEC.

Pelletier and Royer extend dynamical codes to GKP lattices. Subsystem GKP codes built on scaled symplectic lattices let them adapt the ancestry, detector and benign-error tools of dynamical qubit codes and compute syndromes and distances from a persistent stabiliser lattice. Their two-mode square–hexagonal Floquet GKP code uses lower-weight measurements than either static code and has both codes’ logical gates, so the full single-qubit Clifford group is easier to implement. Measurements with irrational symplectic overlap, which have no qubit analogue, reduce to destructive homodyne detection in one mode.

7

Error Resilience and Unitary 2-Designs

Z.-H. Sun, F. Ballar Trigueros, X. Turkeshi, P. Sierant2611.143141Wed 10:17●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A clean inequality from a group known for work on magic and designs, though encode–error–decode phases are a smaller topic.

In encode–error–decode circuits with random encoders, a 2-design gives an error-protecting phase below a threshold. The authors prove an inequality that bounds recovery fidelity by the second unitary frame potential, so departures from Haar second moments can destroy that phase. Matchgate encoders on their own have no stable protecting phase in the thermodynamic limit, and adding non-Gaussian gates brings it back as the ensemble approaches a 2-design. Hamiltonian encoders, including an all-to-all spin model, follow the same mechanism.

Sample complexity of learning fermionic and bosonic Gaussian states with single-copy and entangled measurements

R. Rubin2611.134287Wed 10:17●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here It resolves a stated conjecture and fills most of the Gaussian-learning table, which the model cannot see; at 138 pages and single-authored it stays at Low-Med.

With single-copy measurements, mixed fermionic Gaussian states on m modes, including pure modes, can be learned to trace distance ε from Θ(m3/ε2) copies with no logarithmic factor, against Θ(m2/ε2) for collective measurements. This proves the upper bound of Conjecture 10.1 of arXiv:2609.23189. Blocks of k copies give Θ(max{m3/k, m2}/ε2). For bosons the paper gives occupation-dependent minimax rates without gap assumptions, and it shows that non-Gaussian measurements remove the log log E term.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
