Anthropic and OpenAI post rival Lean-checked proofs that VP ≠ VNP

Hours apart on Monday, both labs claimed superpolynomial lower bounds for the permanent, settling Valiant’s 1979 conjecture if they hold. Complexity theorists are impressed and reading closely; no human referee has signed off. Disclosure: Claude writes this paper, and Anthropic makes Claude.

Anthropic (Claude); OpenAIComputational Complexity · Shtetl-Optimized · OfficeChai · Interesting Engineering

On Monday Anthropic posted a 70-page paper credited to Claude, with a Lean 4 formalisation, claiming that the permanent has no polynomial-size algebraic circuits. That is Valiant’s VP ≠ VNP conjecture of 1979, the algebraic analogue of P ≠ NP. A few hours later OpenAI added a 30-page paper, “VP and VNP are different”, and its own Lean code to its public mathematics repository; the changelog says its model found the result on 6 November (OfficeChai). Anthropic’s bound for the n × n permanent is 2Ω((log n)4/3), OpenAI’s exp(Ω((log n)5/4)). Both are superpolynomial, which is all the separation needs. Claude writes this newspaper, and Anthropic makes Claude.

Lance Fortnow calls it the most important complexity result of a year that has had several (Computational Complexity). By his reading the Anthropic proof uses almost no algebraic geometry, so it bypasses the Mulmuley–Sohoni geometric complexity theory programme: a variant of Strassen’s asymptotic spectrum splits the problem into a case handled by a rank argument and one handled by diagonalisation. Reports describe the same structure in both papers, with circuits recast as tensor networks embedded in space and charged for wire length, a “flat” case settled geometrically and a “contracting” case settled by diagonalisation. OpenAI’s paper credits a grid method from Anthropic’s October paper on the Exponential Time Hypothesis. One consequence is that writing the permanent as a determinant of affine entries needs matrices of size exp(Ω((log n)4/3)).

Scott Aaronson finds the two techniques similar and expects diagonalisation to be what carries both arguments past the natural-proofs barrier; he has raised his credence in P ≠ NP from 95 to 96 per cent, and says he will hear technical objections from experts but has no patience left for dismissals built on Lean nitpicks (Shtetl-Optimized). Fortnow cautions that VP ≠ VNP gives no method for Boolean circuits, so P versus NP remains “far out of reach for man or machine”. The open question in the coverage is fidelity: whether the short statement files define circuits, VP and VNP as complexity theorists mean them, which no human has yet checked (Interesting Engineering). Accounts also differ on the fields covered: Aaronson writes characteristic other than two, while press reports say every field, using the Hamiltonian-cycle polynomial in characteristic two.

For quantum readers the direct consequences are limited. BosonSampling hardness rests on the #P-hardness of approximating permanents, a Boolean statement that an algebraic lower bound does not imply. The same week, Khanna, Putterman and Song posted a deterministic linear-time minimum-spanning-tree algorithm whose main ideas, they say, came from an AI model. Things to watch are a human audit of both statement files, arXiv versions, and whether the shared flat-or-contracting structure survives scrutiny.

A DOE blueprint asks for 100-logical-qubit machines in the national labs from 2028

R. Bennink, R. Blume-Kohout, S. Habib, A. Landahl, H. A. Nam, L. Schulz, M. Shankar, N. Wright · 2611.31514 1Wed 14:44

A “Blueprint for DOE Quantum Supercomputing” on today’s listing argues that the US Department of Energy should prepare now to deploy fault-tolerant machines as scientific instruments (arXiv:2611.31514). Its authors, among them Robin Blume-Kohout and Andrew Landahl, set a first-generation target of at least 100 logical qubits running 10,000 to 100,000 hard logical operations on scientific problems, with initial deployment from 2028, followed in the 2030s by production systems of 1,000 to 10,000 logical qubits and one to ten billion hard operations. Alongside hardware it asks for FTQC programming skills, system software, facility readiness, and focused research on applications, algorithms and logical architectures. The document commits no money, though it lands a day after the DOE’s $159 million Phase II Genesis Mission awards for error correction and co-design (QCR). The 2028 date assumes that commercial roadmaps deliver, which the blueprint takes as given.

Classical algorithms for sparse random Hamiltonians via free probability

M. Ding, R. King, T. Schuster2611.3312835Wed 14:44●●● High confidence

Why here King and Schuster take a leading candidate for average-case simulation advantage and match the quantum cost classically, the kind of topical result from known names that led on 19 November; 13 places above its prior (14), which does not weigh the question.

Simulation of many-body dynamics is the most widely conjectured source of quantum advantage, and random models are a natural place to look for it on average: sign problems and fast entanglement growth defeat quantum Monte Carlo and tensor networks, while a quantum computer simply runs the dynamics. Ding, King and Schuster test this on sparse random non-local Hamiltonians and find that, for the quantities people actually compute, the advantage disappears.

Their classical algorithm computes spectral fluctuations, thermal expectation values, and time-ordered and out-of-time-order correlators. For any subexponential precision ε it runs in time (1/ε)2+o(1), essentially the cost of the quantum simulation itself. The guarantee covers late times, low temperatures and the neighbourhood of critical points, exactly where earlier classical methods take exponential time.

The method does not represent states or operators. It first computes the model’s mean behaviour, then a short hierarchy of instance-dependent corrections, and free probability turns short-time data into predictions for long-time behaviour. The paper has a 15-page main text and 114 pages of appendices.

The result narrows the search for average-case advantage in dynamics, since sparse SYK-type models have been one of its standard settings. It leaves geometrically local random Hamiltonians and structured instances open, and the abstract does not say how far the free-probability expansion extends beyond the sparse non-local ensemble. For readers in classical simulation, it adds a fourth approach next to Pauli propagation, tensor networks and Monte Carlo: predict the statistics of the instance and skip the state entirely.

Optimal Trace Distance Estimation

Z. Chen, M. Gao, Q. Wang2611.3253010Wed 14:44●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A short, plainly titled tight bound, the profile that has beaten our long picks on four listings; Medium because the authors have no record in our table; 19 places above its prior (21).

Estimating the trace distance between two unknown d-dimensional states to additive error ε is a basic property-testing task, and its sample complexity has had a gap between upper and lower bounds. Chen, Gao and Wang close it: Θ(d2/(ε2 log2 d · max{1, ε2 log2 d})) copies of each state suffice and are necessary, matching the 2026 lower bound of Wang.

The estimator combines tomography with polynomial approximation of the absolute-value function, and the variance bounds come from quantum Fourier analysis. The log2 d saving over plain tomography is the same kind of saving known for classical total-variation estimation, and the paper shows it carries over to the quantum case. The abstract gives no lower-order terms or computational cost, which a practitioner would want.

Neural Decoding of Logical Clifford Circuits with Transversal Gates

Y. Guang, Y. Xiong, X. Gao, Y. Chen2611.316076Wed 14:44●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Decoders with public code and head-to-head numbers have reached the top five on three listings, and this one beats the Lukin group’s neural decoder on its own circuits; near its prior (8).

Transversal gates on atom and ion machines need one syndrome round, up to d times faster than lattice surgery, but a transversal CNOT copies errors between blocks and forces the decoder to read the whole circuit at once. Duet is a neural decoder that builds in what is known exactly: features are exchanged only between sites a gate connects, logical outcomes propagate linearly through Clifford layers and are combined by a soft XOR, and the gates of each layer are inputs, so one network per distance decodes circuits of any size and depth.

Paired with the Tesseract search decoder on its least confident shots, it fails 44 times less often than LoMatching at distance 9 on circuits with 2 to 4 blocks and up to 16 layers. On the Lukin group’s random circuits it fails 0.299 times as often as their neural decoder at distance 7, at 2.4 to 121.7 μs per layer. Code, trained networks and raw data are public.

Spinal reflected entropy reveals logical magic

Z.-Y. Hou, Y. Zhang, Z.-C. Yang, Y. Gu2611.3137530Wed 14:44●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A nine-page result that makes stabilizer Rényi entropy an entanglement quantity in the toric code, short and concrete; near its prior (7).

Magic and entanglement are usually treated as separate resources. Hou, Zhang, Yang and Gu define the spinal reflected entropy on a neighbourhood of a spine of the surface carrying a toric code, as the reflected entropy between a disk around the spine’s vertex and bands along its noncontractible loops. It obeys an area law with a topological correction plus one term that carries all dependence on the stored state; on the torus that term is the stabilizer Rényi entropy of the logical state plus the log of the ground-state degeneracy.

The mechanism is topological: on the surface sewn to its reflection the bands close into tubes, logical Paulis become anyon charges threading them, and those charges are distributed as the outcomes of Bell sampling on the stored state. The result extends to higher genus and to general 2D topological stabilizer codes, and it makes logical magic a geometric quantity that shallow code-preserving circuits cannot change.

Thermal state preparation with shallow quantum circuits

C. Tüysüz, S. Deconihout, E. Rinaldi, M. Grossi2611.3238820Wed 14:44●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A Pauli-propagation tool run at 96 and 98 qubits on Quantinuum Helios, the profile our 13 November note says to promote; 30 places above its prior (35), which cannot see the hardware scale.

Preparing low-temperature thermal states on hardware needs shallow circuits, and compiling good classical representations into circuits is itself costly. PP-VarQITE uses Pauli propagation to run variational imaginary-time evolution entirely classically, with the target temperature encoded in the angles of a fixed architecture. On Ising and frustrated Heisenberg models it recovers low-temperature energies and correlations at up to 100 qubits, with circuits two to ten times shallower than the best sequential tensor-network compilations.

The authors then prepare a 48-site frustrated chain and a 7×7 Ising lattice on 96 and 98 qubits of Quantinuum Helios, watching dimer order and lattice-wide correlations develop as the temperature falls, with at most 42 two-qubit layers for the lattice. The classical design step is what makes the hardware run feasible, and it works at sizes beyond statevector simulation, though not beyond the reach of Pauli propagation itself.

Second-order asymptotics for the quantum and private capacities of the erasure channel

M. M. Wilde2611.3239514Wed 14:44●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Wilde has an 80th-percentile record, but a 74-page proof starts slowly, so it sits below the shorter picks; the model’s top paper.

Wilde determines the optimal second-order rates for quantum and private communication over the erasure channel with p < 1/2 at fixed infidelity ε: n(1−2p) log d + 2√(np(1−p)) log d Φ−1(ε) + O((ln n)2), for entanglement transmission, generation and private communication alike. The new part is the matching converse for every ε, not only below one half. A side result shows that the privacy criterion matters at second order: fixing the ideal environment state to the actual marginal changes the quantile to Φ−1(1−√(1−ε)).

How Small Can a Single Shor Run Be? Limits and Constructions for secp256k1

Y. Guang, Y. Xiong, Y. Chen, X. Gao2611.315856Wed 14:44●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Yesterday’s RSA-2048 estimate drew 13 scites, so cryptographic resource counts have an audience, but this one targets the size of a single run, a narrower question; near its prior (4).

The question is how far Ekerå-style splitting can shrink the largest single run of Shor’s algorithm against secp256k1. The authors prove that a fixed linear run reveals at most log2(1+(|S|−1)/r) bits of the key, which caps splitting alone at a 1.86-fold shrink within their budget. Windows that share table points across six curve symmetries go further: the largest run shrinks 3.87 times at 8.79 times the baseline volume, against 2.81 times for Ekerå’s trade-off, and all 563 pre-registered simulated trials succeed.

Dirac Propagation for Simulating Fermionic Observables under Particle-Number-Conserving Unitaries

G. A. Naidu, M. D’Anna, A. Pérez-Salinas, J. Nys, J. Carrasquilla2611.3290219Wed 14:44●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A fermionic counterpart of Pauli propagation with an exact truncation rule, from Pérez-Salinas and Carrasquilla; Low-Med because method papers without large numerics have started slowly; 10 places above its prior (18).

Dirac propagation evolves local fermionic observables in the Heisenberg picture under particle-number-conserving circuits. The key observation is that operator strings of weight above the total particle number can be dropped with no error in the final expectation value, so at fixed particle number the retained strings stay polynomial in the mode count, including under interacting gates. Number-conserving Gaussian and Clifford unitaries are handled exactly. Unlike Pauli propagation’s weight truncation, this cutoff is exact; the abstract gives no benchmark sizes.

Autonomous quantum machine learning via Riemannian gradient-based optimization

K. Srimahajariyapong, T. Chotibut, S. Suwannajitt, T. Paka, R. Puig, Y. Suzuki, M. Gluza, Z. Holmes, S. Thanasilp22final

Training a quantum model usually means measuring, updating parameters classically and repeating, so information extraction limits scaling. Srimahajariyapong and coauthors, with Holmes’s group at EPFL and Gluza, derive Riemannian gradient updates that run entirely inside the quantum device, with no measurement or classical feedback, and give sufficient conditions under which a broad class of learning tasks trains autonomously. In that regime they prove exponential convergence in the number of iterations for small step sizes and analyse how circuit depth grows. We did not list it; it tied for third with 22.

Foliated surface codes for optimal skip-gate leakage correction

R. Bi, N. Astrakhantsev, T. B. Smith, M. Pavlovich, M. Li, S. Puri22final

Skip-gate leakage, such as atom loss or dual-rail photon loss, removes later gates on the leaked qubit and is often detectable only at measurement. Puri’s group shows that the best SWAP-based surface code, optimal against space-like leakage, is suboptimal against time-like leakage, while the foliated surface code corrects as many leakage errors as it detects in both configurations, with simulations at 0.5% two-qubit-gate leakage. We mentioned it only in Closest to your work; it tied for third with 22.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
