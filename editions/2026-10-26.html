New York budgets $360 million for quantum hubs and commissions a study that ranks the state second only to California

$300 million for a Stony Brook hub, $60 million for up to four regional commercialization hubs, and federal fab money in Albany and Malta. The study behind the announcement was written by The Quantum Insider, and its economic projections are the only ones on offer.

Gov. K. Hochul; NYSTAR (Empire State Development)The Quantum Insider

On 23 October Governor Kathy Hochul released a Quantum Technology Landscape Study, commissioned by NYSTAR, the science and technology division of Empire State Development. The Quantum Insider prepared it. It counts 134 operating quantum entities, 219 research groups and 734 patent families across ten regions of the state, ranks New York second to California on businesses, research groups and patents, and says New York is one of two states covering the whole value chain from research to manufacturing to customers. The money behind it adds up to $360 million: $300 million for the Quantum Research and Innovation Hub at SUNY Stony Brook, due in 2029, and $60 million in the FY 2027 budget for up to four regional commercialization hubs of up to $15 million each, which NYSTAR is selecting after applications closed on 14 October.

The state money sits next to larger federal commitments. Quantum Computing Report counts $1.37 billion: a $1 billion CHIPS Act award to Anderon, finalized in September and matched by $1 billion from IBM, for an open 300 mm superconducting quantum foundry at the Albany NanoTech complex, and a $375 million CHIPS award to GlobalFoundries in Malta. Its map of the state’s supply chain runs from Bluefors dilution refrigerators in Syracuse and SEEQC’s SFQ control chips in Elmsford to the 161-mile Stony Brook–Brookhaven network testbed and IBM’s Poughkeepsie site, where the Starling fault-tolerant system is targeted for 2029. For scale, the study puts government quantum commitments worldwide at about $42 billion, $8.9 billion of it federal US money, and private financing at $5.3 billion so far in 2026.

We found no outside reaction. The study’s author is also a news outlet that reported on it. Its forecast that the $360 million will generate $2.3 to $5 billion of economic activity and 7,200 to 10,100 job-years through 2036 is the study’s own modelling, with no independent check. The federal framing is also unsettled: Senator Schumer’s statement credits $1 billion of CHIPS money to IBM for a new fab, while the governor’s release attributes it to Anderon, and neither reconciles the two. Most of the $360 million had already been announced: the Stony Brook award in an earlier release, and the hubs in the 2026 State of the State address, reported by Bloomberg Law.

The new parts are the ranking and a map of the state as one connected industry. Watch for the names of the regional hubs, which NYSTAR has not announced; first wafers from the Albany foundry, the most concrete deliverable on the list; and whether New York’s numbers hold up once other states that compete for quantum money, such as Illinois, Colorado and Maryland, publish comparable counts.

Hardware · Superconducting control

Jülich reports single-qubit gates in 6.8 ns at 99.98% fidelity by cancelling multiphoton transitions

Forschungszentrum Jülich

The Barends and Gao experimental groups and Motzoi’s control-theory group at Forschungszentrum Jülich, with colleagues in Aachen and Cologne, report in Nature Communications single-qubit gates on a transmon lasting 6.8 ns, against the several tens of nanoseconds typical of superconducting processors. The pulse shaping, called R2D, extends DRAG by also suppressing the spectral components that drive multiphoton transitions to higher levels; the institute says these transitions fall by more than a factor of 20 relative to the previous standard. The two gates tested reached 99.981% and 99.989% fidelity, with leakage below 0.002% per operation. The release states the limitation itself: one isolated transmon, no two-qubit gates, and further experiments needed to show it works in larger processors. Shorter single-qubit layers matter most for syndrome-extraction cycles and dynamical decoupling, where they cut idle time, so the open question is crosstalk once neighbouring qubits are driven at the same time. We found no independent comment yet; the institute’s release is the only source beyond the paper.

Neutral atoms · China

A survey maps China’s neutral-atom programme: 2,024-atom defect-free arrays, 99.84% two-qubit gates, and a gap in error correction

Quantum Computing Report

A review from Information Engineering University (arXiv:2608.05010), summarized by Quantum Computing Report on 24 October, collects the Chinese results from the past few years. Pan Jianwei’s group at USTC assembles defect-free arrays of 2,024 atoms in 60 ms, with 99.84% two-qubit and 99.97% single-qubit fidelity and 99.92% readout. Zhai Hui’s group at Tsinghua traps an average of 11,022 atoms on a 135 × 135 grid made by a 2 cm metasurface and rearranges arrays of more than 10,000 atoms in under 20 ms with a graph neural network. Fudan runs a 1,000-atom platform with under 1% loss. Commercial systems include Zhongke Kuyuan’s Hanyuan 1 and 2, with more than 500 tweezers and over 80% of components sourced domestically, and a 1,000-qubit Hanyuan 3 is planned; Liangyi Wanxiang targets an error-corrected system with more than 1,000 qubits by 2027. The review’s own assessment is that China matches or leads Western groups on array size, rearrangement speed and raw gate fidelity, but trails QuEra–Harvard, Atom Computing and Infleqtion on multi-round error correction and logical operations, and is only now moving from mid-circuit measurement to repeated syndrome extraction. Company figures in the survey are as the companies report them.

The Entire Quantum Computer on a Classical Leash

Y. T. Kalai (MIT), D. Khurana (UIUC, NTT Research), J. Raizes (NTT Research)2610.31775—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Sixteen places above its prior: the model does not know that Kalai and Khurana are on the author list, or that the abstract claims to settle two named open problems in classical verification; a result of this kind draws the complexity and cryptography readers in quant-ph in one go.

Can a purely classical party use one untrusted quantum server as if it were its own trusted quantum computer? Mahadev’s 2018 protocol verifies decision problems classically, but its measurements destroy the state, so sampling and stateful computation stayed out of reach. Kalai, Khurana and Raizes build, from the post-quantum hardness of LWE alone, a maliciously secure two-party computation between a classical client and a quantum server for circuits with real gates. The client privately chooses the circuit and how to prepare the initial state. The server may supply quantum inputs entangled with outside systems, and designated classical outputs go to the client. The client can also keep quantum memory on the server across rounds. That memory is hidden from the server and authenticated between steps, even when it is entangled with an external system.

The applications settle two open problems. The first is classical verification of quantum sampling with negligible soundness error, where Chung, Lee, Lin and Wu had reached only inverse-polynomial soundness and asked whether negligible was possible. The second is remote state preparation of any state a real circuit can prepare efficiently, where Gheorghiu–Vidick and Gheorghiu–Metger–Poremba covered restricted families with inverse-polynomial error. A third corollary gives classical revocation of leased quantum keys. The machinery combines Feynman history-state verification with non-destructive testing through a state-preserving 2PC, commitments that stay bound to a quantum state across repeated decoding queries, an input-preserving 2PC for constant-degree polynomials built on noisy trapdoor claw-free functions, and quantum fully homomorphic encryption to hide what the client runs.

The guarantees have limits that the authors spell out. Security is ε-simulation: the simulator runs in time polynomial in 1/ε, although the honest parties’ cost does not depend on ε, so efficiently testable games keep negligible loss. Gates must be real, and complex circuits are handled by the standard encoding. Against the classical party the base protocol is secure only against honest-but-curious behaviour, and a generic zero-knowledge compiler upgrades it. A footnote reports that an AI model proposed a construction removing the ε loss for the key primitive; the authors are still checking it.

Entangled logical qubits on a superconducting quantum processor

R. Harper, X. C. Kolesnikow, C. K. McLauchlan, G. M. Nixon, S. D. Bartlett, B. J. Brown (Sydney, IBM)2610.32347—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A logical-qubit experiment on IBM’s newest chip with Bartlett and Brown on it; last week the outsider-run surface code on Nighthawk finished second with 22 scites, and this one adds lattice surgery and teleportation.

Sydney and IBM authors run surface codes on ibm_phoenix, a 120-qubit square-lattice Nighthawk r2 with fast combined measurement and reset. Defective qubits are excluded by re-purposing ancillas, which halves the distance-5 logical error per round from 1.9% to 0.9%. Decoding uses BeliefMatching with a noise model learned by ACES. Distance-5 memories reach 0.872% (X) and 0.855% (Z) per round, and 1.69% in the Y basis, where SPAM is worse. No Λ is reported. Lattice surgery between two logical qubits gives Bell fidelities bounded above 93%, after post-selection that keeps 40% of shots. With three logical qubits they teleport states and gates, including T states prepared non-fault-tolerantly, at fidelities from 78% to 93%.

A 4×4 stability experiment with a reset-every-two-rounds walking circuit failed 8 times in 50 million runs over 170 rounds, about 2×10−7, which amounts to 603.5 billion two-qubit gates in all. The authors list the weak points: Bell-pair transfer keeps only 1.2% of shots, gate teleportation is checked by state tomography rather than process tomography, crosstalk between couplers driven at the same time costs about a factor of two, and nothing non-Clifford is fault-tolerant. The shots are on a public cloud device, so other groups can repeat them.

The Compendium of Super-Quadratic Computational Quantum Advantages

P. Kammerlander, T. E. O’Brien, R. King, R. D. Somma, …, M. Troyer, B. C. Sanders, M. Christandl, R. Babbush (OQI, Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA, JPMorganChase)2610.32595—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A 70-page reference with 450 citations from Babbush, Troyer, Christandl and the Google algorithms group: surveys by known groups rarely miss the top ten, but reference works are cited more than they are scited on day one.

Which problems have credible evidence of a more-than-quadratic quantum speedup in offline, non-relativized time, and what must a real instance look like for that speedup to survive? Twenty-one authors from the Open Quantum Institute in Geneva, Google Quantum AI, Microsoft, NVIDIA, JPMorganChase and Copenhagen write for domain experts outside the field. They sort entries into Stage II, concrete instances with evidence of advantage, and Stage I, primitives with evidence. Stage II covers the hidden-subgroup family (factoring, discrete log, Pell’s equation, point counting on curves, Gauss sums, elliptic-curve isogenies), optimal polynomial intersection and k-SAT, planted inference (tensor PCA, sparse noisy k-XOR, hypergraph community detection) and out-of-time-order correlators. Stage I covers ground-state energies, spectral densities, equilibrium and dynamical observables, linear systems and differential equations, Jones polynomials and topological data analysis.

The paper is explicit about what it leaves out. Quadratic speedups, space and communication advantages, oracle separations without an efficient oracle (Forrelation, welded trees, Simon), empirical wins on chosen instances and small-size demonstrations are all left out. Each entry spells out the costs that can erase an advantage: input and output, state preparation and data loading. Classical baselines are treated as moving targets, so each entry is also a claim that dequantization work can try to knock down.

Temporal Degeneracy Limits Message Passing Decoding of QLDPC Codes Under Repeated Noisy Syndrome Measurements

D. Chytas, N. Raveendran, B. Vasić (Arizona)2610.31877—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The model’s #1, and decoder papers finished third and fourth in the 20 October listing; it sits fourth because the noise model is phenomenological and the authors are less followed than Friday’s BP-OSD group.

Friday’s Fregona et al. traced BP-OSD failures on bivariate bicycle codes to Tanner-graph symmetries in space. Chytas, Raveendran and Vasić find the time-direction analogue. With repeated noisy syndrome rounds, the space-time decoding graph has a temporal kernel generated by elementary codewords of weight dv + 2. For regular codes of girth above four this gives degenerate error patterns of weight (dv + 2)/2 or dv + 1, with multiplicity growing linearly in blocklength and number of rounds. These patterns defeat any deterministic isotropic message-passing decoder with a flooding schedule. For BB codes they have weight four, below what the distances 12 and 18 would correct. On [[288,12,18]] isotropic min-sum fails on every tested weight-two pattern and on 19,008 of 38,880 weight-four patterns at two rounds.

Their fix breaks the symmetry. Space-time anisotropic min-sum treats spatial and temporal edges differently, and a guided variant runs ND extra randomized realizations. At four rounds and α = 0.02 the guided decoder’s logical error falls from 2.09×10−4 with one realization to 2.58×10−5 with 99, about an order of magnitude below min-sum with OSD-0 and comparable to Relay-BP. Cost stays linear in the number of edges. The noise is phenomenological; circuit-level noise is left for later.

Low-frustration block encodings by patch routing

M. Liu, A. Gnanasekaran, A. Kaushik, S. Hariprakash, A. Marefat Khah, M. Roetteler, G. Salton (IonQ)2610.32946—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Resource-estimate papers with large constant-factor savings on standard benchmarks (15–17× on frustrated J1–J2) do well, and Roetteler is known; a company affiliation and the absence of new asymptotics keep it at Medium.

Block encodings of lattice Hamiltonians usually pay for every Pauli term in the linear combination of unitaries, and the normalization λ grows with frustration. Liu, Gnanasekaran and the IonQ team block-encode a small local patch once. A SWAP-style routing network then brings each patch to a fixed location, applies the encoding and routes it back. The local cost is paid once per query whatever the lattice size, while routing grows linearly with it, with leading cost 8Ns. Larger patches can be encoded with smaller normalization at higher local cost. Symmetries and algebraic structure of the local terms keep that cost down.

On a 120×120 J1–J2 Heisenberg model near maximal frustration (J2/J1 = 0.4–0.6), the leading T cost per qubitized phase-estimation run falls 15–17× below Pauli-LCU constructions with unary iteration. The half-filled Fermi–Hubbard model gains about 3× and time evolution of the honeycomb spin liquid about 5×. Initial-state preparation and overlap are excluded, so end-to-end savings will be smaller.

Finite Nonsignalling Resources Suffice to Simulate Entangled Qubits

Z. Tang, X. Wang, G. Bai (HKUST Guangzhou)2610.32319—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Twenty-nine places above its prior: the model scores foundations low, but this answers a question Gisin posed in 2009 with an explicit bound, and Xin Wang’s group is widely read.

One PR box simulates a maximally entangled qubit pair (Cerf, Gisin, Massar, Popescu), but some partially entangled states need more, and known exact protocols relied on a “millionaire” box with continuous inputs. Tang, Wang and Bai show that finitely many PR boxes run in parallel, with shared randomness and no communication, reproduce all projective-measurement statistics of cos θ|00⟩ + sin θ|11⟩, using at most 128 cot4θ calls whatever the settings. The key step is an exact finite-selection rule from a fixed shared list. It extends to mixed states and binary POVMs; the bound is not claimed optimal.

QuantumMatterExplorer: An Autonomous Agent for the Discovery of Quantum Many-Body Phases of Matter

J. Nys, A. Jreissaty, J. Carrasquilla2610.32001—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Fifty-one places above its prior: the model does not know that agentic AI papers topped two listings this month or that Carrasquilla is a draw, but a many-body phase diagram that agrees with a published one is less striking than records.

An LLM-driven agent takes a Hamiltonian and parameter ranges and returns a phase diagram from its own DMRG runs, checked against exact diagonalization. It keeps a written plan, picks observables and refines scans as evidence comes in. Given a frustrated bilinear–biquadratic spin-1 chain with single-ion anisotropy, whose phase diagram was published recently, it finds Néel, Haldane and dimerized phases and incommensurate correlations in eight hours of compute, in broad agreement with the reference. The paper includes a 13-page research note written by the agent; the model behind it is not named.

Elementary proofs of quantum lower bounds for element distinctness and k-sum

A. Ambainis, M. Kokainis (Latvia)2610.32693—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Ambainis revisiting his own signature problem with an eight-page proof that a course could teach; short notes by famous names do well, but this one proves nothing new.

The Ω(n2/3) query lower bound for element distinctness and the Ω(nk/(k+1)) bound for k-sum are normally proved with the polynomial method or the adversary method. Ambainis and Kokainis reprove both, the latter for fixed k and large enough groups, using only basic properties of quantum states and linear algebra: they track how the algorithm’s states on different inputs drift apart. Eight pages, aimed at teaching and at problems where the heavy methods are awkward.

Toshiba launches a modular quantum-safe networking platform that runs QKD over lit fibre

The QSN platform connects one hub to hundreds of remote sites through active optical switching, and multiplexes quantum signals at 1550 or 1310 nm onto third-party DWDM fibre already carrying traffic, so dark fibre is no longer needed. A key-delivery system hands out QKD, post-quantum and hybrid keys with automatic rerouting, and an SDN controller maps topology. Toshiba says operators, defence contractors and cloud providers are taking it up; it gives no customer names, prices or key rates.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
