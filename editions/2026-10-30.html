Alice & Bob puts RSA-2048 at 22,593 cat qubits and 3.14 days, if bit flips can be held off for a century

A 128-page compilation from Alice & Bob, Inria, CEA and Edinburgh needs about 40 times fewer qubits than Gidney’s 2025 transmon estimate. Coverage agrees that the compilation advance is real and that the hardware it assumes does not yet exist.

A. Pappalardo, D. Goudarzi, …, É. Gouzien, V. Londe (Alice & Bob, Inria, CEA, Edinburgh)Quantum Computing Report

Alice & Bob and collaborators at Inria, CEA and the University of Edinburgh have posted an end-to-end compilation of Shor’s algorithm onto superconducting cat qubits, also filed as IACR ePrint 2026/2665. The headline configuration factors RSA-2048 with 22,593 cat qubits in an expected 3.14 days, assuming a 1 μs error-correction cycle, a 10 μs reaction time, circuit-level phase-flip noise at pZ = 5×10−4 and no bit flips at all. Reusing the same compiled subroutines, ECDLP on secp256k1 needs at most about 30,000 qubits and an hour, and FFDLP-512, which the authors propose as an intermediate benchmark, about 6,700 qubits and 21 minutes. A companion paper applies the same toolkit to FeMoco and gets under 7,500 cat qubits and under a day per phase-estimation run.

For comparison, Gidney’s May 2025 transmon estimate needed just under a million noisy qubits for about five days. PostQuantum puts the new design about 40 times lower in qubits and about 63 times lower in qubit-days, and also sets it beside Iceberg Quantum’s Pinnacle (about 98,000 qubits for a month) and the Oratomic–Caltech neutral-atom estimates (11,000–13,000 qubits, but runtimes of decades). The savings come from biased noise: bit flips are suppressed in hardware, an outer code handles only phase flips, LDPC cat codes more than halve the count relative to the company’s 2023 baseline of 49,649 qubits, and Gidney’s adder is recompiled around a new parity-CNOT gate.

Every piece of coverage we found treats this as a resource estimate, and the critical points concern the assumptions. Quantum Brief notes that on 20 October Alice & Bob reported its best 12-qubit chip running a distance-3 repetition code “not yet below threshold”, and that zero bit flips amounts to a bit-flip time of about a century, compared with roughly 44 minutes measured on one idle cat qubit. PostQuantum counts the paper as a real cut in algorithmic and compilation cost that says nothing about whether the hardware will follow. It adds that the decoder is not designed in the paper, factories take about 13% of the chip, and the ECDLP figure is an upper bound that still exceeds Bitcoin’s ten-minute block time. Tolerating one bit flip per hour, with a distance-3 bit-flip layer, doubles the RSA count to about 45,000. We found no comments yet from independent QEC researchers and no forum thread above our threshold.

Things to watch: whether Alice & Bob’s next chips get repetition codes below threshold and bit-flip times from minutes to hours; whether a real decoder fits the assumed 10 μs reaction time; and whether other groups adopt FFDLP-512 as a staging benchmark. Migration dates are unchanged: NIST IR 8547 deprecates RSA after 2030 and disallows it after 2035, and the paper itself calls for migrating now with hybrid schemes.

Shor's algorithm compiled on cat qubits: 22 593 qubits for RSA-2048 in 3.14 days

Pappalardo, Goudarzi, Hertz, …, Gouzien, de la Vieuville, Londe2610.37839—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A factoring resource estimate with a record-low qubit count, picked up by the press within a day, and the last comparable paper (Gidney 2025) topped its listing.

The question is what Shor’s algorithm costs, down to physical qubits on a chip layout, on an architecture whose qubits barely bit-flip. The paper is long (128 pages, 56 figures, 40 tables) and partly a review: it lays out the modern logical circuits for RSA, ECDLP and finite-field discrete log with their trade-offs, then compiles the dominant subroutines onto two cat-qubit architectures, one with repetition cat codes and one with LDPC cat codes.

The new hardware-level pieces are a parity-CNOT gate for cat qubits, a multi-target CNOT extended to the LDPC-cat layout, and an adaptation of active magic-state injection. With these, the authors rebuild the adder from Gidney’s 2025 RSA-2048 estimate. They take magic-state factories and lookup compilation from the companion FeMoco paper (2610.37840) and use a coarser estimate for the phaseup. The output is a complete chip layout and schedule. At 1 μs cycles, 10 μs reaction time, pZ = 5×10−4 and no bit flips, RSA-2048 takes about 23,000 cat qubits and 3.14 days. ECDLP on secp256k1 takes at most 30,000 qubits and an hour, and FFDLP-512 about 6,700 qubits and 21 minutes. Trading space for time gives RSA-2048 in 1.25 hours on about 611,000 qubits.

The weak point is the bit-flip assumption, which corresponds to a bit-flip time of about 100 years. Relaxing it to about an hour by concatenating a distance-3 bit-flip code roughly doubles the count to 45,000, which is still far below transmon estimates. The phaseup is estimated, not compiled, and the decoder is assumed, not designed. FFDLP-512 gives early fault-tolerant machines a cryptographically meaningful target well short of RSA, and other groups may adopt it.

On the Quantum Chromatic Gap II

Ciardo, Mottet2610.40571—scites●●● High confidence

Why here First improvement on the quantum chromatic gap since Brassard–Cleve–Tapp in 1999, proving a SODA’26 conjecture; the prior misses it because short combinatorics abstracts score low in its features.

Ciardo conjectured at SODA’26 that Khot’s d-to-1 games admit a strong form of pseudo-telepathy. This paper proves it for every d ≥ 4: for any ε > 0 and any k there is a d-to-1 label-cover game of classical value below ε with a perfect finite-dimensional quantum strategy whose measurement contexts have radius at least k. The headline corollary is a family of graphs with quantum chromatic number 3 and arbitrarily large classical chromatic number. Brassard, Cleve and Tapp’s 1999 graphs had χ only exponential in χq, and this is the first improvement since.

The construction stretches the Mermin–Peres magic square by spacing its equations apart and linking them with long equation wires, which increases context radius while keeping the system classically unsatisfiable. Parallel repetition then drives the classical value down, the Dinur–Guruswami–Khot–Regev PCP turns the system into a multilayered Kneser hypergraph, and the Fei–Minzer–Wang reduction from hypergraph colouring to 4-to-1 games finishes the job; the authors show that reduction is quantum-complete and preserves radius. The question left open is d = 2, where the 2-to-1 setting meets unique-games-type questions.

Fast and accurate neural triage decoding of ultra-high-rate quantum LDPC codes

Hu, Wang, Luo, Xiang, Zhao2610.40490—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Decoders have been the hottest subfield in our scored listings, and this one claims near-optimal accuracy at one to two orders of magnitude lower latency on codes with hundreds of logical qubits.

High-rate qLDPC codes now reach encoding rates above 1/2 at around a thousand physical qubits, but their accurate decoders (BP+OSD and relatives) are slow, and no neural decoder had been shown for them. Neural triage decoding spends computation according to syndrome consistency. A local neural message-passing network makes a fast first guess, extra iterations reduce any remaining syndrome violations, and a rarely used diversification stage searches for a consistent correction on the leftover hard shots.

On lifted-product and Kasai codes encoding a few hundred logical qubits, the authors report accuracy comparable to near-optimal algorithmic decoders with latency lower by one to two orders of magnitude, plus robustness to mismatched error priors. The design comes from an analysis of the exact bitwise-marginal decoder, which has a syndrome-inconsistent error floor independent of distance. The current version is a five-page letter, the full supplement is promised in a later revision, and no code is linked yet, so the circuit-level noise setup and hardware latency numbers are still to be checked.

Individually Addressed Logical Pauli Measurements by QLDPC Surgery on a Trapped Ion Quantum Computer

Luthra, Owens2610.39067—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A first hardware run of QLDPC surgery on Quantinuum’s H2, and fault-tolerance demos on that machine have topped past listings; the small code and two-author team keep it at Medium, and the prior underweights hardware QEC.

Using QLDPC codes for computation means measuring one logical qubit inside a block without disturbing the others. Generalized surgery does this with a transient ancilla gadget, and it underlies Pauli-based computing proposals for bivariate-bicycle architectures. Here it runs on Quantinuum’s H2-2 with a [[10,2,3]] generalized bicycle code. A single-round measurement of X̄1 with seven gadget qubits has a 0.72% error rate [0.32, 1.41] and agrees with a later transversal measurement up to 1.03% error.

Decoding across three rounds of syndrome extraction brings the gadget error below 0.26%. The spectator logical qubit survives with 0.21% error, and the syndrome confirms return to the code space. A sequential X̄1-then-Z̄2 measurement that reuses the gadget qubits gives 1.12% error on Z̄2. With distance 3, the code is far from the [[144,12,12]] regime, so the run tests the surgery primitive on hardware for the first time and says little yet about how it scales.

Local Quantum Fault Tolerance Without Expansion

Ponarovsky2610.38847—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Answers Kubica and Preskill’s open question on local sweep corrections and proves a circuit-level threshold for a fixed local rule; single-author and proof-heavy, so it should climb slowly.

Kubica and Preskill’s sweep rule gives cellular-automaton decoding for higher-dimensional toric codes, but whether sweep corrections can be chosen locally on general cubical complexes was left open. Ponarovsky gives a local algorithm for cubical (d,d′)-toric complexes with d′, d−d′ ≥ 2, which includes the 4D toric code. He then proves a circuit-level threshold theorem in which one fixed, constant-depth correction cycle acting only on the current syndrome is interleaved with logical gates. It needs no multiscale decoder and no expansion.

The proof treats errors and syndromes as chains, combining geometric shrinking under the sweep rule with a space-time explanation-tree argument modelled on Toom’s theorem. For memory, nd+1 noiseless sweeps recover the state with failure probability poly(n)·pΩ(n) under local-stochastic noise, extending earlier guarantees that assumed independent errors. The author says explicitly that constant total-depth overhead is not claimed.

Algebraic strong-to-weak symmetry breaking and partial decodability in the surface code

Yan, Bao, Vijay2610.40567—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Mixed-state-phase theory tied to decoding has reached 15–30 scites before, and this paper gives an analytic replica limit backed by large numerics.

Single-qubit Z rotations followed by syndrome measurement on the triangular-lattice surface code drive a transition from a decodable phase to a critical, partially decodable one. Under Wegner duality the same transition takes a strongly symmetric Z2 paramagnet to algebraic strong-to-weak symmetry breaking. Both are described by an SO(n) nonlinear sigma model in the n → 1 limit, taken analytically here. The optimal Pauli decoding fidelity tends to a universal function of the aspect ratio, and the authors argue that no Pauli dephasing channel can produce this phase.

Subsystem Spacetime Code

Fu, Chakraborty, Gottesman2610.40357—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A general tool for computing the fault distance of Clifford circuits from Gottesman’s group, likely to be read by people working on dynamical codes, but short on headline numbers.

The spacetime-code picture of Clifford circuits is refined into a subsystem code whose gauge group, built explicitly from the masking properties of the instantaneous stabilizer groups, is exactly the set of faults harmless to the circuit. This gives a way to find where a dynamical code loses distance and how fault distance behaves when circuits are composed from smaller pieces, which bears directly on detector-error-model construction for Floquet and other dynamical codes.

What Can Be Computed with Indefinite Causal Structure?

Shmueli2610.40573—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A provocative claim (process matrices solve NP in polynomial time) from a cryptography theorist; ranked well above its prior because complexity claims of this kind draw readers whether or not they convince.

In a succinct process-matrix model, where a circuit describes the process and cost is the size of that generating circuit, Shmueli gives polynomial-time algorithms for every NP problem using valid processes that satisfy the Araújo et al. purification postulate and generate unitary channels. Restricting to pure process matrices, he still solves unique-sink orientation. The catch to watch is the model: the generating circuits are not themselves unitary, and readers will ask whether that makes the power an artefact of the formalism.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.
