Cat qubits · Hardware

Kerr cats reach 450-second bit flips at Weizmann, as Alice & Bob lists hours between flips on Helium 2

A Weizmann team turns the readout circuit into a cooler and pushes Kerr-cat bit-flip times up five orders of magnitude. Alice & Bob’s latest figures for its dissipative cats run to four hours. Both results come with the same caveat: the qubits were idle.

S. Hazanov, M. Svintsov, U. Goldblatt, B. Guttel, F. Lafont, S. Rosenblum (Weizmann, QuamCore); Alice & BobarXiv · Quantum Computing Report

Kerr-cat qubits, two coherent states held in the wells of a squeezing-driven nonlinear oscillator, allow fast gates, but their bit-flip times have been stuck at milliseconds because stray excitations hop between wells. In today’s listing Serge Rosenblum’s group at the Weizmann Institute reports 448 ± 57 s without adding any hardware. When an intra-well transition matches the detuning between the squeezing drive and the readout resonator (or its Purcell filter), the SNAIL’s three-wave mixing turns excitations into readout photons that leak away. Bit-flip time grows by a factor of 1.82 per added photon, from 52 μs at n̄ = 7.4 to the record at n̄ = 33.8; the top value comes from 62 flips counted over about 15 hours, and direct decay was measured out to 100 s. Phase flips follow single-photon loss with T1 ≈ 8 μs, which at n̄ = 24.2 gives a noise bias of about 3×107.

Dissipative cats were already in this range. Quantum Computing Report on 2 November summarized an Alice & Bob technical update with datasets. The single Galvanic-cat figure, a mean of 44 minutes (95% interval 33–60) from 41 jumps in 61 hours, was first reported in September 2025. The update adds three cats on the 12-qubit Helium 2 at 189, 252 and 189 minutes for ⟨n⟩ between 11 and 16. The numbers bear on last week’s Alice & Bob RSA-2048 compilation, which assumed no bit flips at all; allowing one per hour and adding a distance-3 outer code roughly doubles its 22,593 qubits to about 45,000.

QCR points out that the Alice & Bob data are for idle qubits, that the Helium 2 values come without confidence intervals or run lengths, and that coherence was checked only on a single qubit. The Weizmann authors list their own limits: an 8 μs oscillator lifetime caps the phase-flip time, cooling needs cold readout modes, and readout degrades near the cooling resonance, so the protocol switches between separate operating points. Rosenblum is a shareholder of QuamCore, which funded part of the work. Beyond the companies, the authors and the specialist press, we found no expert reaction and no forum thread above our threshold.

Things to watch: whether either platform keeps these bit-flip times through a bias-preserving CNOT, which both groups name as the next test; whether a Kerr-cat device with a longer-lived oscillator holds the bias at lower photon number; and whether Alice & Bob’s 12-qubit repetition code, which it described on 20 October as not yet below threshold, gets there.

Policy · European Union

IQM asks for qubit foundries and DARPA-style contracts before the EU Quantum Act

IQM · Quantum Computing Report

In a 3 November manifesto, IQM chief executive Jan Goetz sets out five priorities for the Quantum Act that the Commission is expected to present this month: industrial qubit-chip fabs by the 2030s, milestone-based procurement modelled on DARPA’s Quantum Benchmarking Initiative and pooled through EuroHPC, a Scale-up Europe Fund for late-stage rounds, eligibility of quantum chips as first-of-a-kind projects under a Chips Act 2.0, and a single window for state-aid approval with a fixed deadline. IQM’s case rests on about $10 billion of public money across member states and 21 quantum installations at European HPC centres against two in the US, set against European firms’ 12% share of disclosed venture funding (US firms 44%) and, citing ECIPE, 23% of EU funding spread over 34 sub-scale clusters. Quantum Computing Report notes that most of these figures carry no named source and that IQM, a listed hardware vendor, would benefit from the measures. The timing is also uncertain: in September a Commission official told MLex the proposal could slip into 2027.

Superconducting qubits · Control

One measurement before each sequence halves errors on a qubit that hops between two frequencies

Phys.org · Applied Physics Reviews

Fabrizio Berritta, Ferdinand Kuemmeth and colleagues (Regensburg, Niels Bohr Institute, MIT, Israeli Quantum Computing Center) operated a superconducting qubit whose frequency switches at random between two known values, probably because of a material defect. A single measurement before each sequence tells which frequency is active, and the controls are retuned by feedback in real time. The average error fell from about 0.14% to 0.065%, and the worst case without feedback by 77%, according to the Phys.org account. The method works only when the possible frequencies are known in advance, well separated, and stable for longer than the feedback loop.

Autonomous cooling of Kerr-cat qubits to minutes-long bit-flip times

Hazanov, Svintsov, Goldblatt, Guttel, Lafont, Rosenblum (Weizmann, QuamCore)2611.048681Wed 12:03●●● High confidence

Why here A five-orders-of-magnitude hardware record in a QEC-relevant qubit, from a known group, with clean numbers; experimental records on leading platforms have topped our scored listings.

Kerr cats encode a qubit in the two wells of a squeezing-driven Kerr oscillator. Bit flips should be suppressed exponentially in photon number, but in practice heating out of the ground manifold lets the state hop between wells, and earlier devices (Frattini et al., Hajr et al., Ding et al.) saturated at milliseconds. Hazanov, Svintsov and colleagues remove the excitations with what they call an autoparametric resonance. The SNAIL’s exchange term g21(a2b† + h.c.), written in the displaced frame, contains a beam-splitter coupling of strength 2g21√n̄ between intra-well excitations and readout photons. When the first intra-well transition matches the detuning between the squeezing drive and the readout resonator (or its Purcell filter), excitations leave through the readout line at rate κb. Multilevel anticrossings overlap into a band, so several excited levels are cooled at one operating point.

The bit-flip time TX grows by 1.82 ± 0.06 per photon over seven orders of magnitude, from 52 μs at n̄ = 7.4 to 448 ± 57 s at n̄ = 33.8 on the Purcell resonance, with no sign of saturation. The top value is inferred from 62 flips in about 15 hours of repeated readout; no decay was seen within the 100 s direct window. TZ follows T1/(2n̄) with T1 ≈ 8 μs, so at n̄ = 24.2 the authors report TX = 3.55 s and TZ = 113 ns, a bias near 3×107. A Z(π) gate takes 88 ns, continuous Z driving leaves TX unchanged, and a 4 μs cat-quadrature readout has assignment infidelity 5×10−7 and flips the qubit with probability 5×10−7.

This puts a Kerr cat, with its fast gates, in the regime so far reached only by dissipatively stabilized cats (Berdou et al. above 100 s, Alice & Bob’s Galvanic cat at 44 minutes). The limits are stated plainly. Phase flips are set by the 8 μs oscillator lifetime, well short of the 55.7 μs others have shown. Thermal photons in the readout set the cooling floor. Readout degrades near the cooling resonance, so the authors move between a readout point and an operating point. K, T1 and g21 drift with flux bias, and per-interval flip probabilities scatter more than Poisson statistics allow. No two-qubit gate is shown. Rosenblum holds shares in QuamCore, which funded part of the work.

Breaking the quantum barriers for Graph Collision

Cornelissen (Cambridge), Gilani, Kintali (NVIDIA), Le Gall (Nagoya), Patro (Eindhoven), Zhou2611.0535612Wed 12:03●●● High confidence

Why here It refutes a conjectured n2/3 lower bound for a textbook query problem and breaks the positive-adversary barrier, the kind of algorithms result we have under-ranked most often.

Graph collision asks whether a known n-vertex graph has an edge with both endpoints marked, with the marks accessed by queries. It came up as a subroutine for triangle finding, and since Magniez, Santha and Szegedy the best general algorithm used O(n2/3) queries. The k = 2 case of a marked-clique conjecture by Gilani, Mande, Patro and Raja said that some graphs need Ω(n2/3). The authors refute it with an Õ(n92/139) algorithm: estimate each vertex’s number of marked neighbours from sampled bits, threshold on those estimates to split the edge set, and test each part with a volume-bounded collision search. A refinement first caps the degree at d = Θ(n7/17) and then repeatedly halves the marked volume on bipartite pieces, reaching Õ(n11/17).

The same bound, Õ((nq)11/17), holds for every partial Boolean function whose positive certificates have size at most 2 over alphabet size q, which contrasts with a known Ω(n2/3) bound for large alphabets. On the lower side they prove Ω(√(n log n)) with a general adversary matrix built on the point–line incidence graph over 𝔽q2, going past the O(√n) certificate barrier of the positive adversary. The gap between n0.5 and n0.647 remains open.

Long-range entangled criticality from coherent errors

Negari, Sahu, Hsieh (Perimeter), Behrends, Béri (Cambridge)2611.0381120Wed 12:03●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Mixed-state-phase papers built on conditional mutual information have reliably reached 15–30 scites, and this one comes from Hsieh and Béri with two general theorems.

Coherent errors on topological codes are harder to analyse than Pauli noise, and it was unclear what phase a code enters once they pass threshold. The authors apply a uniform ZZ rotation exp(iθΣZi) to the honeycomb surface code and then dephase the syndrome. Up to θc ≈ 0.09π the code survives: coherent information tends to one bit, the Markov length is finite and a local decoder works. Between θc and π/4 they find an extended critical regime in which the coherent information sits at the Haar value 1/(2 ln 2) ≈ 0.72 bits, the conditional mutual information grows as ln L, open-string fidelities decay as power laws, and only global decoding recovers the partial memory. Maximum-likelihood decoding maps onto a class-DIII Majorana network, and the regime corresponds to its metallic phase, where conductance also grows as ln L.

Two general results carry the argument. If a channel preserves a one-form symmetry of the code as a strong symmetry and keeps the coherent information bounded away from zero, generic encoded states become long-range entangled. And the finite-Markov-length construction of local recovery extends to any locally parameterized family, covering Lindbladian evolution and thermal states. The authors call the regime a quasi-long-range counterpart of 1-form strong-to-weak symmetry breaking.

Reopening the one clean qubit case of a semi-grand challenge

C. W. Mansell (Terra Quantum)2611.050460Wed 12:03●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A short counterexample to a published simulation claim on one of Aaronson’s named challenges, the plainly titled kind of result that took first place on Monday; 43 places above the prior, which cannot see the named problem.

Aaronson’s 2005 semi-grand challenges asked whether a quantum computer that always stays in a separable mixed state can be simulated classically in polynomial time. Yoganathan and Cade (2019) and Yoganathan’s 2021 thesis claimed yes for DQC1, the one-clean-qubit model: every circuit that keeps the state fully separable should be a “product control” circuit in a canonical branching form that a classical algorithm can track. Mansell gives a counterexample. A clean qubit controls three gates on a maximally mixed three-qubit register (a CNOT, a gate that applies controlled-Z on c or a T phase depending on a, and a second CNOT). Every accumulated unitary has an orthonormal product eigenbasis, so the four-qubit state stays fully separable, yet the final unitary has a fixed basis only on qubit a and therefore no canonical form.

The gap in the thesis proof is an assumption that branches keep a common control direction after each gate; here they are controlled along Z on b in one branch and X on c in the other. A continuous-time version keeps the qubits separable throughout, and padding gives a uniform family. The counterexample circuits are themselves easy to simulate (the output is a fair coin), so the question is open again rather than answered the other way.

Quantum Advantage has Few Complexity Implications

Goldin, Lombardi, Morimae2611.0530321Wed 12:03●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A provocative title on proofs of quantumness from three active quantum-crypto names, cross-listed to cs.CC, where we keep missing top-ten papers; 11 places above the prior for that reason.

If proofs of quantumness exist, what does that tell us about complexity classes? Goldin, Lombardi and Morimae argue very little. They build a classical oracle relative to which P = NP and BQP = PSPACE, yet proofs of quantumness exist: some language in IPO ⊆ BQPO is hard on average against PO/poly. A second, unitary oracle makes BQP = QCMA while quantum-verifier oblivious state preparation still exists.

Under relativizing proofs, then, proofs of quantumness cannot yield classical or quantum cryptography, and oblivious transfer with quantum computation and classical communication cannot yield post-quantum one-way functions. The results add to the oracle evidence that quantum cryptographic primitives can survive the collapse of classical hardness assumptions. They are relativizing separations and say nothing about non-relativizing techniques.

Substantially Improved Rate and Distance for Addressable Transversal Phase Gates

B. Wu, T. Lin, B. Cheng2611.049644Wed 12:03●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Explicit code families with transversal non-Clifford gates follow the magic-state theme that has done well with us, but the gains are in constants.

Asymptotically good codes with fully addressable transversal gates at level h of the Clifford hierarchy existed, but with tiny guaranteed rate and distance. Using a cyclic encoding outside the binary CSS framework, the authors separate logical phase control from the design of rate and distance. An algebraic-geometric construction runs in polynomial time and guarantees 52/(675h) for both; a counting construction with exact verification improves this by about 2.86. A matching upper bound within the framework is about 1.28 times that, and they give explicit 48- and 90-qubit codes with addressable transversal T.

On the Hardness of Approximate Quantum Circuit Membership and Optimization

Kjelstrøm, van de Pol, Pavlogiannis2611.043813Wed 12:03●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Hardness of circuit optimization follows Monday’s NP-complete Clifford synthesis, which finished first, though a broad PP-hardness classification is a less quotable headline.

Janzing, Wocjan and Beth showed that deciding whether a circuit is close to the identity is QMA-complete under a promise. Without the promise, the authors show that deciding whether a Clifford+T circuit is ε-close to a member of S is PP-hard for wide ranges of ε, where S is {I}, a set of Clifford unitaries, or a set of generalized permutations, in trace, spectral or operator distance. For S = {I} with trace distances, hardness holds even for classical reversible gate sets. It follows that minimizing total, non-Clifford or superposition depth or count is PP-hard.

Fast verification of fault tolerant magic state preparation circuits with the CNOT + T gate set

Simsek, Macpherson, Self, Amaro (Quantinuum)2611.0490914Wed 12:03●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A public verification tool for magic-state circuits from Quantinuum, the tool type that has done well with us, though distance-5 examples limit its reach.

Checking that a non-Clifford preparation circuit is fault tolerant usually means enumerating fault combinations by hand or by brute force. Restricting to CNOT + T, the authors show that Pauli faults propagate with properties close to those in Clifford circuits, which lets their library Comb verify distance-3 and distance-5 magic-state preparation circuits in minutes on a laptop. Comb found a new distance-5 circuit, and circuit-level noisy simulations agree with its verdicts.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks, showing each event's next deadline: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
