Error correction · Helios

Quantinuum takes the color code well below threshold, and the argument turns to the clock

A suppression factor of 12 per distance step and 5×10−6 logical error per round at distance 7 on a commercial trapped-ion machine. The press release claims a 100× lead over superconductors; the analysts answered with the round time.

A. Lavasani, A. P. Reed, J. M. Dreiling, …, D. Hayes, T. S. Metodi, A. C. Potter (Quantinuum)arXiv:2610.2407770Tue 10:10

Quantinuum’s Helios, a 98-ion barium machine, ran triangular color codes at distances 3, 5 and 7 (13, 37 and 73 qubits including ancillas) for 20 rounds of syndrome extraction. Decoded offline with Tesseract, the logical error per round fell from 5.5×10−4 to 3.8×10−5 to 5×10−6, a suppression factor Λ = 12 (+3/−2) per distance step. Encoded two-qubit Clifford randomized benchmarking gave 7×10−4 per logical Clifford at d = 3 and 7×10−5 at d = 5, against 9.5×10−4 unencoded, with Λ = 9 (+8/−4). The scaling depends on leakage handling: a mid-circuit repump that runs in parallel with cooling and transport, plus a SWAP-based superdense extraction circuit, improve the per-round error by 4× at d = 3 and 8× at d = 5.

The press release calls it 99.9995% logical fidelity, ten times better than the company’s September result with its ten-qubit Helix code and a hundred times better than the best superconducting numbers, and says the accuracy is in the target range for Apollo, the fault-tolerant machine planned for 2029. Robin Blume-Kohout of Sandia, quoted in the release, puts a useful machine at under a million physical qubits. The paper is more careful: it says the results approach megaquop-scale error rates. The emulator error budget attributes 43% of 1/Λ to two-qubit gates, 36% to memory during transport, and 41% overall to leakage.

The reaction concentrated on time. Marin Ivezic’s analysis at PostQuantum argues that Λ, not the fidelity, is the number to plan with: at Λ = 12 a 10−12 target needs distance 21 and about 661 physical qubits per logical qubit, where Λ = 2 needs distance 53 and over 4,000. But a Helios round takes about 200 ms against 1.1 µs for a Willow cycle, and the same post reports that Craig Gidney of Google called the cycle “brutally slow” on X, after first quoting 20 ms. It also notes that the 30× overhead claim is measured against Google’s color code rather than its surface code. The d = 7 point rests on three failures in 29,000 shots, the real-time neural decoders were trained only on emulator data, and only Clifford circuits were run. Hacker News ignored the paper.

What to watch: closed-loop decoding with the streaming Frontier decoder or the 0.2–0.5 ms neural decoders, which the paper simulates round by round but does not run live; magic-state preparation that improves with distance, listed as future work; and whether Λ survives the longer transport paths of Sol, due in 2027. The same morning NVIDIA and Jülich reported the largest simulation of magic-state cultivation to date (below), and today’s listing carries a warning that correlated decoding can lose a code’s effective distance (paper #3).

Simulation

NVIDIA and Jülich simulate a 104-qubit magic-state cultivation protocol on JUPITER

NVIDIA CUDA-Q blog

Holmes, Kloss, Pancotti and Svore ran a state-vector simulation of magic-state cultivation, from a distance-3 color code with extra flag measurements to a distance-7 hybrid color/surface configuration, on 8,192 GH200 GPUs of Europe’s exascale machine. The model includes coherent phase errors and leakage, with some sites as three-level systems; a 45-bit encoded register holds 35.2 trillion amplitudes, 512 TiB in double precision. Packing qutrits into binary storage and reusing memory as qubits are measured made it fit. The press release says cultivation needs 90% fewer qubits than earlier methods; the blog’s stated goal is to find which hardware imperfections matter most for magic-state quality.

Edinburgh algorithm runs Gaussian boson sampling on ordinary computers

The Quantum Insider

A team from Edinburgh’s School of Informatics and EPCC, with Polytechnique Montréal, reports in Science Advances (10.1126/sciadv.aef8431) an algorithm that simulates Gaussian boson sampling with about 5% of the compute previously needed, and says classical machines running it matched or outperformed some current photonic devices on the task. Strong-to-weak spontaneous symmetry breaking transitions on a quantum processor

Z.-Y. Wei, C. Fechisin, Y.-X. Wang, J. Shah, T.-C. Lu, C.-J. Lin, A. V. Gorshkov (JQI/QuICS, Oklahoma, BlueQubit)2610.26229—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Mixed-state-phase papers reliably reach 15–30 scites, and this is the first hardware measurement of SW-SSB criticality, from a large JQI group with a 36-page paper.

Strong-to-weak symmetry breaking is diagnosed by quantities nonlinear in the density matrix, which is why it has stayed a theory subject. The authors prepare bond-dimension-two open-chain MPS with a parameter g interpolating from the cluster state through GHZ to a product state, on 6, 9 and 12 qubits of IBM Heron R3 processors, with up to 21 replicas per shot, then apply pairwise ZZ dephasing whose probability decays as a power law |i − j|−α (α = 1.4 in the main cut).

Two measurement settings suffice. From computational-basis shots, classical post-processing with the known channel kernel reconstructs the fidelity correlator, the Rényi-2 correlator and the linear correlator; a Hadamard test gives linear and Rényi-2 string order. At g = 0.4 the nonlinear correlators show finite-size crossings and scaling collapses across the three sizes while the linear correlator decays, with the fidelity transition point below the Rényi-2 one. They map pc across g and α, finding a non-monotonic dependence on α with a shift near α = 1. Rényi-2 string order survives below pc,II where linear string order dies, and its crossover lines up with the independently estimated transition, which they read as mixed-state SPT order. All numbers are raw: no error mitigation.

What it leaves open is what the channel-kernel post-processing costs at larger sizes, and whether a transition seen at N ≤ 12 under an engineered, known channel says anything about intrinsic hardware noise.

Correlated decoding loses the surface code’s effective distance below threshold

C. Hernando de la Fuente, J. Majaniemi, E. van Nieuwenburg, F. Frohnert (Leiden, Riverlane)2610.2678517Tue 10:10●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A short, sharp claim about the correlated mode of PyMatching and Riverlane’s decoder, with public code and a fix; decoder people will scite it on sight, and short abstracts with a surprise did well last week.

Two-pass correlated matching reweights one subgraph’s edges conditioned on the other’s first-pass correction. Under circuit-level noise a single first-pass edge can be correlated with several partner edges, and the standard update gives each of them an O(1) weight where an unconditioned prior costs log(1/p). The second pass then treats jointly discounted edges as nearly free even though explaining them together needs extra independent faults, so a logically wrong correction can look cheaper than the true one on configurations of (d − 1)/2 faults. The smallest example is a Pauli XY fault on a CZ at d = 3.

The numbers come from rotated planar memories at d = 3–9 under SI1000 noise, with p swept from 10−5 to 3×10−3. Fitted effective distances for two-pass matching are 1.28, 2.25 and 3.74 at d = 3, 5, 7 against the ideal 2, 3, 4; at d = 3 and p = 10−5 correlated matching is 4.4× worse than plain MWPM, and over 80% of its failures are syndromes the uncorrelated decoder gets right. Riverlane’s Local Clustering Decoder shows the same pattern. Scaling the discounted probabilities by a strength α(p), fitted per decoder, restores the nominal exponents and keeps the correlated advantage down to the lowest p sampled.

The fix is a one-parameter empirical regularization, not a derived optimum, and the LCD numbers cannot be reproduced outside Riverlane. The matching code and data are public. The acknowledgments credit Claude Code with help on derivations and analysis, verified by the authors.

A readout-aware theory of approximate Heisenberg propagation

Y. Bhandari, J. S. Kottmann, A. Bärligea (Augsburg, Duke)2610.26222—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A general error theory for Pauli propagation from Kottmann’s group, in the week classical-simulation papers average the most scites per paper.

Truncated Heisenberg methods discard operator components by magnitude, but the expectation-value error depends on how the discarded piece evolves afterwards and on the input state. The authors write mean values as wTetAs in an orthonormal Hermitian basis, so that Pauli propagation, Lie-algebraic simulation and fixed-generator Krylov methods are the same object with different represented spaces, and the sparsity of the adjoint matrix A defines a weighted commutator graph. Mean values are signed sums over walks from the observable’s support to the readout support.

Exact defect-transfer identities split the error into a source term and a time integral of propagated defects. The main theorem, delayed observability, assigns each basis operator a readout grade (for |0⟩⊗n, its number of X and Y factors) and shows that if one adjoint step lowers the grade by at most δ, the transfer moments of a defect vanish below order ⌈grade/δ⌉. That gives finite-order error estimates, state-dependent selection rules, and a proof that keeping only the symmetry-preserving part of a weak perturbation makes the first-order error vanish.

The numerics are small: six-qubit Ising and random circuits, twelve-qubit LMG and translation-invariant models. Corrected estimates reach 10−10 where the norm bound gives 10−1, but on depth-eight random circuits the estimator fails, and the correction beats the best uncorrected method at equal work in only 15–21% of ensemble instances, which the authors say plainly.

BQPcc is not contained in MAcc

D. Gavinsky2610.247421Tue 10:10●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A one-sentence abstract settling a communication-complexity separation from an established name; single-author proofs by theorists did well on Friday (Jain, 13), though this is a cs.CC cross-list.

The abstract is one line: a bipartite language with a polylogarithmic quantum communication protocol and polynomial Merlin–Arthur communication complexity. The language is the complement of Gavinsky’s Cheat-Shape function from his recent separation of quantum from randomized communication for total functions, which has a two-message protocol (one quantum message, one classical) of cost O(log3 N).

The lower bound follows Klauck’s rectangle-size method: amplify the verifier so that one proof covers a 2−a−1 fraction of the hard 0-distribution, observe that accepting transcripts form at most 2q′ disjoint rectangles, and apply a rectangle bound Λ0(R) ≤ A·Λ1(R) + η from the earlier paper. The arithmetic gives MA cost Ω(N1/12/log1/6 N), so the separation is polynomial against polylogarithmic. The paper has three sections and two references.

It is a clean addition to the communication-complexity zoo rather than a new technique, and its reception will depend on how widely the Cheat-Shape result has been read.

LiftDiff: guided discrete diffusion for quantum code construction

Y. Peled, E. Nachmani (Ben-Gurion)2610.252133Tue 10:10●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here AI-for-design has momentum after last week’s circuit stories and it claims codes that beat the gross code, but the authors are new to the field and most of the evidence is code-capacity.

A D3PM-style discrete diffusion model (about 10.7M parameters, bipartite cross-attention) generates base matrices over F2[Zℓ], expanded into lifted-product codes LP(M, M). Training data comes from a progressive-edge-growth sampler. At every reverse step the logits are pushed by the gradient of an energy that scores the implied code: an unrolled 20-iteration BP decoder with a cosine relaxation of parity, 4- and 6-cycle penalties, a spectral distance heuristic, a rank term and weight terms, with weights tuned per run. Candidates are screened by QDistRnd upper bounds and finalists get exact MILP distances.

The table lists 32 codes with n from 65 to 730 and d from 5 to 14, among them [[169,13,11]], [[143,15,9]], [[260,24,10]] and [[247,19,14]], the last with the highest kd2/n among check-weight-8 baselines. Under code-capacity X noise with BP-OSD, [[260,24,10]] has about 20× lower block error than [[144,12,12]] at p = 0.01. Under circuit-level noise with Relay-BP at p = 0.001–0.004, [[247,19,14]] sits below the gross code up to 0.003 and no failures were seen at 0.001, with a 200-failure stopping rule.

Only three codes get circuit-level numbers, the comparison is to one baseline, and no search cost is reported. The question for the field is whether a learned sampler finds anything that a direct search over the same small polynomial space would not.

Supervised learning with quantum Hamiltonian embeddings

R. Molteni, M. Jeon, P. Braccia, A. Deshpande, V. Dunjko, L. Jiang, …, K. Sharma (IBM Research, Leiden, Oxford, Chicago)2610.268335Tue 10:10●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A large IBM–Leiden–Chicago author list with Dunjko and Jiang, provable guarantees and a 50-qubit Heron run; a Leiden QML paper was yesterday’s sleeper at 13.

Each training pair contributes a term U†(x, α) O(y) U(x, α) to a data Hamiltonian, with label observables O(0) + O(1) = I and A = (1/n)ΣZi; the classifier is the ground state of this Hamiltonian, and a new input is labelled by which O(y) has the larger expectation after encoding. Training alternates ground-state approximation with Hellmann–Feynman parameter updates, so the gradient is a parameter-shift rule on a frozen state and never differentiates the ground state.

The guarantees are a matrix-Hoeffding generalization bound with sample complexity linear in qubits and parameters up to logs, a no-barren-plateau statement that a constant fraction of final-layer parameters keeps gradient variance Ω(1/n2) when the energy is bounded away from ½, and a cryptographic hardness construction (the discrete cube-root appendix) for hypothesis classes a classical learner cannot reach. On IBM Heron, with sample-based Krylov diagonalization as the solver, they run a 50-qubit binary Fashion-MNIST instance and report performance competitive with quantum kernels and classical baselines, without figures in the text we read.

The missing numbers are the accuracy and the cost of the ground-state step at scale, which is where such frameworks usually stall.

End-to-end optimization of partial fault tolerance in neutral-atom arrays

S. Veroni, A. Deger, A. J. Daley2610.264715Tue 10:10●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Daley’s group on the STAR architecture for ytterbium arrays with a Fermi–Hubbard target; architecture-plus-resource-estimate papers out-scite foundations work, though 42 pages is a slow read.

The STAR architecture saves overhead by relaxing fault tolerance for non-Clifford operations, which exposes them to hardware noise. The paper co-designs it with 171Yb Rydberg gates: at the physical level it identifies a favourable noise bias and sharpens it with error-selective pulse optimization; at the logical level it finds an intrinsic suppression that makes logical coherent noise vanish at leading order. Circuit-level simulations of surface-code implementations then give more than an order of magnitude lower logical error than previous estimates for the architecture.

The target is the Fermi–Hubbard model with periodic boundaries, with a simulation circuit built for early machines. Partial fault tolerance cuts state-injection depth by about an order of magnitude and Clifford resources by more, and the authors identify regimes where it gives both lower overhead and lower error than fully fault-tolerant synthesis, at p ~ 10−3 with a few thousand qubits.

The comparison with classical methods is the part to read critically: the claim is a pathway to early advantage on a specific model, not a demonstration.

Task-aligned spectral inductive biases in parameterized quantum circuits

F. Frohnert, A. Pérez-Salinas, S. Lange, M. Schwaebisch, E. van Nieuwenburg, V. Dunjko13final

A data re-uploading circuit normally takes one input, the sample. The authors add a second, a fixed spectral query defined by the task, and show that encoding the query changes how the circuit combines the signal frequencies it already has: the hypothesis class shifts without any new sample-dependent information entering. The natural fit is nonuniform Fourier-transform tasks, where the evaluation frequencies are the query and the sampling positions dictate how to encode it. They test on a classification task modelled on synthetic-aperture radar, where the acquisition geometry sets the query, and on small spectral and radar-like problems; aligned queries learn from less data than parameter-matched circuits with a mismatched or absent query. We skipped it as a QML paper. It drew 13 scites from a Leiden group whose names (Dunjko, van Nieuwenburg, Pérez-Salinas) carry weight on SciRate, a signal the forecast should have used.

Recursive operator localization and spectral gap descent via the geometry of local spectral expansion

R. Hayakawa10final

A 56-page single-author framework that builds hierarchies of local energy problems linked by linear maps between Hilbert spaces, indexed by a weighted simplicial complex. Spectral gaps of auxiliary classical random walks on the links propagate geometric estimates up the hierarchy, so local gaps bound the global operator’s gap. Two sanity checks anchor it: for scalar functions on facets the decomposition reduces to the law of total variance and recovers the Alev–Lau bounds from local link expansion; for positive binary spin distributions, conditional influence captures subspace overlap and recovers the heat-bath gap bounds from spectral independence. The stated target is quantum Gibbs samplers, where the missing ingredient is model-specific geometric estimates. We listed it in “also” with one line. Ten scites for a long, abstract paper suggests the Gibbs-sampling community is reading anything that promises gap bounds.

FlipFlop Tuoyuan (Beijing, founded July 2026) emerged from stealth with seed and angel rounds worth hundreds of millions of RMB from Source Code Capital, Oasis Capital and CICC Capital; its CEO Qiao Mu did a PhD with Kihwan Kim and a postdoc with Antoine Browaeys. Both name 100 logical qubits as the medium-term goal.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under the live SciRate box, so pasted counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.

Industry, deadlines, conferences

Industry & Startups covers company and funding news that matters but is not front-page material; minor items become one-line briefs, and quiet days stay short.

Deadline watch counts down the days to submission and registration deadlines in the next eight weeks: filled red means less than a week, tinted less than a month. Conferences lists upcoming meetings; the list is checked every week.
