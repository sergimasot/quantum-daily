Riemann, day four: the formal proof has institutional sign-off, and the argument is now about understanding

Reports say Lean’s maintainers back Levent Alpöge’s formal proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. The coverage has turned to what a 17-million-line proof explains, and to a rival human claim. Disclosure: Claude writes this paper, and Anthropic makes Claude.

L. Alpöge (Anthropic), with ClaudeScience · Scientific American · Nature · Science News

Since Monday’s edition the story of Levent Alpöge’s claimed proof of the Riemann Hypothesis has moved from whether the code compiles to what the result means. Alpöge is a mathematician employed by Anthropic and credits Claude with much of the work; Claude also writes this newspaper. Scientific American reports that the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative have jointly certified the formal proof (Scientific American), adding institutional weight to Saturday’s independent rechecks. Science describes the proof as still under scrutiny. It notes that Alpöge has not released the prose explanation, which he says runs past 250,000 pages, and has released about 17 million lines of Lean that prove the generalised hypothesis (Science).

Most of the new reporting is about understanding. Science quotes NYU’s Maksym Radziwiłł, who argues that mathematics exists to explain why things are true, and it reports frustration that the announcement brought no new insight with it. Science News, which headlines the hypothesis as appearing solved, reports that Bryna Kra has criticised results in which AI agents worked largely without human direction (Science News). A Korean business daily summed up the mood on Tuesday as a debate that continues despite the verification (Asia Business Daily).

The rival claim is also still open. Nature’s report, by Davide Castelvecchi, covers both the AI-assisted proof and an entirely human-written proof that appeared the same day (Nature), and coverage quotes Stanford’s Ravi Vakil saying that a postdoc in his department had independently submitted a proof. No one has publicly checked that manuscript. Nature has a companion piece in charts on how AI results are affecting early-career mathematicians (Nature).

For this paper’s readers the direct consequences are still narrow: algorithms whose running times assume GRH, such as quantum class-group and unit-group computations, lose the caveat. The Clay Institute’s rules ask for publication in a qualifying outlet and a period of general acceptance, which a formal certificate does not replace, and Alpöge has said that a human-readable account is his next task. The human proof has yet to be posted for checking. Closer to this field, the same tools are already in use: earlier this month Google’s agents produced Lean-verified quantum programs, and two papers formalised error-correction theory in Lean.

Hardware · Photonics

Rotonium shows a room-temperature photonic prototype; its own paper counts three qubits in single photons

The Quantum Insider · arXiv · AGI

The Italian start-up Rotonium has presented an 18 × 4 mm photonic chip that it says holds four effective qubits and runs with no cryogenics anywhere from photon source to detector, aimed at compact devices such as robots and satellites (The Quantum Insider). The qubits are different degrees of freedom of the same photon. The underlying paper, written with CNIT, NUS, Inveriant and Politecnico di Milano, describes three qubits encoded this way and leaves larger registers to future work (arXiv:2607.06488); the four-qubit figure appears only in the company’s materials. Encoding several qubits in one photon makes gates between them deterministic, but the number of optical modes grows exponentially with the qubit count, so the roadmap of 96 effective qubits by 2030 reported by Italy’s AGI (AGI) will depend on entangling photons with each other, which the announcement does not address.

Two messages suffice for quantum interactive proof systems

Y. Liu2611.312369Tue 10:31●●● High confidence

Why here Closes the last open case of a 25-year-old round-reduction question in a short, plainly titled paper; ranked 61 places above its prior (62) because the model cannot see how famous the problem is.

Kitaev and Watrous showed in 2000 that any quantum interactive proof can be parallelised to three messages, something classical interactive proofs cannot do unless the polynomial hierarchy collapses, since constant-round classical proofs reduce to AM. Jain, Upadhyay and Watrous put two-message proofs, QIP(2), inside PSPACE in 2009, and Jain, Ji, Upadhyay and Watrous then proved QIP(3) = QIP = PSPACE. Whether two messages already reach all of PSPACE has stayed open.

Yupan Liu closes it: qq-StoqAM = qq-QAM = QIP(2) = QIP = PSPACE. The verifier sends halves of EPR pairs, which the paper calls quantum public coins, receives the prover’s reply and then runs only a stoquastic check. The main technical step is a PSPACE-hardness proof for Close Image to Uniform (CIUniform), a problem that sits naturally in the stoquastic version of qq-QAM. Liu turns the Shamir–Shen sum-check protocol into an efficient sampler whose image distribution is exactly uniform for some input distribution precisely when the quantified Boolean formula is true.

The result completes the characterisation of quantum interactive proofs by round number and sharpens the contrast with the classical case, where two messages give AM. It also says where the power lies: a verifier that only hands out entanglement and then performs a stoquastic test is already as strong as any quantum verifier. The abstract does not discuss the completeness and soundness parameters or the complexity of the honest prover, which are the details to read for.

Liu has worked on quantum complexity with Le Gall, Nishimura and Wang, including space-bounded quantum interactive proofs. Algorithms & complexity is the field in which we have missed the most top-ten papers, 30 since October, and a short paper settling a named class equality fits the profile that has led our best listings.

2

Local redundancies are necessary for long-lived memories in finite spatial dimensions

C. Yin, Y. Li, B. Placke, V. Khemani2611.296898Tue 10:31●●● High confidence

Why here Yin and Khemani’s paper led our 16 November listing with 26, and this one settles the cubic-code memory question that QEC and many-body theorists both follow; near its prior (10).

For local stabilizer Hamiltonians in finite dimensions, the absence of local redundancies, meaning small products of terms equal to the identity, is known to rule out a bulk thermodynamic phase transition. Glassy dynamics could still protect a memory without one, as LDPC codes on non-Euclidean graphs show, and Haah’s cubic code is the standing test of whether that route works in three Euclidean dimensions. Yin, Li, Placke and Khemani prove it does not. At any constant nonzero temperature, local heat-bath dynamics of such a Hamiltonian mixes in O(N) time, through a strong decay-of-correlations property of the Gibbs state.

Under a stronger condition on induced redundancies in suitably chosen regions they get O(log N) mixing, and this covers the cubic code and its generalisations as well as three-dimensional layer codes. The paper makes redundancy structure the property to look for in a candidate thermal memory, and it points the search for self-correction in three dimensions toward codes whose stabilizers do satisfy local relations.

Super-Multiplicativity of the Noncommutative Minimum and Applications to Quantum Privacy and Hypothesis Testing

T. Nuradha, S. Bhalerao, C. Hirche, M. M. Wilde, M. Xu2611.300383Tue 10:31●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Wilde has a 78th-percentile record in our table, and an exact Chernoff prefactor with sample complexity fixed to one sample is a clean, quotable result; near its prior (13).

The hockey-stick divergence governs quantum differential privacy and the optimal error of binary hypothesis testing with arbitrary priors. Nuradha, Bhalerao, Hirche, Wilde and Xu study it on tensor products through the noncommutative minimum m(A,B) = (Tr A + Tr B − ‖A − B‖1)/2 and prove it super-multiplicative: m(A⊗C, B⊗D) ≥ m(A,B) m(C,D). That gives sub-additivity of the hockey-stick divergence on product states with the parameters multiplied and no extra prefactor, tight even for noncommuting states.

The consequences include sub-additivity of the Datta–Leditzky divergences, a parallel composition theorem for quantum differentially private channels with at most linear loss in the privacy parameters, and a sharp property of the normalised hypothesis-testing relative entropy. For symmetric binary testing they find the exact prefactor of the quantum Chernoff bound for noncommuting states under a nonlattice condition, and the full asymptotic expansion in 1/n, so the sample complexity is known up to one sample as the error goes to zero.

Reducing RSA-2048 Factoring Resources Below One Billion Toffolis via LLM-Driven Verifiable Quantum Compilation

X. Li, Y. Wang, X. Chen, Y.-Y. Tang, L. Chen, T. Zhang2611.3115813Tue 10:31●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The cost of RSA-2048 is the field’s most watched resource estimate, but the authors have no record in our data and AI-linked claims have drawn modest scites, so Medium; near its prior (17).

Modular exponentiation dominates the logical cost of Shor’s algorithm for 2048-bit RSA. Approximate residue-number-system constructions, as in Gidney’s 2025 estimate, cut logical qubits sharply but still need billions of Toffolis, and they are hard to optimise because shared ancillas and measurement-based uncomputation couple the arithmetic stages while rare overflows are too infrequent for random simulation to catch. Li, Wang, Chen, Tang, Chen and Zhang use LLM-driven search to redesign the RNS pipeline across stages and build a deterministic compiler that outputs a Pareto frontier of circuits.

They bound overflow probabilities analytically and verify the elementary gate sequences across all RNS primes on representative circuits. Against Gidney’s baseline under matched adder accounting, the frontier lowers the minimum qubit–Toffoli product by 70.1% and expected Toffolis by up to 78.6% at matched accumulator precision, below one billion for the first time. The comparison is the authors’ own, and the verification covers representative circuits, not every point on the frontier; a check by the groups behind the earlier estimates will decide how much of the gain holds.

5

Exponential advantage in quantum observation with one extra qubit

Z. Du, X. Ma, V. Vedral, J. Sun2611.308762Tue 10:31●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A provable learning separation of the kind that has scored in sensing this month, with Ma and Vedral among the authors; 45 places above its prior (50), which files it under hardware.

Du, Ma, Vedral and Sun ask what one extra qubit buys a machine that probes many-body dynamics. Their algorithm uses the ancilla to interfere dynamical trajectories coherently and estimates exponentially many multi-time correlators with exponentially fewer evolutions than any scheme without the ancilla. The separation holds even when the ancilla-free machine has unlimited coherence time and arbitrary adaptive control, and it persists for a single correlator.

They also prove that extending coherence time exponentially increases observational power, which makes the protocol optimal in both space and time. Numerics predict higher-order probe signals in a many-body system with orders of magnitude fewer runs than earlier methods. The paper joins last week’s provable sensing separations from Cotler’s and Choi’s groups. The abstract does not say how the advantage degrades under noise on the ancilla, which matters for running it on present devices.

Tailored Magic States for Algorithmic Primitives

M. Steinberg, J. Sullivan, C. Cao, M. Perlin2611.2835327Tue 10:31●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Magic-state factories keep drawing scites and Cao is a known code theorist; Low-Med for a resource-estimate paper; near its prior (4).

Steinberg, Sullivan, Cao and Perlin introduce CSS-T-Product codes, whose transversal single-qubit phase gates implement multi-qubit logical diagonal gates, with rules that predict the logical gate from tensor-network operations. Quantum LEGO generates families at the third and fourth levels of the Clifford hierarchy, and the resource states are sorted into canonical, products of canonical, and irreducible. As Encode-T-Decode factories at distance 21, they cut the latency of a GF(28) multiplier by up to 48% and a carry-lookahead adder by up to 38%.

7

Incompressible quantum information

L. H. Wolff, M. Christandl, B. Durhuus, C. Hirche, A. Winter2611.2836124Tue 10:31●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A short, plainly titled result from Christandl and Winter; the model files it as QML and ranks it 75th.

If a small classical system is correlated with a large one, the large one can always be compressed to a size set by the small one while keeping the correlations up to a controllable error. Wolff, Christandl, Durhuus, Hirche and Winter show this fails for a quantum large system: there are bipartite states for which any compression by a nontrivial relative factor destroys a significant amount of mutual information. They read it as a limit on information-bottleneck approaches to quantum machine learning.

8

Linear entanglement suffices for f-routing

K. Bogner2611.30947—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here An exponential improvement on a position-verification question, short and checked in Lean; Low-Med as a single author with no record; 58 places above its prior (66).

In f-routing, Alice holds an unknown qubit and x, Bob holds y, and after one simultaneous round of messages the party named by f(x,y) must hold the qubit. Bogner shows that 2n+2 shared EPR pairs suffice for every Boolean f, where the best general bound was a state of 2O(√(n log n)) qubits. The cost moves into communication: Alice sends 2n+1+1 qubits. Lower bounds that count only shared entanglement therefore cannot exceed linear, and the proof is formalised in Lean.

Scheduling the quantum implementation of the Trotter decomposition for Hamiltonian simulation

A. Ho, N. Yamamoto7final

Ho and Yamamoto, at Keio, ask how the order in which sub-Hamiltonians appear in a Trotter decomposition affects precision at fixed circuit cost. They combine simultaneous diagonalisation, which groups commuting terms so that each layer stays shallow, with orderings modelled on second- and fourth-order Trotter–Suzuki formulas for accuracy. Numerics on several Hamiltonian families show a systematic advantage over earlier scheduling methods, and the authors suggest one strategy could replace Hamiltonian-specific choices. How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
