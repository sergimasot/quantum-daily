NIST ties a location to a loophole-free Bell test

Kavuri, Shalm and colleagues certify that an untrusted device sits between two verifier stations 195 m apart, against attackers who share no entanglement or only a sliver of it. NIST says practical use is more than a decade away.

Kavuri, …, Glancy, Knill, Shalm (NIST and collaborators)Nature · NIST · arXiv

Nature published on 18 November what NIST calls the first complete demonstration of quantum position verification (Nature; preprint arXiv:2601.16892). On the Boulder campus, two verifier stations 195.1 m apart send random challenge bits timed to reach the prover’s claimed location, 92.8 m from one of them, at the same moment. The prover uses the bits to choose polarisation measurements on one photon of an entangled pair and sends the outcomes back. An instance passes only if the round trips are fast enough and the outcomes violate a Bell inequality, with detection efficiency above 81% so that no fair-sampling assumption is needed. At about 250,000 trials per second, a 30-million-trial instance takes two minutes; 224 of 232 basic instances and 102 of 103 extended ones passed, each at soundness 2−64 (NIST).

Buhrman and coauthors showed in 2014 that position verification cannot be unconditionally secure against attackers who pre-share enough entanglement, so every protocol is secure only relative to some bound on that resource. Here the basic protocol holds against attackers with no prior entanglement and unlimited classical and quantum communication. The extended one tolerates an average robustness of entanglement of 8×10−6 per trial, well below the 2×10−3 that the source itself produces; the authors note that a garden-hose attack using two such pairs would break it, and that l-bit challenges raise an attacker’s requirement to about log(l)/2 qubits. Against a classical protocol with the same latencies, the certified region is 4.5 times smaller in volume. The protocol is device-independent, so nothing about the prover’s hardware is trusted, though the verifier labs and their classical electronics must be.

We found no comment from researchers outside the team. The Quantum Insider and NIST’s own release repeat the lab’s list of uses, from guarding medical records to blocking remote commands to power grids, and Science News covered the preprint in March, when Abigail Gookin presented it at the APS summit, without quoting outside experts (Science News). Aliro’s white paper on the topic names the standard objection to QPV, attacks by colluding parties with large stores of pre-shared entanglement, and argues such resources are out of reach today (Aliro). The NIST release drew eight points on Hacker News. NIST itself says real use needs entanglement-distribution networks that do not exist yet.

What to watch: longer challenges (the authors call 20-bit challenges feasible) to raise the entanglement an attacker needs; faster Pockels cells, which the authors estimate could cut an instance to about a second; low-latency electronics below 30 ns to shrink the certified region; a test over deployed fibre; and theory on how much entanglement attacks on Bell-test QPV really require, the question today’s Hasegawa–May paper on non-local computation also touches.

Roadmaps · Cat qubits

Alice & Bob doubles its 2030 target to 200 logical qubits

Quantum Computing Report · PostQuantum

Alice & Bob’s new roadmap puts Carbon in 2030: 200 logical qubits from 3,600 physical cat qubits in nine dilution refrigerators, joined by cryogenic coaxial links that exchange Bell pairs, with degree-five connectivity, hybrid LDPC and repetition codes, a 10 µs cycle, 10 million logical operations and a target logical error rate of 10−7 (Quantum Computing Report). Before that comes Beryllium in 2028 with six logical qubits and a universal fault-tolerant gate set; after it, Oxygen (1,024 logical qubits, 2032) and Neon (2,048, 2034), which the company lists for RSA-2048 “stress testing”. It credits AI-assisted chip design and algorithm work with a 40-fold cut in its RSA-2048 resource estimate. The December 2024 roadmap promised 100 logical qubits by 2030. All figures are company targets, and the report adds no analysis; PostQuantum’s profile notes the company has published several different sets of numbers for its 100-logical-qubit machine and has revised its timing before (PostQuantum).

Analogue simulation · Nature

QuEra and Berkeley Lab report a dynamical phase transition with no equilibrium counterpart on up to 180 atoms

The Quantum Insider · Nature

Researchers from QuEra, NERSC at Berkeley Lab, Harvard and Boston University, Mikhail Lukin among them, report in Nature on quench dynamics on QuEra’s Aquila, accessed through Amazon Braket, in chains of up to 52 atoms and 2D arrays of up to 180 (The Quantum Insider; preprint arXiv:2510.11706). Prethermal patterns persist far longer than expected and melt at sharp resonances that the team explains quantitatively. In 2D the response sharpens with array size, which the authors identify as a first-order dynamical phase transition with no equilibrium counterpart. Exact simulations on NERSC’s Perlmutter checked the device only at small sizes, so the large-array conclusions rest on trusting the analogue hardware where nothing classical can compare.

Fault-tolerant quantum processor with minimal external control

Pattison, Haah, Preskill2611.23919—scites●●● High confidence

Why here A threshold theorem from Haah and Preskill that rebuilds fault tolerance around a fixed, clocked chip fed by a handful of wires: famous names and a new question, and the model agrees (prior 4).

Every qubit in today’s solid-state processors has its own control line, and error correction adds a stream of syndrome data sent off the chip. Pattison, Haah and Preskill ask how little external control a fault-tolerant quantum computer needs, and take classical processors as the model: billions of transistors steered through few wires because data moves through hard-wired operations in step with a clock.

Their architecture is a planar array in which every qubit, apart from a few attached to external wires, undergoes the same geometrically local depth-3 circuit, applied periodically and fixed when the device is fabricated. Classical instructions arrive as a stream through a constant number of wires, each attached to a single qubit, and quantum information flows through the clocked array. Assuming a noiseless global clock, so that every operation happens on schedule though imperfectly, they prove a threshold theorem: if the noise on every gate, measurement and wire is below a universal threshold, a chip with n physical qubits runs any algorithm using n/polylog(n/ε) logical qubits, where ε is the error rate per logical gate.

Each logical gate takes n/polylog(n/ε) steps, since instructions have to travel across the chip. A second design keeps quantum operations local and noisy but adds on-chip wires that broadcast noiseless classical signals instantly, which brings the time per logical gate down to polylog(n/ε).

The paper turns control wiring, usually treated as an engineering problem, into a question for threshold theorems. What it leaves open is mostly in the assumptions: a noiseless global clock, noiseless broadcast in the faster design, and a gate time linear in n in the strict one. The abstract gives no numerical threshold. A reader’s guide sits at the end of Section 1.

A provably efficient classical algorithm for simulating OTOC(1) in 1D

Lewis, Mele, de Wolf, McClean, Abbas2611.24640—scites●●● High confidence

Why here The first polynomial-time algorithm with guarantees for first-order OTOCs, with McClean and de Wolf among the authors, a day after Google theorists’ heuristic version; 24 places above its prior (26) because OTOC-based advantage claims have the field’s attention.

Higher-order OTOCs in random circuits have been proposed as a route to quantum advantage, and for first-order OTOCs the numerical evidence pointed to classical tractability without a proof. Lewis, Mele, de Wolf, McClean and Abbas give a provably polynomial-time algorithm for first-order, infinite-temperature OTOCs in 1D Haar-random brickwork circuits at any depth. Window propagation evolves the two-copy operator B⊗B backward through the circuit one layer at a time. After each layer it drops terms whose non-invariant support spans more than k sites, then projects onto operators that are the identity to the left of a window of width 3k and the swap to its right, the shape of the averaged dynamics, keeping circuit-specific corrections inside the window.

With k = O(log(d/ε)) the deterministic runtime is poly(n, d, 1/ε) and the mean-squared error over the ensemble is at most ε; the result also holds when the gates form a 4-design. Numerics match exact values on up to 512 qubits for Clifford circuits with 768 T gates and on 22 qubits for Haar circuits, with observed wall-clock scaling near n2.2. The authors stress that second-order OTOCs in 2D, the setting of current advantage proposals, are out of reach of this method.

The limits of magic-state cultivation

Balada Gaggioli, Mareček2611.24852—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A plainly titled stabilizer result claiming that the S-gate stand-in used to design cultivation overstates its fault distance, read the day after Q-CTRL’s cultivation record; Medium because the authors have no track record in our data.

Fault tolerance is analysed with Pauli algebra, which breaks down at non-Clifford gates, so magic-state cultivation has been designed and benchmarked with S gates standing in for its T gates. Balada Gaggioli and Mareček give an exact theory of harmful faults for any circuit that prepares a magic state with T gates and postselection: one stabilizer code on the non-Clifford gates decides whether a fault is harmless. This places cultivation and distillation in one framework and yields a T-count lower bound, at least max(2d, d+7) T gates for fault distance d ≥ 2, so nine for fault distance two.

The S proxy can only overstate a check’s fault distance, by any amount, because S turns an X error into a detectable Y while T leaves the bare X in superposition. In their simulations the published distance-3 and distance-5 cultivation circuits have fault distances 2 and 3, against 3 and 4 for their proxies, and checks that measure logical X through a CNOT-tree GHZ fold keep at most about half the code distance. Their alternative grows a cat state from its middle and verifies it by the parity of its ends; on a square grid it keeps the full distance, certified up to distance 9 with 151 qubits. The abstract does not say how far this moves cultivation’s simulated logical error rates.

4

Addressable transversal CCZ gates on almost good quantum LDPC codes and nearly optimal magic state protocols

S. J. S. Tan, G. Zhu2611.23762—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here The model’s top pick, addressable transversal CCZ on nearly good qLDPC codes; held at Medium because it runs to 69 pages plus appendices and rests on an expansion hypothesis left to a companion paper.

Cheap, addressable non-Clifford gates are what qLDPC codes lack for low-overhead magic. Tan and Zhu construct qLDPC codes with distance Ωε(N/log2N) and at least N1−ε individually addressable logical CCZ gates on disjoint triples of logical qubits, for any fixed 0 < ε < 1. With the logical qubits outside these triples set to |0⟩, any subset of the CCZs is implemented by a transversal physical CCZ circuit. Two applications follow: CCZ distillation with overhead exponent γ → 0 and nearly linear error suppression, and a magic-state fountain that prepares N1−ε encoded CCZ states with nearly linear distance.

The codes are balanced products of expanding graph covers built to share many classical codewords, whose pairings select the CCZs independently. The construction assumes uniform subfield product expansion for Reed–Solomon codes over three characteristic-two subfields with pairwise coprime extension degrees, studied in a companion manuscript. Without that assumption the covers still give classical LDPC codes over a fixed field with almost linear dimension and linear distance, in which the coordinatewise product of any three codewords lies in a second LDPC code of linear distance.

Scrambling leads to entanglement cost in NLQC

Hasegawa, May2611.23378—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Settles a Cleve–May conjecture with the first faithful lower bound on entanglement cost in non-local computation, a short theory result of the kind we keep underrating; 23 places above its prior (28).

Non-local quantum computation implements a joint channel using shared entanglement and a single simultaneous round of quantum communication. It appears in attacks on position verification and in holography, and most known lower bounds on its entanglement cost apply only to specially built examples. Hasegawa and May prove that a unitary has zero entanglement cost if and only if it does not scramble, and that the tripartite mutual information, a standard measure of scrambling, gives a quantitative lower bound on the cost.

This is the first faithful lower bound for unitaries: it vanishes only on unitaries that are free. From it they show that the controllable correlation, an earlier candidate, is also faithful, which settles a conjecture of Cleve and May. Since attacks on position verification are non-local computations, bounds of this kind tell protocol designers which tasks force an attacker to hold entanglement, the assumption at the centre of today’s NIST experiment.

6

Separating QMA from the QCMA Hierarchy via a Switching Lemma for Polynomials

Y. Wang2611.23693—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A single-author oracle separation of QMA from the whole QCMA hierarchy, the kind of short complexity result our scorecard misses most (21 missed top-ten papers in algorithms); 33 places above its prior.

Yiming Wang builds a classical oracle relative to which QMA is not contained in any level of the QCMA hierarchy. The tool is a switching lemma for bounded polynomials whose parameters do not depend on the number of input variables, which gives a new view of earlier sampler-based QMA–QCMA separations. The construction uses the code intersection subset size problem of Bostanci, Huang and Vaikuntanathan (FOCS 2026). Two more oracles go the other way: one where QCMA collapses to P but QMA does not, and one where QMA collapses to P but PP does not.

High-rate low-distance color codes

Derks, Berent, Webster, Wille, Eisert, Kubica2611.23420—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Small high-rate colour codes with proved optimal rates and break-even numbers for trapped ions, from a well-known group (prior 6); below the theory above because the gains are for near-term codes.

Derks, Berent, Webster, Wille, Eisert and Kubica design 2D colour codes with several logical qubits: distance 3 at rates up to 1/3 and distance 4 at rates up to 1/4, and prove these rates optimal for colour codes with only colour boundaries on surfaces of non-negative Euler characteristic. In simulations of Steane-style correction with real-time feedback and no postselection on a linear trapped-ion chain with all-to-all connectivity, the [[20,4,4]] and [[16,4,4]] codes break even at two-qubit gate infidelities of 0.22% and 0.26%, matching Shor-style correction on bivariate-bicycle codes with weight-5 measurements.

Quantum Simulation of First-Quantised Chemistry with a Sum-of-Squares Representation

Ivanov, Sünderhauf, Kunjummen, Blunt, Izsák2611.24885—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Compiled Toffoli and qubit counts against the best second-quantised method, the kind of explicit resource estimate that has placed well as a sleeper (prior 3); Low-Med because the theory above it is stronger.

Ivanov, Sünderhauf, Kunjummen, Blunt and Izsák write a first-quantised phase-estimation algorithm for generic orbital basis sets, using a non-convex sum-of-squares form of the Hamiltonian and spectral-gap amplification, and compile it with the same logical-level techniques as the leading second-quantised algorithm. For six molecules with 4 to 24 electrons in 5 to 14 times as many orbitals, it needs up to 16 times fewer Toffoli gates when only a few hundred logical qubits are available, stays cheaper up to several hundred to about 1,400 qubits, and has a minimum qubit count 1.4 to 2 times lower.

How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Click a count to open the paper on SciRate.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. We said is the rank we had forecast (— if we didn't rank it). The scorecard counts how many of our top five made the real top five and explains the misses. Worth a second look summarises high scorers we missed or underrated. If no counts are available yet, the section lists the previous picks instead (Yesterday's Picks, Revisited).

On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
