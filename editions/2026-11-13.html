Funding · Software

Q-CTRL raises $290 million at a valuation above $1 billion

Salesforce Ventures led the Sydney company’s Series C, which Forbes calls the largest yet for a quantum software firm. AMD Ventures took part on the same day that AMD said it would invest in PsiQuantum.

Q-CTRL (company announcement)Bloomberg · Capital Brief · Quantum Computing Report

Q-CTRL, the Sydney company that sells error-suppression software for quantum computers and quantum navigation sensors, has closed a $290 million Series C (about A$415 million) that values it at more than $1 billion, Bloomberg reported on 12 November. Salesforce Ventures led the round. New investors include Jared Kushner’s Affinity Partners, AMD Ventures, the UAE defence group EDGE, MPower Partners, SPARX and Australia’s National Reconstruction Fund, which put in A$70 million according to Capital Brief. In-Q-Tel and Lockheed Martin Ventures returned. The company says the round was oversubscribed and expanded twice. Neither revenue nor a planned use of the money was disclosed.

Q-CTRL has two lines of business. Fire Opal and its other software suppress errors on cloud hardware, mostly IBM’s; this week it put a dynamics simulator on IBM Quantum Functions and reported a 12× speedup on Nighthawk. Ironstone Opal is a magnetic navigation system that works without GPS, with trials for Airbus, DARPA and the Australian and British navies, and much of the defence money follows that product. Forbes calls the Series C the largest round yet for a software-focused quantum company. The same day, PsiQuantum and AMD widened their partnership with a planned AMD equity stake of undisclosed size. PsiQuantum’s PRISM decoder, which led SciRate on Wednesday, runs on AMD Versal chips.

We found no comment from researchers outside the company, and no Hacker News thread above our threshold. The coverage so far repeats the company’s figures. The headline on the Forbes piece says Q-CTRL considers quantum advantage already here. The company’s earlier advantage claims rested on a 2025 navigation field trial and a May 2026 Fermi–Hubbard run on IBM hardware, and neither has been tested in public by an independent classical-simulation group that we know of. Most large rounds this year have gone to hardware makers, among them OQC, whose £260 million Series C in June was the largest for a European quantum computing company (Fortune).

What to watch: whether Q-CTRL says how the money splits between computing software and sensing; what AMD Ventures’ stakes in two quantum companies within a week amount to, and whether AMD and PsiQuantum give the promised technical detail at SC26 in Chicago next week; and whether Q-CTRL publishes the benchmarks behind its advantage claims in a form that classical-simulation groups can test.

Hardware · IBM

IBM’s Starling progress report puts its chip links at 1% average error, ten times the target

IBM Research blog

A day after the Nighthawk r3 announcement, Matthias Steffen and Matthew Marwick posted a progress report on Starling, the fault-tolerant machine IBM plans for 2029 (100 million operations on 200 logical qubits). Two Nighthawk chips joined by four l-couplers reached 1% average error, best 0.7%; Starling needs more than 100 l-couplers per module at 0.1%. The revised bivariate bicycle code needs five connections per qubit instead of six, and IBM says it gives 20–100× lower logical error. Fewer Josephson junctions lifted the share of fully working Loon processors from about 1 in 12 to 1 in 3. IBM says Relay-BP already meets Starling’s decoding requirement and that a real-time decoder ASIC is being designed on a 2 nm process. Starling may need up to twelve linked cryostats. These are all IBM’s own figures, and the post, like Wednesday’s release, does not mention Kookaburra.

Chemistry · Hybrid computing

IBM partners report chemical accuracy at 80 qubits and a 12,000-atom binding calculation

Quantum Computing Report

Two IBM-partner chemistry results came out on 12 November. Qunova reports an extrapolated ground-state energy of −327.2482 ± 0.0007 Ha for the [4Fe-4S] cubane on 80 qubits, 1.1 mHa from the DMRG reference, using its HI-VQE method with 2,518 Fugaku node-hours (arXiv:2601.06935). Cleveland Clinic, RIKEN and IBM report embedded sample-based diagonalization of solvated protein–ligand complexes of up to 12,635 atoms, with up to 94 active qubits on two Heron r2 chips and 239 QPU hours (arXiv:2605.01138). In both cases the heavy lifting is classical, the agreement is with classical references rather than experiment, and the Cleveland binding energy uses a minimal basis.

CSS Circuits: useful quantum circuits that can be simulated with $O(1)$ cost per gate

C. Gidney, C. Jones (Google Quantum AI)2611.1494410Fri 10:20●●● High confidence

Why here A short, plainly titled simulation result from Gidney: papers like this have beaten our longer picks on three recent listings.

Gidney and Jones isolate the gate set {X, Z, MX, MZ, CNOT} and show that circuits built from it, which they call CSS circuits, can be simulated with constant work per gate. The class is narrow but covers teleportation, CSS-code error correction and entanglement purification. Stim-style Pauli-frame (“flip”) simulation is already O(1) per gate but needs a fixed reference sample and cannot handle errors that are themselves Clifford operations, such as an atom vanishing mid-circuit. Tableau simulators handle those but cost Ω(n2) per gate.

The simulator stores two bits per qubit, xq and zq, for whether the qubit is bit-flipped or phase-flipped relative to the all-zero reference. CNOT XORs xc into xt and zt into zc; MZ reports x and re-randomises z, and MX the reverse. This works because every CSS circuit is degenerate in their sense: the all-zero outcome record is always possible, so the reference never has to be computed, even with adaptive or probabilistic gates. The whole simulator fits in a short code listing.

The two bits per qubit fix every measurement result locally, so the simulator is a local hidden-variable model. The authors use this to show it cannot be extended: they give CSS+H, CSS+S and CSS+CZ circuits that win the GHZ game every time, which no local model can do. This rules out only simulators of this local form, not every O(1) scheme for Clifford circuits, and the paper says as much.

The proposed use is benchmarking QEC under atom loss. Loss produces Clifford errors that a Pauli frame cannot follow, while the CSS simulator keeps O(1) cost per gate with them, so loss-aware sampling of CSS-code memories could become cheap. The paper only suggests this and gives no numbers.

Scalable Pauli propagation through $\mathbb{F}_2$-linear partitioning

A. Orfi, A. Kille, J. C. Peacock, J. Dunham, L. Devos (Flatiron, NYU, Columbia)2611.172620Fri 10:20●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A practical tool that lifts a known bottleneck, with public code; we rank it 23 places above the prior because classical-simulation tools have drawn about 10 scites per paper.

Pauli propagation is limited by memory, not arithmetic: every gate must be followed by merging duplicate strings, and once the operator is spread over many nodes that step needs global synchronisation. The Flatiron group partitions strings by a linear hash h(p) = Mp over F2 of the 2L-bit symplectic vector. Because the hash is linear, multiplying by a fixed Pauli shifts a whole partition to one predetermined partner, so a k-qubit channel couples each partition to at most 4k others and a Pauli rotation to one. Gate application then splits into independent tasks with bounded communication.

Their Rust implementation, paulistrings-rs, runs a 16×16 transverse-field Ising quench on 32 nodes (96 cores and 1.47 TiB each) with budgets up to 1.6 × 1011 strings, at 68–74% weak-scaling efficiency. On a single node, kicked-Ising runs stay near ideal up to 16 threads and drop to 38–61% at 96. Hashes tailored to the circuit beat random ones by up to a factor of two on 64 ranks.

The paper is about engineering and makes no new accuracy claims. Truncation error is controlled as usual by a coefficient threshold.

Learning Sparse Graph States with Local Pauli Measurements: Tight Nonadaptive Bounds and an Adaptive Separation

M. De Francesco2611.16456—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Answers an open question of Ouyang and Tomamichel with matching bounds, in the short, sharp form of stabilizer-learning result that has done well with this audience.

How many copies of an unknown d-regular n-qubit graph state are needed to learn the graph when each qubit is measured in X, Y or Z? Ouyang and Tomamichel showed O(d2 log n) suffice against an Ω(d log n) lower bound and asked whether the d2 is needed. De Francesco shows it is for nonadaptive learners whenever d3 ln n ≤ n, even with an unbounded classical decoder and a small fraction of edges allowed wrong.

Adaptivity beats this. On connected bipartite d-regular graphs of logarithmic diameter with a hidden bipartition, nonadaptive learning still costs Θ(d2 log n) copies, while a breadth-first adaptive learner needs only O(d log2 n / log log n). Revealing the bipartition brings the nonadaptive cost down to Θ(d log n). The lower bounds rest on one observation: a single copy reveals at most the binary nullity of a principal submatrix of the adjacency matrix. The author controls this nullity for random unions of perfect matchings with Fourier estimates and a method-of-types argument.

Exact Quantum Algorithms for Composed Boolean Functions

G. Chiruvella, C. Kayal, R. Mittal, M. Paraashar, N. Saurabh2611.16412—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A cs.CC cross-list that improves two query-complexity separation exponents; we rank it 35 places above the prior because algorithms and complexity papers of this kind are our largest source of misses (16 top-ten papers).

Exact quantum query complexity QE composes badly, and few general upper bounds are known. The authors prove QE(f∘g) = O(mD + QE(f)·Q1/(4D)(g)) for an outer function of degree D and an inner function on m bits. The algorithm runs approximate inner evaluations, then makes a finite exact correction whose size is set by the outer degree. For any fixed total gadget h they determine the exact recursive rate as max{deg(h), Adv±(h)} and show QE(h∘k) = Õ(R(h∘k)2). Recursive ternary not-all-equal gets an exact algorithm with (3/√2)k(1+o(1)) queries.

The composition limit lets them carry bounded-error separations over to exact complexity, as Tal and Kulkarni did for fractional block sensitivity. They obtain a family with s = bs = fbs ≥ QE3/2−o(1), up from the previous exponent 1.1527, and one with R ≥ QE2−o(1), up from 3/2. There are analogous results for communication.

Scaling Pêlagos: Multi-GPU Orchestration for Relay Belief-Propagation Decoding

X. Tang, …, B. R. Johnson, Y. Eckert (AMD, IBM)2611.16285—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Decoder papers took the top three on Wednesday, and this one runs IBM’s Relay-BP on the gross codes, but it is about simulation throughput rather than decoding, so it sits below the theory results.

Relay-BP is IBM’s heuristic decoder for bivariate bicycle codes, and judging it means very large Monte Carlo runs. Splitting syndrome batches across GPUs naively stalls on host-side preprocessing and collecting outputs. The AMD–IBM team adds process-isolated workers, parallel host preprocessing and shared-memory output to the Pêlagos implementation.

On the one-gross [[144,12,12]] circuit at R = 50, eight MI300X GPUs give a 5.68× end-to-end speedup and eight MI355X GPUs 6.23×. On the two-gross [[288,12,18]] circuit the figures are 6.92× and 7.01×, and 7.58× and 7.45× when counting GPU decoding time alone, close to linear. The code is deployed on Lux, Oak Ridge’s MI355X machine, as a pathfinding application for the DOE Genesis Mission. The paper reports no new logical error rates.

Sensing frequencies at the discrete Grover-Heisenberg limit using a single qubit

B. Chen, A. Coladangelo, J. Li, H. Zhang2611.150575Fri 10:20●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A short optimality result from known theory-CS names; we rank it 28 places above the prior because the model underweights sensing papers with a clean bound.

Allen et al. showed that detecting a weak oscillating field of unknown frequency obeys a “Grover–Heisenberg” limit and met it with a protocol that runs Grover’s algorithm on auxiliary qubits. Chen, Coladangelo, Li and Zhang show that a single sensing qubit is enough in the discrete case, with known signal strength and the frequency drawn from a known finite set of N candidates. Sensing time grows as √N, and they prove this is optimal in the worst case. The continuous single-qubit question remains open.

7

Infinite or indefinite: the query cost of simulating the quantum switch

D. Li2611.162291Fri 10:20●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here A query lower bound with a noise-characterisation angle; we rank it 28 places above the prior because query-bound papers have been the strongest theory thread this month.

How many calls to two black-box channels does a circuit-model computer need to reproduce the quantum switch? With one unitary box, three. For general channels on dimension d, exact simulation needs unboundedly many calls, and any approximation with error below 1/2 in half-diamond norm needs Ω(d1/4), even with feed-forward and unlimited memory. The application: telling dephasing in an unknown basis from depolarising noise with the same average fidelities takes a d-independent number of switch uses but Ω(d1/16) calls in the circuit model.

Why here A qLDPC construction from known coding theorists, but for qudits; code constructions have scored unevenly unless the result applies to qubit hardware.

The authors extend lifted-product codes over cyclic group algebras to prime-power qudit dimensions, giving quasi-cyclic qudit CSS codes for every stacking order; circulant structure is what hardware decoders exploit. How to Read This Paper

the same in every edition

What this is

A weekday paper on quantum computing and the quant-ph arXiv. The front page covers the most important general news and what people are discussing online. Then come the day's eight papers most likely to draw attention, a check of the previous forecast, which subfields are busy, and industry news, conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate, news sites, blogs and public forums. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the source before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate. The public copy counts visits with GoatCounter, which sets no cookies and keeps no personal data.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Papers are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.

The forecast learns from its record. Every morning each past forecast is scored against the real SciRate ranking, a small statistical model trained on past listings (subfield, kind of claim, authors' past record) gives a second opinion on each new paper, and confidence tags are checked against how often they came true. Our record, under Yesterday, Settled, shows the average number of our top five that landed in the real top five, next to the model alone and to chance.

Confidence

  • ●●● High We expect it in the listing's top five.
  • ●●○ Medium Likely in the top ten.
  • ●○○ Low–Med / Low Plausible but uncertain: often a specialised or slow-burning paper we think deserves a look.

Each pick also says why it is ranked where it is.

Scite counts

  • 12early Count when the paper was written, a few hours after announcement. Unreliable.
  • 47final Count after about a day and a half, close to settled.
  • 31Tue 10:10 Count read from SciRate at that time during the day; the public paper is refreshed a few times a day (31live in the private edition, which updates instantly).
  • —scites No count yet.

Yesterday, Settled

Shown beside the papers, under SciRate today (today's ranking so far, with our predicted rank for each paper), so new counts update both. The previous listing's actual top papers by scites. On Fridays, Last Week in Quantum does the same for the whole week, with the forecast record for each day.

Front page and The conversation

The lead story is the biggest general quantum story of the day, often the one people are arguing about. The conversation lists where the discussion happened: Hacker News threads, expert blogs, company posts, videos. We link to it and paraphrase it; a source being listed is not an endorsement.

Trending

Every new paper is sorted into subfields (error correction, algorithms, devices, many-body, sensing and so on) by keywords in its title and abstract, so a paper can count in more than one. The small chart shows each field's share of the last 15 listings; share, 5 listings compares the last five with the ten before (▲ up, ▼ down); scites/paper is the average SciRate attention from saved counts; top-10 slots counts how many of each saved listing's ten most-scited papers belong to the field, which shows where the standout papers are; cross-listed is the share of the field's recent papers that come from other arXiv categories, a sign of interest from outside quant-ph. Rising means the share grew by a quarter or more; Hot marks fields where the attention or the discussion is, whatever the volume.
