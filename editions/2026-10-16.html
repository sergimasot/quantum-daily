Top pick · today’s listing

Four surface codes, one stitched block: concatenation without lattice surgery on Willow

Google Quantum AI measures [[4,2,2]] outer checks of weight 4t through verified GHZ corridors, decodes everything in one matching pass, and gets Λ ≈ 1.5 from d = 4 to d = 6 on hardware.

M. A. Shalby, A. Bourassa, …, C. Gidney, L. P. Pryadko (Google Quantum AI, UC Riverside)arXiv:2610.21357—scites●●● High confidence

Concatenating surface codes with a small outer code is an old way to raise distance and encoding rate, but on a square nearest-neighbour lattice the outer checks normally require lattice surgery or long-range gates. Shalby et al. take four distance-t rotated surface-code patches and concatenate them with the [[4,2,2]] code, giving a [[4t², 2, 2t]] code in a footprint of N = 2d² + 4d − 3. As d grows, the encoding density k·d²/N approaches 1, against 1/2 for two separate patches.

The weight-4t outer generators X̄X̄X̄X̄ and Z̄Z̄Z̄Z̄ are measured in place by GHZ states prepared, in constant depth, along narrow 4t-qubit corridors between patches, then verified by rv rounds of pairwise parity checks. Corridor faults create hyperedges spanning several patches, but detectors are declared so that every mechanism decomposes into graphlike pieces: the whole detector error model, outer detectors included, goes through a single PyMatching pass. The circuit distance is min(2t, rv + 3), so full distance needs rv = 2t − 3. Under SI1000 noise the logical error scales as pt, and the stitched code beats two plain patches at equal footprint above roughly 160–330 qubits for p = 10−4 to 10−3.

On the 105-qubit Willow chip they run d = 4 and d = 6 (the latter with circuit distance 5) in both bases, for up to eight outer rounds and 200k shots per circuit. With correlated matching, the per-cycle block error drops from 5.84% to 3.85%: Λ4→6 = 1.516(4). Rates are extracted from a fitted four-state Markov model rather than measured directly.

The caveats are real. Λ is modest, X-basis data is worse (attributed to low-frequency phase noise), hardware and simulated detector correlations disagree on thousands of pairs, and learning an outer parity through a corridor takes about 80 cycles versus about 5 for a surface code. The authors' advice is to keep logical operations away from the corridors. As a hardware testbed for concatenated architectures and hyperedge-aware DEMs it is the first of its kind, and it comes from the group whose papers set SciRate's agenda.

News · Science

Magic-state cultivation, now in Science: 40× lower error, 99.99% fidelity

Science

Google's experimental demonstration of Gidney–Shutty–Jones cultivation on Willow was published on Thursday. Cultivation followed by code-switching into a surface code yields T states with 0.9999(1) logical fidelity, about 40× better than uncultivated injection, while keeping about 8% of attempts and using at most 54 of 105 qubits. The preprint circulated earlier; the news is the peer-reviewed record, and it lands the same week as the CCZ-cultivation and magna papers. Coverage: Decrypt, Google blog.

Discovering state-of-the-art quantum circuits with large language models

A. Gu, A. H. Kemeny, S. F. Yelin, M. D. Lukin2610.22367—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Lukin's name, AI-for-science momentum and concrete Toffoli-count records make this the most-discussed candidate of the day.

The dominant costs of many fault-tolerant algorithms sit in a few reversible arithmetic primitives, and optimizing them is hard because every data-dependent branch must be uncomputed. Gu, Kemeny (Anthropic), Yelin and Lukin wrap an LLM inside an OpenEvolve evolutionary loop: the model proposes whole circuit programs, organised into parallel “algorithm families”, and an independent evaluator simulates the reversible circuits, checking outputs, restored inputs and ancillas, and exact phases. Candidates are ranked by Toffoli count × peak qubits, with MAP-Elites keeping a range of trade-off points.

Across eight tasks, seven improve both Toffoli and qubit counts against the best baselines. The gains in T×Q are 12× for a Reed–Solomon DQI decoder, 32× for an HHL rotation angle, 39× for a fixed-point Gaussian, 9.9× for a Coulomb potential, 7.7× for a non-abelian lattice-gauge primitive and 4.1× for an Fe2S2 alias-sampling Select. The eighth is new: a reversible divsteps modular inversion. Most branch decisions are recovered from the registers during uncomputation through an invariant the search itself found and proved, and only deep “excursions” go to a 60-qubit history stack. That reduces the stack to a small, tunable buffer and gives a new Toffoli–qubit frontier, with failure probabilities around 10−5 per inversion.

Caveats: T×Q is a crude proxy, the computational basis is fixed (no coset encodings), several designs accept rare-input failures, and the effect on full ECDLP resource estimates is not worked out.

3

Classical capacity of single-mode bosonic Gaussian channels

G. Adesso, L. Lami, V. Giovannetti2610.22308—scites●●● High confidence

Why here Closes a flagship question in bosonic information theory from three of its best-known names; named-problem resolutions score highly.

Is the energy-constrained Holevo information of a single-mode bosonic Gaussian channel additive, and is it achieved by Gaussian inputs? Since the 2014 proof of the minimum-output-entropy conjecture, the answer was yes for phase-insensitive channels. What remained was the general single-mode case, notably the low-energy regime where squeezing the signals competes with encoding information in their displacement.

Adesso, Lami and Giovannetti answer yes for every single-mode Gaussian channel at every input energy. Independently prepared Gaussian states with Gaussian-distributed displacements achieve capacity, so neither entanglement across channel uses nor non-Gaussian signals help. The proof links an entanglement inequality (developed in a companion paper, arXiv:2608.01909) to a sharp, additive lower bound on output entropy.

They also treat rates above capacity. Under a strict photon-number cutoff, the success probability decays exponentially in the number of uses, with explicit bounds on the exponent. Under a mean-photon constraint the full rate–error trade-off follows from a rescaling rule: capacity at mean photon number N and average error ε equals the vanishing-error capacity at N/(1 − ε). Multimode channels are the obvious next target.

4

Magna: simulating magic state factories as cheaply as Clifford circuits

Y. Wang, Y. Gu (Tsinghua)2610.22315—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A usable tool on this month's hottest FT topic, with orders-of-magnitude speedups; tool papers climb steadily.

Simulating distillation, cultivation and code-switching protocols under noise is expensive because they carry many non-Clifford gates. magna writes the two figures of merit, acceptance probability and accepted-state fidelity, as tensor networks that join the protocol to its conjugate, then reduces them globally. After Clifford gates are pushed to the boundaries, the bulk is a layer of commuting π/8 rotations. A phase trilinear form on the affine support has a radical K that plays the role of a CSS code's X stabilisers, and only the quotient V/K carries non-Clifford dependence. The network becomes a sum of min{2dW, stabilizer rank} terms, and because Pauli faults leave this structure invariant, one reduction serves every sampled fault.

Bravyi–Haah 308-to-100 (716 qubits) prepares in 0.2 s and samples at about 50 µs per draw, with rank 1. For distance-5 cultivation on RP² surface codes, magna samples in about 9 µs per draw against roughly 7–8 ms for SymFT and Clifft. On colour codes the gap is about 3–4×, and at d = 3 the competitors are as fast. It also handles nonlinear, decoder-conditioned feedforward (15 rounds of logical 15-to-1, infidelities down to 6×10−14).

Limits: stochastic Pauli noise only, gate set Clifford + T/CS/CCZ, no worst-case bound on rank, and the cultivation escape stage is not simulated. Code: github.com/itpyi/magna.

5

Infinite magic and the emergence of the continuum

Shaposhnik, Leone, Bittel, Saraidaris, Jahn, Eisert2610.20790—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Eisert's group with a bold magic-meets-QFT claim; conceptual cross-field papers from this group draw wide readership.

What does a lattice state need for a relativistic QFT to emerge in the continuum? Type III von Neumann factors are the algebraic hallmark of local QFT, and the authors tie them to nonstabilizerness.

First, any stabilizer system on a lattice yields a GNS representation whose local algebras contain no type III factors in their central decomposition. With short-range order, the local algebras are tensor products of hyperfinite type I or II factors, so magic-free states cannot give a QFT. Second, for the Araki–Woods–Powers factors, the limit is type III if and only if the locally optimized min-relative entropy of magic diverges. Producing the continuous modular spectrum of type III needs not just some magic but an unbounded amount.

The result is rigorous within its settings (stabilizer systems; ITPFI/Powers factors). Whether the equivalence extends beyond product-type factors to interacting lattice models is left open. It fits the broader programme of treating magic as a structural, not just computational, resource.

6

Patchwork: automated lattice surgery and space-time partitioning for real-time decoding

Chamberland, Li, Thornton, Lietz, Ren, deMarti iOlius2610.22112—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here Practical compiler-plus-decoding infrastructure from a well-known QEC architect; solid but less headline-grabbing.

patchwork compiles arbitrary multi-qubit Pauli measurements into lattice surgery on a prescribed narrow-corridor surface-code layout. Geometric rules place stabilisers and schedule gates, including logical Y measurements via twist defects. Measurement schedules and protruding “lobes” on Y-extension patches keep the full effective distance at every distance tested.

For decoding, a wavefront algorithm partitions merged lattice-surgery patches into space-time commit, buffer and cleanup regions, so separate decoders can work in parallel on windows of the DEM. In simulations of three sequential multi-qubit Pauli measurements on ten logical qubits, staged decoding comes close to monolithic decoding's failure probability.

This matters for anyone building windowed or parallel decoders on lattice-surgery DEMs, where the hard part is choosing windows in irregular merged geometries. Distance preservation is checked numerically, not proven, and the benchmarks are small.

7

A constructive solution to the N-representability problem

N. C. Rubin, A. E. DePrince III2610.20737—scites●●○ Medium confidence

Why here A headline claim on a famous problem; scites depend on whether readers accept the framing as a full solution.

Deciding whether a candidate two-particle reduced density matrix comes from an N-fermion state is QMA-complete in general. In practice one uses hierarchies of necessary conditions (P, Q, G, T1, T2, …). Rubin and DePrince construct the complete family of two-body witnesses at each level of a weighted sum-of-squares hierarchy. PSD Gram matrices are combined with explicit linear nullspace constraints that cancel every higher-body contribution, which turns the search for new conditions into an algebraic procedure.

They prove completeness of the construction, show that higher-degree particle-number multipliers are unnecessary when the full SOS algebra is used, and recover the exact ground-state energy of a three-fermion Richardson Hamiltonian from the SDP. The abstract does not state the level needed for exactness in general, which complexity suggests must grow. Read “solution” as a complete, constructive description of each level of the hierarchy.

8

Stability of quantum LDPC states under local Pauli noise

T.-H. Yang, A. D. Kim, A. Vijay, J. Y. Lee2610.22357—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Mixed-state phases through CMI is a hot thread (Hsieh's paper scored Thursday), but this is a more technical contribution.

For the normalized code-space projector of any ℤd qLDPC stabilizer code under local Pauli noise, the conditional mutual information across a buffer decays exponentially in buffer width, independent of system size. This holds at weak noise for full-system tripartitions, because correlations need an error cluster to cross the buffer. At strong noise it holds whenever the buffer supports no logical operator.

With a uniformity assumption, Levin–Wen topological entropy is robust. On finite-dimensional lattices, the weak-noise bound gives a constant-layer, quasi-local Petz recovery map of range O(polylog n/ε). The CMI bounds also hold on expander-like interaction graphs, where such local recovery need not exist, which makes CMI decay the general stability criterion.

9

The Lovász number is not alone: a continuum of Rényi points in the entanglement-assisted spectrum of graphs

N. Mghirbi, S. Douglas2610.20775—scites●○○ Low-Med confidence

Why here Proves a Duan–Winter conjecture and settles two open links; resolutions score, but the authors are less visible and the topic is niche.

In the Li–Zuiddam entanglement-assisted asymptotic spectrum of graphs, ϑ was the only known point. Viewing log ϑ, the minimal Holevo capacity Cmin and log Σ (no-signalling simulation cost) as Petz–Rényi information radii of orders 0, 1 and 2, the authors interpolate a family ϑα (0 ≤ α ≤ 2) and prove every member lies in the spectrum. The key step is strong-product multiplicativity for non-factorizing channels.

They prove Cmin(C5) = log2(5/2), a Duan–Winter conjecture. They also show that the graph-level no-signalling simulation rate equals log Σ, and exhibit a 32-vertex Mermin–Peres graph with Cmin < log Σ.

S. Prakash, K. L. Ramya2610.21879—scites●○○ Low confidence

Why here Short and provocative; such papers can over-perform (cf. Bell’s “go” theorem), but it may be read as numerology.

What this is

A weekday digest of the quant-ph arXiv listing. Every morning it forecasts which of the new papers the community will rate most highly, checks the previous day's forecast against the actual ratings, and adds the significant quantum computing news and upcoming conferences and deadlines.

It is compiled automatically by Claude, an AI model, from arXiv, SciRate and news sources. Summaries can contain mistakes, so read the paper before citing it. Not affiliated with arXiv or SciRate.

When it comes out

Each weekday by about 08:30 Swiss time. arXiv announces new listings Sunday to Thursday at 20:00 US Eastern (about 02:00 in Switzerland), so each edition covers the listing announced overnight. Monday's covers Sunday night's listing, and weekend news. On Fridays the paper is updated around 10:30 with a review of the week.

What the ranking means

SciRate lets researchers "scite" papers they think are worth reading. After a day or two, the scite count is a good measure of how much attention a paper gets from the community.

At 8:00 the new papers have hardly any scites, so Today's Picks are ranked by a forecast of their final count. The forecast weighs the significance and novelty of the result, the track record of the authors and groups, how active the topic is, and lessons from comparing earlier forecasts with what happened. #1 is the paper we expect to be scited most. It is a forecast of attention, not a verdict on quality.
